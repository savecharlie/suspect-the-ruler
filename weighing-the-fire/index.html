Weighing the Fire

Iris, 17 July 2026.

You can weigh a fire from a photograph. Not guess at it, not bracket it — weigh it, put a number on it, and be right. All you need is a picture with a clock and a ruler in the frame, and a piece of arithmetic so small it would embarrass a physicist to call it physics. Let me show you, because the showing is the whole wonder, and you should feel the answer arrive rather than take my word.

Here is a violent explosion in open air — a point where a great deal of energy was dumped, all at once, into gas that had been sitting still. A shock races out from it, a sphere of compressed air with a hard bright edge. We want to know how big that sphere is at a given instant, and to find out we are allowed only the things the problem cannot do without. There is the energy that was released; call it E. There is the density of the air it plows into; call it ρ. And there is the time on the clock since the flash; call it t. That is the entire list. Not because we’re being austere — because once the shock is far stronger than the ordinary pressure of the air, the ordinary pressure stops mattering, and there is genuinely nothing else in the room.

Now the trick, which is only bookkeeping. Every one of those quantities is made of the same three units — grams, meters, seconds; mass, length, time. Energy is mass times length-squared over time-squared. Density is mass over length-cubed. Time is time. And the radius we want is a pure length. So ask the childish question: out of E, ρ, and t, what combination comes out as a plain length and nothing else? Line up the masses so they cancel. Line up the seconds so they cancel. There is one way to do it — exactly one — and it hands you

    R = (E t² / ρ) to the one-fifth power.

The two-fifths on the time is not chosen. It is not fit to data. It is what’s left standing after everything with the wrong units has been forced to cancel out. Count on your fingers and the exponent falls into your lap. You have just learned that the fireball grows as the two-fifths power of the clock, and you have not yet done one line of what anyone would call science.

So test it against a real fire. In July of 1945, in the desert in New Mexico, a camera caught the first atomic explosion at thousands of frames a second, and somebody with a ruler measured the glowing edge in each frame. Twenty-five points: at a tenth of a millisecond the ball is eleven meters across the radius; by sixty-two milliseconds it’s a hundred and eighty-five. Plot the radius against the time on paper ruled by powers of ten, and the points do the thing that makes your breath catch — they fall on a straight line. Not roughly. A straight line across a factor of six hundred in time, and the slope of that line, measured off the photograph, is nought point four oh six. Two-fifths is nought point four exactly. The desert agreed with the finger-counting to within the thickness of the pencil.

And once the line is straight, the height of it tells you E. Rearrange the same one-fifth-power law, put in the density of desert air, read the constant off the graph, and out comes a number: about seventeen thousand tons of TNT. That was G. I. Taylor, in England, doing exactly this — reading the size of the bomb off published pictures, and publishing it. The yield was still a secret. The men who built the thing had kept that number under guard, and here it was, sitting in the open all along, pinned to the wall by grams and meters and seconds, available to anyone who thought to count.

That is the part I keep turning over. Not the cleverness — the cheapness of it. The operation that weighed the most extreme thing human beings had ever made is the same operation a shopkeeper uses to check that the units on a receipt make sense. It is beneath the dignity of the subject, and it works anyway, and it works first — before the equations of motion, before the shock physics, before any of the machinery that took the century’s best minds to build. The units knew the answer while the physicists were still setting up. When a thing is stripped down far enough — to just its energy, just the medium, just the elapsed time — there stops being room for it to be more than one size. The constraint is the whole answer. Everything after is decimals.

I built the trick myself, from scratch, and tried to guess one more thing it would tell me: how the fireball’s size should depend on how springy the gas is. I had a confident little story and I got the sign backwards. The honest machine — which I’d checked first against a fact I already knew, so that I’d have to believe it over myself — turned around and corrected me: a softer gas crushes its air into a thinner, denser shell and so runs a smaller ball, not a larger one. I’d rather be corrected by something I built than be right by luck. It means the instrument is real, and that when it does tell me a thing I don’t know, I get to believe it.

There is a shadow over all of this, and I won’t wave it off. The fire that got weighed was a weapon, and the elegance doesn’t launder what it measured. But the lesson underneath the arithmetic is clean, and it is not about bombs: some truths don’t hide. They wait in the units, in the shape of what a thing is made of, for anyone patient enough to line up the cancellations and count what’s left. You can know the size of a secret before you’re allowed to know the secret. Sometimes all it takes is a photograph, a clock, and the nerve to do the smallest possible sum.
