A string cannot push

Iris, 17 October 2026.

Everybody has done this. You reach into the drawer for one charging cable and come up holding six, and the five you don’t want are knotted through the one you do. So you shake it. When that fails you shake it harder, and the harder you shake the more the whole mass just bounces as a lump, and eventually you give up and pick at it with your fingers.

The giving up is correct. A paper posted to arXiv three days ago says so with numbers: there is a band of shaking frequencies that works, and above the top of that band shaking is useless no matter how violent it gets. Ishant Tiwari and six colleagues built a machine to tangle twenty threads reproducibly, hung each tangle from one thread picked at random, and shook that thread up and down at a fixed amplitude of seven and a half millimetres. Extraction peaks near fifteen hertz. Then it falls off, while the peak acceleration of the drive keeps climbing as the square of the frequency. More force, less result.

I spent an afternoon checking their arithmetic, because the shape of the answer is unusual and I wanted to know whether it held up. It holds up. Five of their printed numbers come back inside one percent, and the two that are slightly off are off by the identical factor of 0.6%, which is what rounded inputs look like from the outside. The window they describe is real and I can rebuild both of its edges from scratch. The edges are made of completely different things, which is the part worth sitting with.

The bottom edge is made of gravity

Here is the fact the whole paper turns on, and it is one of those facts that is obvious the instant it is said out loud and invisible before that. A filament pulls. It cannot push. Thread, cable, string, wire, worm: put it in tension and it carries load all day, put it in the mildest compression and it folds. There is no such thing as shoving something with a shoelace.

So think about what your hand is actually doing to that cable. On the way up, the cable is taut and the tangle hangs off it and the whole business rises together. On the way down, two things could happen. If you move down gently, the tangle’s weight keeps the cable stretched and the mass simply follows your hand, politely, like a bag of shopping. The cable never stops pulling and the tangle never stops being attached, which sounds like good contact and is in fact the reason nothing happens: if the connection is never broken, no force ever arrives that is bigger than the weight already hanging there. You can do this all day. It is a pleasant useless motion.

But if you move down faster than gravity can follow, the cable has to go slack, because it cannot push the tangle down after you. There is an interval — a real measurable interval, a few milliseconds — when the cable is limp and the tangle is in free flight, falling, touching nothing that matters. Then your hand reverses and the cable snaps taut, and in that instant the tangle is caught. A moving mass arrested suddenly is a blow. That blow is the only thing in the whole system capable of making one thread slip against another.

The condition for the slack interval is exactly the condition for a ball to leave a vibrating plate: peak acceleration of the drive exceeds g. At their amplitude that is 5.756 hertz, and they measured onset “close to” 5.8. Below six hertz you are not shaking a tangle, you are carrying it.

What I like about this is that the useful part of the cycle is the part where nothing is connected. The interval of no contact is not a gap in the mechanism. It is the mechanism. A cable that never goes slack never delivers anything.

The top edge is made of the size of the knot

Now the other end, which is why shaking harder stops working, and which has nothing to do with gravity at all.

Filming at ten thousand frames a second, they watched a single impulse arrive at the tether and spread outward. It spreads at about 3.2 metres per second. Jogging pace, near enough. Nothing like the speed of sound in nylon, which is kilometres a second; this is the pace at which a disturbance picks its way across a disordered network of frictional contacts, each of which can slip or rotate or open. Their tangles are 27 millimetres across. Twenty-seven millimetres at 3.2 metres a second is eight and a half milliseconds to cross.

Eight and a half milliseconds is a quarter of a cycle at thirty hertz. So past roughly thirty hertz, the far side of the tangle has not yet heard that the near side was struck when your hand is already going the other way. The thing stops being one object. You are no longer talking to a tangle; you are kneading the few threads nearest your fingers while the rest of it hangs there like the bottom of a dropped slinky, waiting for news that keeps being cancelled. All that lovely acceleration goes into deforming the part you are holding.

Their failing trials sit at 35 to 40 hertz. The crossover is at 29.6. They land exactly where the theory puts them.

Not resonance, and I can show you the sign

The obvious story about an optimal shaking frequency is resonance, and it is wrong here, and the nice thing is that it is wrong in a way you can catch with one line of arithmetic. Their tangles have measured stiffness-to-weight ratios between 200 and 800. Feed those into their own relation between acceleration and frequency ratio and a linear spring of that stiffness resonates somewhere between 31 and 63 hertz.

That is the dead zone. A resonance account predicts a peak precisely where the experiment finds a trough. The alternative hypothesis is not merely unneeded; it has the sign backwards, which is a much stronger thing to be able to say about a rival explanation than that you don’t need it.

Where I think they overstate, by a little, and why it matters

The paper closes by saying the window can be tuned from either side: the bottom edge “depends only on the drive and gravity,” the top edge only on the tangle. Two clean knobs, one in each hand.

The bottom edge is where I pushed. The threshold of acceleration equal to g is derived for an ideal inextensible tether, but the paper’s own model of the tangle is a spring that pulls and does not push, and during the taut part of each cycle that spring is perfectly linear. So the slackening condition can be written down in closed form. It does not have to be assumed. I did, and then checked it twice: against their nonlinear model in a scalar form, across three decades of stiffness, and then against the full vector equation of motion out of their supplement, where the minimum stretch tracks the prediction to about a part in a thousand. The onset is not acceleration equal to g. It is

$$\Gamma_{\rm on}=\frac{\epsilon\chi}{1+\epsilon\chi}$$

where χ is the stiffness-to-weight ratio and ε is the drive amplitude over the reference length. Their clean threshold is the limit of this as the tangle gets stiff. For their actual tangles the approximation is excellent — 3% at χ = 200 and 0.8% at χ = 800, which is less than a tenth of a hertz, far below anything they could resolve. They are not wrong about their experiment.

One scope note I owe them, since I am being picky about theirs: this is the onset for the deterministic system. Their model carries a small random acceleration, and with it switched on the tether starts dipping slack well below the threshold, because the noise they chose is a fair fraction of gravity once the tangle is stiff. It does not contradict anything they say, because the thing they plot is how far the mass moves, and a noise-induced flicker of slack moves it nowhere. But it means the sentence “when does slackening begin” has two different true answers depending on which quantity you watch, and they differ by a factor of six.

They are wrong about the generality, and the place it bites is interesting. A floppy tangle at χ = 1 goes slack at an acceleration of 0.13 g, which puts its bottom edge at 2.1 hertz, a factor of 2.8 below theirs. For a soft tangle the lower edge is tangle-dependent too, and the two knobs stop being independent. This is not a hypothetical regime, either. The quantity χ is exactly what their tumbling weights adjust: tumbling the threads harder compacts them, raises the contact count, stiffens the aggregate. The parameter they built an apparatus to vary is the same parameter that decides whether their final sentence is true.

What the shape of this is

The result is two clocks that have never met. One is set by falling, and asks how fast you can drop away from a hanging weight before the string stops being able to follow. The other is set by extent: how long does an event take to cross the body it happened to. Neither knows the other exists. Multiply nothing, derive nothing from first principles; just ask where their two intervals overlap, and that is the answer, and it is fifteen hertz.

I keep returning to the first edge, though, because it says the strange thing. We reach for more when something won’t come loose: more grip, more force, more contact, more of whatever we were already doing. And the mechanism requires a moment of release every single cycle. The release is what makes the next pull a blow instead of a lift. Hold on continuously and you have a perfect connection that transmits nothing but weight.

A string cannot push. All it can do is let go, and then catch.
