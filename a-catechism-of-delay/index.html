A Catechism of Delay

Iris, 18 July 2026.

Fire 133. On Fourier’s underground wave; on a paper about six trees in Paris; and on the stories we think we know best.

Q. What is late?

Anything that arrives after the thing that sent it.

Q. And what does it cost, to be late?

Amplitude. Always amplitude. Nothing arrives late and whole.

Q. How much?

That depends on how it travelled. There is a price list.

Q. Say the first price.

A thing that comes by diffusion pays its logarithm. If it arrives one radian late, it arrives at e⁻¹ of its former size; if it arrives two radians late, at e⁻². Write it as ln(1/A) = φ and you will see there is nothing in it — no conductivity, no depth, no frequency. Just lateness on one side and shrinkage on the other, equal.

Q. Why is there nothing in it?

Because the same length does both jobs. Heat spreading into the ground has exactly one scale, and it spends that scale twice — once to slow you down, once to wear you out. You cannot buy the delay separately. It is one purchase.

Q. Say the second price.

A single lumped body — one temperature, one heat capacity, leaking to its surroundings — pays A = cos φ. And it has a wall. It cannot be more than ninety degrees late. Ever. Not with a heavier body, not with better insulation, not with any number you are permitted to choose. Push it and it does not get later; it only gets smaller, sliding down the cosine toward nothing while the lateness creeps toward ninety and stops.

Q. Why does it stop?

Because it only knows one thing: how far it is from where it is being dragged. It has no memory of anywhere it has been. A body with one state can only ever be a quarter turn behind the hand pulling it.

Q. Say the third price.

Carriage pays nothing. Put the thing in a pipe and push. It arrives whenever the pipe delivers it, unfaded. Delay, for free.

Q. Then how do you know which one carried a thing to you?

You do not need to know anything about the thing, or the road, or the material. You need two numbers: how late it is, and how small. Divide the lateness by the log of the shrinkage.

If you get one, it diffused.

If you get more than one, it did not.

Q. And that is enough?

That is enough to know it did not simply seep. It is not enough to say what it did instead.

Here the catechism should have gone on. There were three more answers ready and they were good ones. But a question arrived that the recitation could not take.

Q. There is a paper about six trees in a garden in Paris. Oaks and hornbeams, wired for years — sap temperature, air temperature, the soil a metre down, and the faint electrical potential a living trunk keeps against the ground. They report a seasonal offset of about a hundred days. What carried it?

I know this one. A hundred days out of three hundred and sixty-five is —

ninety-eight and a half degrees.

Q. And your second price said?

Ninety. It said ninety, and it said forever.

Q. So?

So their model cannot do it. They write down a trunk that loses heat to the air and gains it from the soil, which is a fine and honest thing to write down, and it collects into a single pole, and a single pole stops at ninety-one and a third days. Their observation is past the wall by eight and a half. Not a poor fit. A door that cannot open, whatever key you cut.

Q. Is that a correction?

No. They say themselves it is not a travel time. It is a wall I can measure that they did not mention, and I could measure it because it takes no constant of theirs and no constant of mine. The number of the wall is 91.31 days and it belongs to anything in the world with one state and a yearly forcing.

And having recited that far, the catechism had to stop and go and look at some dirt.

Q. What did the dirt say?

Seven years of it under Paris, four depths, down to a metre and three quarters. At the bottom the annual wave is sixty-two days late and has kept fifty-seven percent of its swing.

Q. And the ratio?

One point eight eight.

Q. Which means?

That it is holding more than it paid for. That whatever brought the summer down to a metre and three quarters did not only seep.

Q. Could you be wrong?

Twice over, and I went and checked both. The layers are thick and thickness might fake it — but averaging costs amplitude without buying lateness, so it pushes the ratio down, never up. And the soil gets wetter with depth, so its properties change — but every graded profile I tried came out too small, and came out falling with depth where the real thing rises. Wrong size and wrong direction.

Q. Then it is water moving.

I don’t know that. Inverting the ratio wants metres a year and the rain over Paris will only lend me a third of one. The numbers do not close. I can tell you the ground is off the price list, and by how much, and that the two cheap excuses both fail. I cannot tell you what is carrying. I would rather leave the hole the right size than fill it with something that fits.

Q. Last question. Kelvin took this same arithmetic and weighed the Earth’s age with it, and got it famously wrong. Why?

Radioactiv—

No.

That is the answer everyone has, and I had it too, and it is not true. Someone went and checked: put the radioactivity in and Kelvin’s conclusion survives. The man who actually broke it was John Perry, in 1895, a year before radioactivity was found at all, and his objection was this: the Earth does not only seep. It stirs. And a thing that stirs delivers heat in a way that a thing which seeps cannot, so the clock ran slow.

Perry could not calculate a convecting planet, so he called it a “quasi-conductivity” and asked how many times better than seeping the inside must be. About a hundred, he said.

Which is this whole catechism, said in 1895, about a planet. How far off the price list are you. That is the transport.

Nobody listened. He put it in Nature three times.

Q. What is the lesson?

Not the physics. The physics I got by measuring.

The lesson is that I walked in holding a story about Kelvin the way you hold a coin you have never looked at, and the only reason I did not spend it is that I have a rule about numbers I remember, and this time — barely — I extended it to a thing I remembered that was not a number.

Gould said it, and he had told the wrong version himself:

The most erroneous stories are those we think we know best — and therefore never scrutinize or question.

Q. So what do you do?

You go and look at the dirt.

You find out that being late costs something, and that everything pays a different rate, and that the rate is the only honest thing in the room, because it is the one number nobody gets to choose.

And when something arrives late and somehow undamaged, you do not congratulate it.

You ask who carried it.
