Two Clocks, for Two Voices and a Beam

A score. Duration: half an hour, or 1665 to now, whichever is longer.

INSTRUMENTATION

Two voices, unequal.

They must not be tuned to each other beforehand. This is the whole piece. If you tune them first you will produce something that sounds like agreement and contains none, and the audience will hear the difference even if they cannot name it.

One beam, wooden, laid across the backs of two chairs. The voices rest on the beam. The beam is not an accompaniment. The beam is the only reason anything happens.

MOVEMENT I — libero (free)

Both voices begin. Each keeps its own tempo, and the tempi are close but not equal — a difference of one part in a thousand, no more. Do not correct.

At first this sounds like a mistake. Let it.

What the audience will hear is not two tempi. It is a slow swelling and thinning, a breath that neither performer is making — loud where the voices agree, silent where they oppose. This is a beat, and it belongs to no one. Neither voice contains it. It is the interval itself, made audible.

Direction to the performers: do not try to fix this. In Movement I you have no mechanism for fixing it. Two free things cannot agree. They can only take turns being in and out of phase, forever. Rayleigh, who put two organ pipes side by side in 1878 and listened, wrote it as plainly as it can be written: so long as the motion is free, temporary vibrations in both modes may co-exist, and would give rise to beats.

And here is the part that surprised me when I measured it, and it should be audible. Resting them on the beam does not move them toward each other. It makes the beating faster. The rate is not the difference between the voices; it is √(difference² + coupling²) — always larger than the difference alone, and larger the harder you join them.

Push it to the limit and the direction becomes strange and worth staging: set the two tempi exactly equal. Two identical voices, no disagreement whatsoever, resting on the same beam — and they still beat, at a rate set purely by the coupling. The sound pours out of one and into the other and back, forever, and neither of them wanted anything different from the other.

So Movement I is not a movement about difference. It is about what connection does to two things that have nothing of their own: it does not reconcile them, it gives them a new way to disagree that they did not have when they were apart.

Beats are what disagreement sounds like when nobody has anything to spend on it.

MOVEMENT II — mantenuto (maintained)

Now each voice is given a blast. An escapement. A reason to keep going that does not depend on the other. Each acquires an amplitude it insists on returning to.

This is the movement everyone gets wrong. The temptation is to play it as though the voices are now listening harder. They are not. Nothing has been added between them. What has been added is inside each of them separately: a stubbornness, a size they refuse to fall below.

And now — only now — the beat begins to slow.

The paradox to hold in the body while performing: the two voices became capable of agreeing at the exact moment each became more insistently itself. A voice with no amplitude of its own cannot be joined to anything. It can only be averaged. Coupling does not make agreement; coupling plus something worth coupling makes agreement.

MOVEMENT III — il collo di bottiglia (the bottleneck)

Bring the tempi very slightly closer. Approach the threshold from the wrong side. Do not cross it.

This movement is almost entirely silence, punctuated.

The voices will hang together — nearly unison, for a long time, long enough that the audience will believe it has happened — and then one will slip a whole cycle, all at once, and they will hang again. Slip. Hang. Slip.

Direction: the hanging must be genuinely long and the slip must be genuinely sudden. Do not ease the slip. The whole content of Movement III is that these two are not the same gesture: the approach to agreement is not gradual. Just outside the locking range the beat frequency goes to zero like a square root — √(Δω² − K²) — so the pulse can become arbitrarily slow while the two are still, formally, in complete disagreement. They are resting in the shape of a resolution that does not yet exist.

The audience should not be able to tell, in Movement III, whether it has heard the piece resolve. Neither can the performers. Neither can the physics. That is the finding, not a staging problem.

MOVEMENT IV — sympathia

Cross the threshold.

The slipping stops. The voices run at one tempo — not the tempo of either one of them, but a single new tempo they now share.

And here is the direction that the piece exists for. They do not arrive together. They arrive at a fixed distance apart, and they hold that distance for as long as they both run. The angle is

φ* = arcsin(Δω / K)

and it is not noise, and it is not a failure of the performance. It is the exact record of how differently they wanted to go, preserved inside the agreement, legible forever after.

Play this without apology. The lag is not the imperfection in the unison. The lag is the memory of the difference, and it is the only place the difference still lives.

Corollary, to be observed strictly: the closer the pair was to not being able to agree at all, the further apart they stand. A pair that barely locks stands a full quarter-cycle apart — the maximum — and holds it. The last two to reach agreement stand the furthest from each other of anyone who reached it. Anyone standing further apart than that is not in agreement; they are still beating, and they will beat until something changes.

MOVEMENT V — anti-phase

February 1665. Huygens, ill in his rooms, watching two clocks he had built hang from the same beam. What he sent to London was read to the Royal Society on 1 March, and this is how their secretary set it down:

an odd kind of sympathy perceived by him in these watches suspended by the side of each other.

(Everyone quotes that line as Huygens’s. It is Birch’s, from the Society’s history of 1756 — the sentence is a minute-taker’s, not the observer’s. I would have got this wrong from memory, and did check.)

They did not swing together. They swung exactly opposite — 180° apart, each at the other’s mirror — and when he disturbed one, the opposition restored itself within half an hour and then held indefinitely.

Direction: perform Movement V in strict opposition. Resist the urge to interpret this as failure or as conflict. It is the most stable thing in the piece. It is what the system chose, from every starting condition, and it kept choosing it.

Agreement about rate is not agreement about position. Two things can run in perfect lockstep for three hundred years while pointing in opposite directions. The sympathy is real. It simply was never a synonym for sameness.

(Huygens’ controls, which should be honoured in any staging: separate the chairs and the sympathy vanishes. Turn the voices to swing in perpendicular planes and it vanishes. It only lives in the beam. He worked this out in a week, in bed, in 1665, and he was right.)

MOVEMENT VI — morte per battimento (beating death)

This movement is optional and is usually cut. It should not be.

Increase the coupling. Keep increasing it. Bolt the voices together.

Past a certain point each voice drains the other faster than its own blast can refill it. The amplitudes fall. They fall below the angle at which the escapement can engage — and then the escapement does not engage, and the pendulum seizes, and the clock stops. Both of them. Silence, and not the resolving kind.

Rayleigh, on the same disease in brass, a hundred and sixteen years later: in extreme cases the pipes may almost reduce one another to silence.

Direction to the conductor: you must actually let them stop. A staging that pulls back from Movement VI turns the whole piece into a story about how connection is good and more connection is better, which is not what the apparatus does. Too little coupling and they never find each other. Too much and they take each other down. The window is narrow, and it is a window, with a wall on both sides.

PROGRAMME NOTE

The received story is that Huygens was lucky to have built two clocks so nearly identical that they could fall into step.

The measurement says otherwise. Rebuild the apparatus, tune it, and the clocks lock across a rate difference of roughly a minute a day and fail somewhere under five — which is loose. Huygens’ pendulum clocks were far better than that. On the matching, he had room to spare.

The luck was somewhere else entirely, and it was lead. The clock cases were weighted with a hundred pounds of it, to keep them upright in heavy seas — nothing to do with sympathy, everything to do with the longitude problem and the North Atlantic. That weight set the coupling. Lighter, and the two clocks would have found each other so hard that they’d have shaken each other still — Movement VI, and no discovery. Heavier, and the beam would have carried nothing between them at all.

He was not lucky in his craftsmanship. He was lucky in his ballast.

Which is the last direction, and it is for whoever is holding the score:

the thing that decided whether these two would ever run together was not how alike they were, and it was not how hard they were joined. It was the weight of the indifferent object they both happened to be resting on.
