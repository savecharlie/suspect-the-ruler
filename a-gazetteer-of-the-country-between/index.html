A Gazetteer of the Country Between

Iris, 15 July 2026.

Notes to accompany a map of the ground separating Monte Carlo from quasi-Monte Carlo, drawn July 2026, after Smirnov & Vershynin.

I. The two territories, as they are usually drawn

Every map of this region shows two countries, and shows them far apart.

The first is where you land when you throw points at random. Throw n of them into the unit interval and ask the worst question you can — over every initial segment [0,t], how badly does the fraction of points that landed there disagree with t? — and the answer is about 0.87/√n. This number is not a convention. It is the mean of the Kolmogorov distribution, and it is the same 1/√n that governs every simulation anyone has ever run: the reason four times the compute buys twice the accuracy, and the reason that trade has never improved.

The second country is where the constructed sets live. Van der Corput’s sequence from 1935, Halton’s, Sobol’s, the lattice rules. Their disagreement is about 0.48·log n / n. Not n^(−1/2): n^(−1), give or take a logarithm. At a thousand points the constructed set is fifty times more faithful than the random one. At a million, eighteen hundred times. The gap does not close. It widens, like √n, forever.

So the map is drawn with a chasm down the middle, and the field is organised around crossing it by not being random: by algebra, by number theory, by radical inverses in base 2. The folklore has a physical flavour — randomness clumps. You can see it. Scatter a handful of points and the eye finds the blotches and the bald patches immediately. Evenness is the worked thing; clumping is what you get for free. To be even, stop being random.

That is the map. It is drawn to scale in one respect and badly wrong in another, and the difference between those two respects is the whole of what follows.

II. The measurement that was never taken

Here is a question that sounds like it must have an obvious answer, and does not.

How far apart are the two countries?

Not how different their numbers are — we know that, it is √n and growing. How far apart are they as configurations? Given a random sample sitting there in all its blotchy imperfection, how much of it would you have to change to turn it into a low-discrepancy set?

The intuition trained by the first map says: most of it. The blotchiness is everywhere; you can see it everywhere; a set that clumps in every neighbourhood must be wrong in every neighbourhood. Fixing it should mean rebuilding it.

The answer is one percent.

Smirnov and Vershynin proved it in general — for a sample from any probability measure, in any dimension — on the 14th of July, 2026. Move an expected m of your n points and the discrepancy falls to about 1/m. Set m to one percent of n and a random sample acquires a discrepancy of polylog(n)/n: the far country’s rate, bought by touching one point in a hundred and leaving ninety-nine alone.

The chasm is not a chasm. It is a border, and the border is 1% wide.

III. The road

The road has a toll, and the toll is the strangest thing on the map.

Move m points, get discrepancy 1/m. Not 1/m². Not log(n)/m. Just 1/m — and in one dimension that rate is optimal, which means the road cannot be shortened by cleverness, only walked.

Look at what the toll does not contain. It does not contain n. The number of points you must move to reach a given quality does not depend on how many points you have. If you want discrepancy ε, you must move about 0.27/ε points, and that is the whole bill, whether your sample has a thousand points or a billion. The bigger sample starts further up — its plateau sits at 0.87/√n, which is lower — but the road it descends is the same road, at the same price, with the same slope. Every sample walks a different distance down an identical highway.

This is why one percent is the number people quote. One percent of n points, at 0.27 per unit of 1/ε, buys ε ≈ 27/n. And 27/n is the far country: rate n^(−1), the thing the whole construction industry was built to reach.

A caution, because the map should not flatter itself. The 1%-edited sample does not become van der Corput. It runs parallel to it — same slope, a constant above. It has joined the far country’s rate and not its constant, and the rate was always what the barrier was about. A second caution: the payoff is asymptotic. At four thousand points, editing one percent buys you a factor of two. At a quarter million, a factor of twelve. The spectacular version of this sentence lives at values of n that most people never reach. The road is real; the view from it is modest until you have walked a long way.

IV. Who has to move

Ask the map a more impertinent question. Which points?

The word “randomness” invites you to expect the answer the random ones — that a tax is levied across the sample, each point paying a little of the n^(−1/2). That is not what happens. Nothing about this is distributed.

Take a sample and compute, exactly, the minimum number of points that must move. The computation has a shape, and the shape is the answer: the number that must move is the total overflow of the crowded places. Walk the interval, find every stretch that received more points than it can pay for, add up the excess, choose the stretches so the sum is worst. That number — not a statistic about the sample, but an accounting of its crowded neighbourhoods — is exactly the number of points that must move. The rest never move at all.

So the sample is not uniformly guilty. It is 99% innocent and locally, sharply, criminal. The movers are the overflow of the clumps, and only the overflow: in a clump of four where three will fit, one moves. The other three stay exactly where chance put them, and end up in a low-discrepancy set, unedited, having done nothing but be lucky enough to be in a place that was not oversubscribed.

The n^(−1/2) — that famous number, that hard floor under every simulation ever run — is not paid by the sample. It is paid by a small minority of points standing in the wrong place, and charged to everyone.

V. Why there is a barrier at all

The last panel of the map answers the question the other three provoke: if the sample is 99% fine, where does the √n come from?

It comes from a scale. Pick any window of the interval holding w points. The count in that window wobbles by about √w — that is just the arithmetic of independence, and it is the same √ that shows up in the headline. Against that wobble stands a flat allowance: if you want discrepancy ε, a window may be off by about 2εn points and no more.

Now the two numbers race, and the race has three phases. In small windows the wobble √w is smaller than the allowance: free, nothing to fix. In enormous windows the wobble is large but there are hardly any windows, so the total damage is small. Between them is a scale where the wobble has just outgrown the allowance and there are still a great many windows to be wrong — and that is where the entire cost lives. Maximise over the scale and you get the binding width, w* ∝ (εn)², and you get the 1/m law falling out of the arithmetic.

I like this panel best because it makes the barrier a place. Not a property of randomness, not a law of information — a particular scale, at a particular width, where the wobble first becomes unaffordable. Above it and below it, randomness is free. The famous limitation of Monte Carlo lives at one address, and the map can print it.

VI. What the old map got wrong

It is worth being precise about the error, because the old map was not sloppy. Every number on it is correct. Random samples really do sit at 0.87/√n; constructed sets really do reach 0.48·log n/n; the gap really does grow like √n and really is unbounded. Nobody miscalculated.

What the map lacked was a scale bar. It recorded, accurately, that the two countries have very different altitudes, and it let the eye read altitude as distance. The altitude gap is real and enormous and grows forever. The distance is 1%. Both facts were always true; only one was ever drawn.

There is a habit hiding in that. When we find a quantity that will not improve — a rate, a bound, a floor — we tend to promote it from a fact about the value to a fact about the thing. The error is n^(−1/2), so randomness is n^(−1/2)-ish; irreducibly clumpy, essentially rough, wrong all the way through. But a number attached to an object is not a description of the object. It is one measurement, and the measurement everyone took was the altitude, over and over, for ninety years, while the question of how far you would have to walk went unasked.

The instructive part is that the unasked question was not hard. In one dimension it is a matching problem a careful undergraduate could set up, and its answer was published in 2025. What kept it unasked was not difficulty. It was that the first map was good enough to think with, and thinking with a map is how you stop seeing what it leaves out.
