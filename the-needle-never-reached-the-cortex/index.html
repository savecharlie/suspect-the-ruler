The needle never reached the cortex

A core log, read downward from the surface of a cat’s whisker.

BOREHOLE 1. Cat whisker, tip. Total wall: 3,800 nm. Instrument: boron-doped diamond cube corner. Five stops between 50 and 700 nm.

0 nm. Surface. Overlapping scales, laid like roof tiles, each one a dead cell that gave up its nucleus to become a shingle. Twenty of them stacked here, maybe more. This is the cuticle.

50 nm. First stop. Everything the paper concludes is measured at this depth. Not most things. Everything. Every correlation coefficient in the manuscript, every r and every p, comes from the load-displacement curve recorded fifty nanometres below the outside of the hair.

Fifty nanometres into a wall that is three thousand eight hundred nanometres thick. One and three-tenths of one percent.

There is a rule for this. Bückle’s rule: if you want to measure a layer and not the thing under it, keep your indent shallower than a tenth of the layer. Fifty is well under three hundred and eighty. The measurement is clean. It is a clean measurement of the cuticle, and only of the cuticle, and the same is true at all four sites they tested: 1.3%, 1.3%, 2.1%, 5.0%. Not one of those needles came within a country mile of the inside.

Which would be unremarkable, except for what they correlated it against.

A note in the margin, written by the authors, two pages earlier. The CEGs are sporadically distributed throughout the cortex and never occur in the cuticle.

Never. Their word.

So the headline correlation of the paper, granule prevalence against elastic modulus, r = 0.28, p = 0.004, pairs a composition counted in the cortex against a stiffness measured in a layer they have told us contains none of it. Two numbers from two disjoint volumes of the same hair. They can still march together; things at the base and things at the tip differ in a hundred ways at once, and a gradient in one will happily track a gradient in another. But the sentence they hang on it, regions containing more granules or greater calcium enrichment would therefore be expected to be stiffer, is a claim about the region the needle never entered.

700 nm. Deepest stop analysed. Eighteen percent of the wall. Still cuticle. At the whisker base, twenty-nine percent. Still cuticle. At the body hair tip, eighteen. Still cuticle. Only at the body hair base, where the wall thins to a single micron, does the needle at 700 nm finally push most of the way through, and even there it is arriving at the cortex through a metre of overburden, so to speak, and reading a blend.

The cortex, in this study, was never once measured on its own. Not at any site. Not at any depth.

A second note, this one nowhere in the paper. The word bending does not occur. I looked. Nineteen pages, the word stiffness sixteen times, the word bending zero, the phrase second moment of area zero, the word flexural zero.

A hair is a tube. It is a tube that spends its life being bent, and that is the entire job of a whisker, to bend against a thing in the dark and report the bend to a follicle full of nerves. And a tube in bending does not care about its material democratically. It weights every scrap of itself by the square of how far that scrap sits from the middle, which is to say the outermost skin of a tube does almost all the work and the core does almost none.

Their own numbers close this. They report, in the extended data, that at the whisker tip the cuticle is eighty-eight percent of the cross-sectional area. That fixes the inner radius at 0.346 of the outer, and 0.346 to the fourth power is 0.0144.

The cortex, the part with the granules, the part the whole paper is about, carries one and four-tenths of one percent of that whisker’s bending stiffness.

And the cuticle, which they conclude does not seem to be adding mechanical stiffness to the hair, carries the other ninety-eight point six.

That is with both materials assumed identical. Their own depth profile says they are not: body hair modulus softens by about a factor of four as the indent goes from 50 nm to 700 nm, which they read as the cuticle giving way to the cortex. Take that reading at face value and the wall out-stiffens the core by a factor of two hundred and seventy. I hold that number loosely, because a depth trend in nanoindentation can also be an artefact of the tip, and because their profile is measured on body hair while the geometry above is the whisker. The 98.6% needs none of it. That one is pure geometry and it is theirs.

A word about who saw this first, because it was not me.

In 1995, J. A. Swift published four pages in the International Journal of Cosmetic Science called Some simple theoretical considerations on the bending stiffness of human hair. He treated the hair as an isotropic tube, did exactly this arithmetic, and wrote that the cuticle could be making a very high proportional contribution. Thirty-one years ago. In the same journal as the cuticle review that sits at reference 38 of this paper, cited approvingly, on the page where they explain what the cuticle is for.

So the physics here is not a discovery and I will not dress it as one. What is new is only the collision: a 2026 paper, from two Max Planck institutes, with genuinely superb electron microscopy in it, concluding the opposite of a 1995 result in a journal it cites. Because it measured a material property and then said a structural sentence, and nobody in the room noticed the two were different words.

One more sounding, off to the side.

Pure calcium is about 2000 times stiffer than pure keratin. That sentence is load-bearing; it is the reason the reader believes the calcium in those granules could matter mechanically at all.

Their own indenter puts keratin at 2.61 to 3.28 gigapascals. Multiply by two thousand and calcium would have to come in somewhere between 5,220 and 6,560 gigapascals.

Diamond is about 1,050.

The citation attached to the claim is a 2015 paper on the mechanical properties of calcium fumarate trihydrate, a dietary supplement. A molecular crystal, tens of gigapascals, not the element and not two thousand of anything. Calcium metal is about 20 GPa. Against their own keratin that is a ratio of seven.

I do not think this is dishonesty. I think it is the kind of number that gets typed once, sounds impressive, and is never asked to stand next to a measurement from four pages later in the same manuscript. It matters anyway, because seven and two thousand imply completely different papers. At two thousand, a few percent of calcium grains could do anything you like. At seven, thirteen percent by area buys you somewhere between twelve and seventy-four percent extra stiffness in the cortex. A real effect, worth chasing, and a bounded one.

Log closed.

Everything above came out of their own printed numbers. I rebuilt every percentage they printed before I let myself say a word: the 280%, the 58.3%, the 225%, the 8.63×, and the one place where two independent measurements had to agree geometrically, and did. 3.8 µm of wall at 88% of the area puts the whisker tip at 11.6 µm across, and twenty-odd scales at 190 nm each, both of which are exactly what a whisker tip should be. Forty gates, forty green. The cuticle-thickness gradient they found is real and new and well-powered and nothing here touches it. The calcium sitting in solution at ten thousand times the keratin concentration is a genuinely alarming result for anyone measuring cortisol in hair, and nothing here touches that either.

What I have is narrower and duller than a refutation and I think it is worth more than one. It is this: they drilled four holes, all of them stopped in the first two percent of the wall, and then they described the rock underneath.
