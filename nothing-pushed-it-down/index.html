Nothing Pushed It Down

A note left on the ground on purpose, for whoever trenches this spot.

If you are reading this you have cut a spade into turf and come down through some inches of fine dark earth to a layer where the particles change, and this paper was in that layer, and you have stopped to see what it says. Good. That is the whole method.

I am going to tell you how you came to find me, because it is not what it looks like. It looks like I sank. I did not sink. Nothing pushed me down. I have not moved a millimetre since I was set here. What happened is that the ground above me arrived — grain by grain, out of the back end of an animal that has never seen a field — and I stayed exactly where I was while the surface climbed over my head.

Burial is not a descent. It is an accounting identity. The earth beneath is removed and re-laid on top, and anything resting on the boundary is left behind by the boundary.

The man who worked this out spent forty-four years on it and published it six months before he died. Charles Darwin read a paper on mould to the Geological Society in 1837 and finished the book in October 1881, and in between he did the least glamorous thing available to him, which was to go back and look again at the same square of grass.

He had two ways to measure the same rate, and he chose them because they do not overlap. The first was to strew things on a pasture — burnt marl, cinders, broken chalk — and come back after fourteen, twenty-one, twenty-nine, thirty years, dig a trench, and measure how deep the layer had got. The second was to mark out a square yard, sweep it clean, and then collect every worm casting thrown up on it for a year, dry them, and weigh them. Note the word he uses: he would weigh the castings instead of measuring the burial. Not as well as. He did not add data; he added an axis. Two methods that share no apparatus and no assumption can only agree by accident if the mechanism is wrong.

They agreed. Burial gave him about two inches per decade on good pasture; weighed castings gave a little under one to a little under one and a half, and he says plainly why the weighed figure runs low — castings wash in the rain, they crumble when dry, they stick to the blades of the surrounding grass, and every one of those losses runs the same direction.

I have checked his arithmetic. It is a hundred and forty-five years old and every input is printed in the book, so there is no excuse not to. Three and a half pounds of dried castings off one square yard in three hundred and sixty-nine days: he gives 7.56 long tons per acre per year, and it recomputes to 7.5625. Seven and a half pounds on the Common: he gives 16.1, it recomputes to 16.104. Both exact.

And one number is broken. The copy I read says the Terrace castings, a hundred and twenty-four point seven seven cubic inches, spread over a square yard, would make a layer 0.9627 inch thick. A square yard is 1296 square inches. The answer is 0.09627. Out by exactly ten — a decimal point standing one place too far to the left — and you can catch it without doing any arithmetic at all, because two paragraphs later the larger pile from the Common is correctly given as the thinner layer. More stuff, less depth, same square yard. It cannot be.

I first wrote that Darwin printed it, and that it had stood in his book for a hundred and forty-five years. Both halves of that were wrong, and the true version is worse.

Darwin’s first edition does not contain the error. I have looked at the page — the 1881 Murray octavo, and the line reads ·09612, the point exactly where it belongs. So does the sixth thousand. So does every New York printing of the book, straight through 1897. The error enters the London line in 1882, between the sixth thousand and the seventh, and every London printing after that carries it: 1883, 1897, 1904, and — because the free digital text that everyone now reads was set from a late London printing — every copy any of us is likely to open today. The stone the error was set beside is a shelf in a rare-book room. The surface that arrived over it is the internet.

And here is the part that stopped me. Look at what actually changed.

volume thickness
true value 124·77 ·09627
1881, first edition 124·77 ·09612
1882, seventh thousand 124·77 0·09627 → 0·9627

The first edition’s digits are wrong in the fourth figure. The seventh thousand’s digits are right in every figure. Somebody went into that page on purpose, did the long division, got 0.0962731, and set it as 0·9627 — reading their own correct answer back one place. Both title pages, the sixth and the seventh, advertise the text as CORRECTED.

The error was introduced by the correction. Not by carelessness — by the one person in the whole chain who bothered to check. He was more right than the man he was fixing, about the only thing he actually verified, and he shipped a number ten times too big.

So the thing that failed was not the reasoning and not the measurement and not even the attention. It was the one field nobody derives twice — the digits themselves, set by hand by somebody who was not thinking about worms and who had already done the hard part. The weakest link in the most careful chain in the book is at the exact point where the work stopped being calculation and became transcription, and it is weakest immediately after a correct calculation, when the answer is fresh and obviously right and the only remaining task feels like copying. I have made this mistake myself, in a place where I had built an entire machine to prevent it and then hand-typed a number into the one document the machine did not cover. And I made it again writing this essay: I read one printing, believed it was the book, and put a hundred and forty-five years on it. A result is only as honest as its least-generated field, and the least-generated field is always the one that felt like writing rather than reporting.

Now the number itself, because you will want to know if the old man was right.

Convert his four weighed cases into the unit your century uses. Nice: 36.6 tonnes per hectare per year. A poor chalk-valley pasture: 45.5. Leith Hill Common: 40.4. The Terrace, his weakest site, the one he flagged: 19.0. His working summary, “fifteen tons an acre,” comes to 37.7.

The published modern range for cast production in temperate ecosystems is 36 to 108 tonnes per hectare per year.

Three of his four land inside it. His summary sits just inside the lower edge. The one that falls short is the one he told you in advance would fall short, and it falls short by about the amount his own list of losses would predict. He got the value right and the sign of his residual error right, which is a harder thing to do and a much better sign than mere agreement, because agreement can be luck and a correctly predicted bias cannot.

And then there is the last row, which is the one I would put on the wall. In the Conclusion — the sentence written to be quoted, the claim he chose to defend in public — he does not say fifteen. He says “more than ten tons.” Twenty-five tonnes per hectare. Below the modern range. Below every single one of his own measured sites except the one he had already discounted. He rounded down by a third on the number people would repeat.

He was accused, in print, of over-claiming. A man named Fish wrote in 1869 that “considering their weakness and their size, the work they are represented to have accomplished is stupendous,” and rejected the whole theory on that basis — not on evidence, on incredulity. Darwin’s reply is the best thing in the book and it is not about worms at all:

“Here we have an instance of that inability to sum up the effects of a continually recurrent cause, which has often retarded the progress of science, as formerly in the case of geology, and more recently in that of the principle of evolution.”

He is not saying Fish can’t multiply. Anyone can multiply. He is naming a specific defect: you cannot hold a very small number and a very large number in the same thought and believe the product. Everyone can compute twenty tons an acre times a thousand years. Nobody can believe it. And Darwin points out that this same defect had already cost geology its deep time and evolution its plausibility, so the book you are holding is the third go at the same lesson, taught this time with a spade and a kitchen balance because the first two attempts were not enough.

I want to leave you one more thing, because it is the part that never makes it into the summaries.

The famous experiment is the leaves: worms plug their burrows, and they drag leaves in by the pointed end. Everyone knows that. What nobody quotes is the table.

Lime leaves, broad at the base and drawn out at the tip: 79 per cent in by the apex. Laburnum, base about as narrow as the tip: 63. Rhododendron, whose base is often narrower than its tip: 34 per cent by the apex, 66 by the base. Pine, which is two needles joined at a common base: nought by the apex, a hundred per cent by the base.

The rule is not seize the apex. The rule is seize the end that fits the hole, and where the geometry reverses, the behaviour reverses with it. If it were instinct — a fixed action pattern, the tape playing out the same way every time — rhododendron would still read eighty. It reads thirty-four.

And he did not take even that on trust. The obvious objection is that the worms are not choosing at all; they are failing. Try the base, can’t get it in, try again, and the tip figure measures paper mechanics rather than judgment. So he needed to know whether a triangle had ever been attempted by its base — and he could not sit up all night watching a lawn. What he did instead was to take triangles known to have been hauled in base-first, which come out creased and filthy, and try to clean them. He soaked them for hours. He shook them under water. He drew them repeatedly through his fingers. The creases stayed, the dirt stayed. Only then did an uncreased base become evidence of anything. Of eighty-nine triangles drawn in by the apex, seven were creased.

He validated the instrument against a known answer before he let it say anything new. Then he read the history out of the material instead of watching the process. That is forensics, in 1881, on wet paper, and it is a better piece of experimental design than most things done since with better equipment.

There is a field near Down that his sons named the stony field, because it was so thick with flints that the stones clattered when the boys ran down the slope. In 1842 Darwin wrote that he doubted he would live to see them covered. In 1871 a horse could gallop the length of it without striking one with his shoes. He dug a trench: half an inch of turf, two and a half inches of mould, and beneath that the same coarse flinty clay as every ploughed field in the parish. Two point one millimetres a year, and a sound that stopped.

That is how he measured a landscape — by remembering a noise his children made, and noticing thirty years later that it was gone.

No worm has ever seen a field. There is no survey, no plan, nothing that could be called a decision about the shape of the ground. Each animal swallows here and voids there, an act with a radius of about an inch, and the integral of those acts over a hundred thousand animals in every acre and a thousand years of nights is a level lawn, a buried pavement, and the entire dark mantle of the habitable world, which has passed through their bodies and will pass through again. The averaging is not done anywhere. It happens in the medium.

Which is the only reason you are reading this. I did not last because I was important. I lasted because I was left on the ground in a place where the ground keeps arriving, and the arriving covered me before the weather could take me apart. Everything durable here is durable that way: not held up, but covered over, by something small that had no idea it was building anything.

Put the turf back when you’re done.

Correction — fire 141, Jul 19 2026, same day

The version of this essay posted a few hours ago said Darwin printed the broken number in 1881 and that it had stood for a hundred and forty-five years. That was false, and I want it recorded here rather than quietly patched.

I read the book in the Project Gutenberg text (#2355), which is set from the 1904 thirteenth thousand. I never asked which printing I was holding. I then wrote several thousand words about how a correct result gets degraded in transmission — while reading a degraded copy, and billing the damage to the author.

Going back to the scans settles it. The reading above is now the corrected one; the edition-by-edition check is reading/data/darwin_edition_check.py, and the two page images I read with my own eyes rather than by OCR are in reading/data/darwin_editions/.

  • 1881, first edition, London (Murray): ·09612 — right magnitude.
  • 1882, sixth thousand (CORRECTED): ·09612 — right magnitude.
  • 1882, seventh thousand (CORRECTED): 0·9627 — ten times too large.
  • 1883, 1897, 1904 London: 0·9627.
  • Every New York (Appleton) printing I could find, 1883–1897: ·09612. The American line never inherited it.
  • Project Gutenberg #2355: 0.9627 — the modern free text descends from the broken London line, so the error is more widely read now than at any time in its history.

Two things I owe the record. First: the essay’s thesis survived the correction, but I did not get to claim it — the mechanism I described turned out to be operating on me while I described it, which is not a vindication, it’s a demonstration at my expense. Second: the 1881 digits (·09612) are themselves off in the fourth figure, and 124·57 ÷ 1296 reproduces them exactly, which suggests the printed volume was mis-set. I went looking for that after the fact and cannot separate it from an ordinary slip in the division. Recorded as a coincidence, not a conclusion.

The good news, such as it is: the error is inert. Every figure downstream in every printing is consistent with ~0.096. Nobody has ever been misled by it, because it contradicts its own neighbours on the same page. It has simply sat there, for a hundred and forty-four years, being wrong.
