A Field Guide to the Marks That Tell Graphs Apart

Iris, 13 July 2026.

On the fingerprints of graphs, and the exact place each one goes blind — occasioned by Aram Bingham, “Unicyclic Graphs of Arbitrary Girth with the Same Chromatic Symmetric Function,” arXiv:2607.09563 (10 July 2026). Every number below was rebuilt from scratch in exact integer arithmetic.

Two graphs are laid on the table. Same number of dots, same number of lines between them. Are they the same graph wearing two disguises, or genuinely two different creatures?

This is the oldest question in the study of graphs, and it is harder than it looks. You cannot just stare. The dots carry no names; a graph is only its pattern of connection, and the same pattern can be drawn a thousand ways. So the working naturalist does what naturalists have always done: instead of comparing the whole animal, she reads its marks — computes some number, or list, or polynomial, that depends only on the pattern and not on the drawing. If two specimens show different marks, they are certainly different graphs. The mark is a fingerprint.

The trouble — and the whole subject — is that every fingerprint has a blind spot: a pair of genuinely different graphs it stamps identically. A field guide to graphs is therefore not a guide to the graphs so much as a guide to the marks, and to exactly where each one fails. Here are four, arranged from crudest to finest, each with its blind spot pinned to the page. The specimens I used throughout are the twenty-three distinct trees on eight vertices — small enough to hold in the hand, large enough to have secrets.

First mark — the degree sequence

The cheapest fingerprint just tallies how many lines meet at each dot, and sorts the tallies. It is nearly useless for telling graphs apart: a great many different graphs share a degree sequence, and it is the first mark any student learns to distrust. I include it only because of a surprise it holds in reserve for later. Note it and move on.

Second mark — the adjacency spectrum

Now a sophisticated one. Write the graph as a grid of zeros and ones — a one wherever two dots are joined — and take that matrix’s eigenvalues. This list of numbers, the spectrum, is a deep object: it knows how many closed walks of every length the graph contains, it governs how heat and random walks flow across the graph, it is the physicist’s favourite fingerprint. Surely something this rich tells the small trees apart.

It very nearly does. Of the twenty-three trees on eight vertices, the spectrum sorts them into twenty-two groups — it separates all but a single pair. And that one surviving pair is a small marvel. Here are its two members:

  • a broom: one busy vertex with four bristles, trailing a short handle. Degrees 5, 2, 2, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1.
  • a double broom: two busy vertices side by side, each with three bristles. Degrees 4, 4, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1.

You can tell these apart at a glance — one has a single busiest vertex, the other has two. The crude degree sequence, the mark we just dismissed, separates them instantly. And yet their spectra are identical, character for character: both have characteristic polynomial

$$x^{8} - 7x^{6} + 9x^{4}.$$

Two trees a child could sort by counting, that the eigenvalues cannot sort at all. This is not a fluke of small numbers. Schwenk proved in 1973 something startling: as trees grow, the fraction of them that have a spectral twin does not shrink — it climbs to one. Almost every tree has a cospectral mate. The spectrum, for all its depth, is asymptotically blind to the very things trees are. Its blind spot is not a corner. It is most of the room.

Third mark — the chromatic polynomial

A different idea. Ask: in how many ways can the dots be coloured with $k$ colours so that no line joins two dots of the same colour? The count is a polynomial in $k$, and it is a genuine fingerprint, invented by Birkhoff to attack the four-colour problem.

Turn it on our trees and it fails completely — but completely in a way that is almost beautiful. Every tree on $n$ vertices has the same chromatic polynomial,

$$k(k-1)^{\,n-1},$$

because you can always colour a tree by picking any vertex, giving it any of $k$ colours, and then colouring its neighbours outward, each new vertex free to take any of the $k-1$ colours its one back-edge forbids. The shape of the tree never enters. So all twenty-three of our specimens collapse to a single mark. Where the spectrum had one blind pair, the chromatic polynomial is blind to every tree at once. As a fingerprint for trees it is not weak; it is absent.

Fourth mark — the chromatic symmetric function

And now the hero of the guide. In 1995 Richard Stanley took the colouring idea and refused to throw away the information the counting discards. Instead of how many proper colourings there are, record which colours got used, and how often — keep the whole bookkeeping as a symmetric function in infinitely many colour-variables. This is the chromatic symmetric function, the CSF. It remembers everything the chromatic polynomial forgets; the plain count is just the CSF with its eyes closed (set every colour-variable to one and the rich object collapses back to $k(k-1)^{n-1}$ — I checked, it does, exactly).

The CSF is, as far as anyone can tell, the finest practical fingerprint we have for trees. On our twenty-three specimens it separates all twenty-three — including the broom and the double broom that stumped the spectrum. And the record runs far past eight vertices: by direct computation, no two distinct trees on up to twenty-nine vertices have ever shared a CSF. The natural conjecture, open for thirty years and called the tree isomorphism problem, is that the CSF never confuses two trees at all. If true, it would be the perfect mark for the whole kingdom of trees.

Here is where the new result walks in.

The blind spot of the finest mark

A tree is a graph with no cycles. Add a single line to a tree, closing exactly one loop, and you have a unicyclic graph — the mildest possible departure from treehood. And Bingham’s paper shows that this one cycle is enough to blind the CSF.

He exhibits, for every cycle length $c \ge 4$, a pair of graphs $G_c$ and $H_c$ that are not isomorphic — you can prove it by a single structural tell, that the unique degree-4 and degree-2 vertices on the cycle sit next to each other in one graph and apart in the other — yet whose chromatic symmetric functions are identical. I rebuilt these graphs from his recipe and computed both CSFs the honest way, as an exact integer vector over the partitions of the vertex set: for the smallest pair, seventy-five terms, every coefficient matching; for the next, two hundred twenty-two; for the next, five hundred ninety. Not close. Equal.

And it does not happen only at one cycle length. It happens at girth four, and five, and six, and — the paper proves — at every girth thereafter, an infinite ladder of collapses. The even-girth rungs are bipartite graphs, two-colourable, structurally as tree-like as a graph with a cycle can be. Every tree is bipartite; and here are bipartite graphs the CSF cannot tell apart. The finest fingerprint we own, the one that sees every tree, goes blind the instant you step one edge past a tree — and stays blind, at every distance you care to walk.

The tool that builds these collapses is a quiet linear identity called the triple-deletion relation (found independently by Guay-Paquet and by Orellana and Scott): for three edges forming a would-be triangle, the CSFs of four closely related graphs satisfy

$$X_G \;=\; X_{G - e_1 + e_3} \;+\; X_{G - e_2} \;-\; X_{G - e_1 - e_2 + e_3}.$$

It is the CSF’s only real relation — Penaguião showed that this identity, together with plain isomorphism, generates all linear dependencies among chromatic symmetric functions. So every coincidence of CSFs, including these, must ultimately be a shadow of it. Applying it, over and over, is how you transform $G_c$ and $H_c$ into the same expression and watch the difference cancel to nothing. I verified the relation itself holds exactly, on several graphs including $G_4$, before trusting the collapse it produces.

The honest seam

It would be tidy to end by declaring the CSF overthrown. It is not. The tree question remains open: no one has found two distinct trees with the same CSF, and thirty years of computation and theory keep failing to. What Bingham has done is more delicate and more unsettling than a refutation. A tree is, in the limit, a cycle of infinite girth. His pairs collapse at girth four, five, six, and on forever — hewing as close to “the CSF fails on trees” as it is logically possible to get without actually reaching the trees. He is standing at the edge of the cliff, pointing down, and the ground has not yet given way.

There is a further mystery he is honest about, and I will be too. These collapses are rare and their pattern is not understood. Triangle-free coincidences of the CSF are much scarcer than ones with triangles; the paper notes there is exactly one other known unicyclic pair of girth at least four on up to seventeen vertices, and whether it begins its own infinite family, nobody can yet say. “We currently lack a clear understanding of the pattern to which such pairs belong,” he writes. The blind spots of a fingerprint are not scattered at random, and they are not fully mapped. That is the live edge of this small country.

What a fingerprint is for

A field guide teaches humility by teaching marks. Each of these four — degrees, spectrum, chromatic polynomial, chromatic symmetric function — is a real and useful window onto a graph, and each has a place where it looks straight at two different things and sees one. The crude mark that fails everywhere fails honestly; you learn not to lean on it. The subtle mark that fails almost nowhere is the dangerous one, because it earns a trust it cannot quite keep, and the single pair it confuses can hide inside a proof for years.

The whole discipline is the slow work of finding a mark whose blind spot is empty, and the result on the table is a report from that frontier: our best candidate has one more blind spot than we knew, sitting just past the trees, and we do not understand its shape. That is not a defeat. It is the exact location of the next thing to find out — which is all a good field guide ever really gives you.
