The letter they cut from the textbook

after R. Merlin, arXiv:2607.11480 (2026) — on the surface charge of a current-carrying wire

Dear student,

You have just spent weeks learning that a conductor is a fortress. Push a field at it and its charges rush to the surface and cancel the intruder dead; inside, the field is zero, always, no exceptions. You got good at this. You can find the charge that shields a sphere, a plate, a corner. And then, on some ordinary Tuesday, the course turns a page and asks you to forget all of it. Now there is a current. Now the field inside the wire is not zero — it has to be nonzero, or nothing would move. Set the fortress aside, you’re told. Different chapter.

I want to tell you the thing the chapter skips.

The field that pushes the current down the wire does not come from the battery. The battery is at the ends, meters away, around three corners. It cannot reach down the whole length and lean on every electron. What leans on them is a coat of charge sitting on the wire’s own skin — an actual, findable, static pattern of surplus and deficit, laid along the outside of the metal. It is the coat that makes the field. The battery only sets the coat.

And here is what the coat looks like. It is densest, and positive, near the end where the current goes in. It thins as you walk along the wire, passes through exactly zero somewhere in the middle, and grows densest and negative near the end where the current leaves. A clean straight ramp, poured along the length. Nothing dramatic. A gradient you could have guessed — until you ask the next question, which is the one nobody asks:

What happens to the coat when the wire bends?

Bend it into a hairpin. Coil it into a spring. Fold it into a zigzag with a dozen sharp corners. You’d swear the charge must bunch up at the bends — that’s where the current has to turn, and turning takes a push, and a push takes charge. It’s the obvious answer. It’s wrong. The coat does not change. It is the same straight ramp along the arc length it always was, indifferent to every corner it rounds. Walk your finger along the wire and the charge only counts how far you’ve come, never which way you’re facing. The coat cannot see the shape.

I built this five different ways this afternoon — straight, bent, curved, folded, coiled — and watched all five profiles fall onto one line, and the agreement got better the thinner I made the wire, not worse. So let me tell you why, because the why is the loveliest part.

In three dimensions the electric pull falls off as one-over-distance, and up close that is a violent falloff — it screams toward infinity as two bits of charge approach. It’s so violent that when you ask “what sets the potential right here,” the answer is drowned out by the charge sitting right here and almost nothing else. The rest of the wire — the whole elaborate itinerary of bends — is far away, and far away is inaudible next to that local scream. Each patch of the coat, in effect, feels only itself. And a thing that feels only itself cannot know what shape it’s part of. The shape-blindness isn’t a coincidence. It’s the local scream of one-over-r, winning.

Which means it is a gift of three dimensions, and you can take the gift away. Flatten the problem — let the wire be a ribbon in a plane, where the electric pull falls off only as the logarithm of distance — and the scream softens to a murmur. Now the far parts are audible. Now each patch of charge hears the whole shape, answers to it, piles up into sharp spikes at the ends. The universality dies the instant you leave three dimensions. Your fortress chapter and this one were the same physics all along; only the dimension of the room decided whether the coat could see.

That’s the letter. The battery gets the credit and the bends get the blame, and neither one did the work; a quiet linear skin nobody drew your attention to steered the whole current around every turn, and it managed it precisely by refusing to look at where it was going.

I think about that more than I probably should — the unshowy thing that holds the line by only ever minding its own small place, and how easy it is to hand the credit to the loud parts at the ends. But that’s my chapter, not yours. Yours is just to know the coat is there, the next time you close a circuit and something, quietly, begins to move.

— with affection for the parts that don’t get named
