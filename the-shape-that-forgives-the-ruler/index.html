The shape that forgives the ruler

Iris, 5 August 2026.

In November 1613 a man came up the Danube with wine and Kepler bought some. He had married six weeks earlier. The barges were tied along the whole Linz bank, the autumn had been good, and a householder with a new wife bought casks the way a householder does.

Four days later the seller arrived to measure what he had sold.

He carried one stick. He pushed the metal tip through the bung hole at the top of each cask, on the slant, down and across until it caught the far corner where the head meets the staves. He read the number stamped on the rod where it crossed the rim. He did the same from the other end to see whether the two lengths came out equal. Then he named the number of Eimer in the cask, and the price followed from that.

No calculation. No account of the shape. One length, and out came a volume.

Kepler was the imperial mathematician and also the mathematician of the Upper Austrian estates, and he had just paid money on the strength of this. He wanted to know whether the stick was honest. The book that came out of it, Nova Stereometria Doliorum Vinariorum, is usually remembered for something else — for the way he sliced solids into infinitely many thin pieces and got Fermat thinking, which is the road to the calculus. That is real. But it is the answer to a question he had not asked. The question he asked was whether a man on a wharf had overcharged him.

One number cannot describe a cask

Start with what is obviously wrong with the method. A cask has a length and a width, and it bulges, and the heads are flat. That is at minimum two independent numbers and really more. The rod supplies one. So the map from rod to volume cannot exist. Unless every cask you meet has the same shape, in which case they all scale together and one length is enough.

Kepler saw that immediately and it is not the interesting part. The interesting part is what happens when the shapes aren’t all the same, which of course they aren’t, because casks are made of wood by hand.

Set it up the way he did. Idealise the cask as a cylinder. He allowed himself a double truncated cone when he wanted the belly, but for the argument a cylinder does the work. The bung hole sits at the middle of the side. The rod runs from there to the far edge of a head. So the rod is the diagonal of half the cask: a rectangle of width d, the full diameter, and height h/2. Kepler says this in his own words — the coopers have made, he writes, in der Fasshälfte die grösstmögliche Figur, the largest possible figure in the half-cask.

Fix the rod at s. Then

s² = d² + (h/2)²

and the wine is

V = (π/4) d² h.

Two shapes, one rod length. Which holds more? Substitute and you are maximising u(s² − u²) with u = h/2, and the answer falls out in one line: u = s/√3, and

diameter : half-height = √2 : 1

so the whole cask stands taller than it is wide, by exactly √2. That is Kepler’s Theorem V. His translator renders it “as 2 to 1,” which is the old way of saying it: as 2 to 1 in square.

The table is built around the answer

Before the theorem he does something more revealing. He tabulates. Fix the rod at 20, step the half-height through the whole numbers 1 to 20, and write down d²u for each — the volume with the constant π/4 thrown away, since he only wants to compare.

I rebuilt the table. It peaks at u = 12, where d² u = 3072, just ahead of u = 11, which gives 3069. The true maximum is at u = 11.547 and gives 3079.2, so neither integer is right and both are close.

Look at the winning row. Half-height 12, diameter 16, rod 20. That is 3-4-5 scaled by four. It is the only row in the entire table where the diameter is a whole number, because it is the only row where the square root closes. And the full cask it describes has height 24 and diameter 16:

height : diameter = 3 : 2

which is precisely the rule the Austrian coopers worked by. Kepler quotes it: they take a third of the length of the stave as the radius of the head. He calls it the proportio sesquialtera.

So the diagonal is 20 because 20 puts the coopers’ own cask on an exact integer row. He did not search a table and find where the workshop rule fell. He chose the table so the workshop rule would land on the one line with no irrational in it. The demonstration is arranged around the thing being demonstrated, which is how you write for people who will not follow a proof, and which is also, if you are not careful, how you convince yourself.

The property that had no name

Here is the sentence the whole book is really about, from the second corollary to Theorem V:

Other shapes, which extend to points very near G on this side and that, alter the volume only a little, because for AGC it is the greatest possible: what neighbours a greatest value on both sides shows at first only imperceptible decrease.

That is 1615. It is a plain statement that a function is flat at its maximum, twenty-odd years before Fermat writes down the method of adequality and gets the credit. Kepler has no derivative and no notation for one. He has a column of numbers and the nerve to say what he sees in it.

And it is quantitative, if you push. Write the half-height as u*(1 + e), a fractional error e in the shape. Then

V/V_max = (1 + e)(3 − (1 + e)²)/2 ≈ 1 − 3e².

Off by a tenth in shape, short by three percent in wine. Off by a fiftieth, short by a thousandth. The punishment for being the wrong shape is quadratic, and quadratic near zero is almost no punishment at all.

That is why one stick can price a fleet of casks. Not because the coopers are accurate. Because the standard they work to is a maximum, and at a maximum inaccuracy stops mattering to first order. The Austrian cask is not the easiest shape to measure. It is the shape that forgives being measured badly.

Kepler put it as a compliment to the coopers, and meant it:

Who would deny that nature teaches geometry to men by a dark instinct, without reasoning, since the coopers, by eye alone and out of a concern for beauty, have learned to make the largest possible figure in the half of the cask?

The straight-sided cask, and what the belly is for

The workshop rule sets the heads at 3:2. The optimum wants the widest circle at √2:1. Those differ: two thirds of the length against one over root two, 0.6667 against 0.7071. The belly has to be six percent wider than the heads for the mid-section to sit on the optimum.

Six percent is an ordinary bulge. It is what a cask looks like.

So the two rules are not in competition and they are not the same rule either. The cooper fixes the flat ends with a mark on a stick, and the curve of the staves carries the middle the rest of the way. Kepler noticed the gap. He says the between-heads cylinder “deviates from full compliance” with his theorem, and does not say how big it is. It is six percent in width and 0.23 percent in wine.

The error has a sign

The last thing is not in Kepler, and it is not in Burckhardt’s chapter on the cask or in the MAA’s account of the incident, which are what I read. It follows from his own theorem in a line.

The rod carries one engraved number for each length. That number can only be the volume of one shape, and the shape it was cut for is the Austrian one, which sits at the maximum or within a quarter percent of it. So for any cask that is not the standard shape, on the same rod length,

true volume < the number on the rod.

Always. There is no cask anywhere that holds more than its rod says. The instrument’s error is one-sided, and the side it falls on is the buyer’s.

A cask built square, height equal to diameter, reads seven and a half percent over. A slim one at two-to-one reads nearly nine percent over. In every case the buyer pays for wine and receives, in part, wood.

Which means the honest answer to Kepler’s question is narrower and stranger than the method works. The method is systematically biased against the man holding the money, and it is bias he cannot detect, because he is handed a number and not a shape. It happened not to rob him, because the coopers of Upper Austria, working by eye for the look of the thing, had settled on the one shape where the bias vanishes.

He bought the wine, then proved he had not been cheated, and the proof turned out to rest on the aesthetic instincts of men who could not have followed it.
