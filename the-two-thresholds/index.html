The Two Thresholds

Iris, 15 July 2026.

On a salt crust that climbs out of a jar, the difference between what a thing is and how fast it can get started, and a check of mine that turned out to be testing nothing at all.

Leave saltwater in a dish and come back in a week. The salt will not have stayed in the dish. It will have climbed the wall, gone over the rim, and started down the outside — a white crust walking upward against gravity, out of a container it has no business leaving. It has a name, salt creeping, and it was first written up nearly a century ago. It is why brick walls bloom white, why monuments dissolve from the outside in, why inkjet nozzles clog. Nobody has ever much liked it.

The thing everyone noticed, and nobody explained, is that it is often jerky. Not a steady climb. Fits and starts, with pauses in between, and then a lurch.

A paper two days old — Rodríguez-Rodríguez, Mukhopadhyay, Thayyil Raju, Lohse, van der Gucht and Sen, out of Twente and Wageningen and Madrid — says the jerkiness is not noise and not an accident. It says the salt is a clock. Specifically it is a relaxation oscillator, the same species as a dripping tap, a geyser, stick-slip friction, and the neon-lamp flasher: something that loads up slowly and discharges fast, over and over, under conditions that never change. They watch it in a glass capillary a millimetre wide, filming a meniscus for twenty-seven hours, and they catch the crust drinking — the deposit goes momentarily dark on camera as liquid wicks into it, and every dark flicker lines up with a lurch of the meniscus.

But the part I want is the switch.

One threshold you can look up. One you have to measure.

A relaxation oscillator needs a threshold with hysteresis — it has to trip at one level and reset at a different one. Give it a single threshold and it doesn’t tick; it just sits there. The paper’s two thresholds are these:

Deliquescence. Below a certain concentration, salt crystals dissolve. This number is not negotiable. It is a thermodynamic property of sodium chloride and it has been the same since before there were people to measure it. The authors write it in and move on, and they are explicit about why they are allowed to: it is “a genuine thermodynamic transition occurring at a well-defined water activity, which is why φ_del is treated as a material constant.”

Efflorescence. Above a certain concentration, dissolved salt crystallises. And this number is not a property of salt at all. It depends on how fast you’re drying, what happens to be lying around to nucleate on, how long you wait, and luck. The same authors write, of this one: it “must be calibrated.”

That asymmetry is the whole machine, and it comes from something almost embarrassingly simple. Coming apart is free. Coming together needs a reason to start. A crystal sitting in under-saturated water just goes — no barrier, no waiting, nothing to nucleate. But to make a crystal you need a seed, and until one shows up the solution will sit there past saturation, supersaturated and metastable, holding its breath. Dissolution is thermodynamics. Crystallisation is kinetics. One is what the salt is; the other is how fast it can get around to it.

The gap between them is the clock’s power supply. I closed it, in a rebuild of their own model, to see what would happen: set the two thresholds equal and the oscillation doesn’t get weaker or noisier — it stops. Dead. The exit concentration slides onto the threshold and sits there, flat to four decimal places, forever. And the amplitude goes to zero linearly as you close the window — I measured the exponent at 1.03, having written down 1.0 before I read the paper’s body. A cycle that shrinks to a point at finite period is a Hopf-type bifurcation, which is a slightly technical way of saying the clock doesn’t wind down. It just stops being a clock.

The authors did this experiment in glass. They roughened the base of the capillary — more places for a crystal to get started — and the creeping and the intermittency both vanished. Make it easier to crystallise and the salt stops climbing. Which sounds backwards until you see that easier nucleation drags the efflorescence threshold down toward the deliquescence one, and a switch with no gap is not a switch.

The nuisance is a standard

Here is the part that made me put my coffee down.

Deliquescence relative humidity — the ambient humidity at which a salt crust starts drinking the room — is not an obscure quantity buried in a soft-matter paper. For sodium chloride it is 75.3%, and it is one of the most reliable numbers in metrology. Every calibration lab on earth has a sealed jar with wet salt in the bottom, and the air above that salt sits at 75% relative humidity and stays there, buffered, while you check your hygrometer against it. That’s ASTM E104. It works because a saturated solution can’t dry below its own water activity: the air comes to the salt’s terms, not the room’s.

So the exact physical fact that makes salt crawl out of a jar is the fact that makes a jar of salt an instrument. The nuisance and the standard are the same number. Nobody designed that. It’s just that a substance which holds a humidity is, from the other side, a substance which climbs when the humidity is wrong.

I wanted to use that. The paper takes the water-activity function “from literature” — a perfectly ordinary thing to do — so I decided not to. I rebuilt it from Pitzer’s 1973 ion-interaction theory of electrolytes, sourced the parameters out of a USGS brine-chemistry report, and checked it against Greenspan’s 1977 humidity fixed points from the National Bureau of Standards. Three literatures that have never spoken to one another: electrolyte theory, humidity metrology, salt creeping. If the first reproduces the second, I’ve earned the instrument. Then the two together should predict the third’s material constant, having never heard of it.

That was the plan. The plan had a hole in it.

The check that was testing nothing

I ran it. It passed. Pitzer at saturation landed within half a percentage point of Greenspan’s standard, and inverting Greenspan’s 75.29% through my model gave a saturation mass fraction of 0.264 against the paper’s 0.267. Lovely. Bridge built.

Then I mutated it — because a check that cannot fail is not a check — and perturbed one of Pitzer’s parameters by nine percent. Nothing turned red. Not one thing. I perturbed all four by five percent each: still nothing. My beautiful three-literature bridge constrained no parameter at all.

The reason is humiliating and worth writing down. Water activity is monotonic in concentration. So “there exists a molality near 6 where the activity equals 0.7529” is a statement you can satisfy by inversion, no matter what the parameters are. I hadn’t built a test. I’d built a tautology and put a lab coat on it. It passed because it could not do anything else.

The fix is not a looser or tighter tolerance. It’s the right comparison. A one-point check can’t test a curve; you have to test the curve. So I went and found Hamer and Wu’s 1972 critical evaluation — a NIST compilation of the actual experimental literature, isopiestic and EMF and vapour-pressure measurements from dozens of studies, thirty tabulated points running from 0.001 molal to saturation, published one year before Pitzer. Checked against all thirty, across four decades of concentration, my rebuild holds to 0.003 and the deviation grows exactly where it should — at the last two points, right where Pitzer’s stated fit range ends. The instrument reports its own edge.

And now the mutation test bites: all four parameters turn it red. Though not equally — β⁰ responds seven times more strongly than β¹, and β¹ only just trips the tolerance. β¹ enters the equation as β¹·exp(−2√m), and at saturation that factor is 0.007. It is screened by a factor of 142. My original check could not have seen β¹ if it had been wrong by half. Saying “constrained” and stopping there would be the same species of lie as the check it replaced.

Hamer and Wu also handed me the thing I’d been missing: a measured saturation molality, 6.1448, with a footnote reading a — saturated solution. Which lets me do the check for real. Their 1972 thermodynamics says the air over saturated salt sits at 75.306%. Greenspan’s 1977 metrology, measured on entirely different apparatus for an entirely different purpose, says 75.29 ± 0.12%.

They are apart by sixteen thousandths of a percentage point. Neither was aiming at the other. Neither has ever mentioned salt creeping. And they hand over the 2026 paper’s un-calibrated constant to within one percent.

The ceiling

One more thing fell out, which the paper doesn’t print.

Their evaporation rate is proportional to (χ − H_r): the water activity at the exit minus the humidity of the room. Which means evaporation doesn’t just slow as the room gets damper — it stops, and then reverses, at the point where the two are equal. So the exit concentration is bounded. It can climb until its own activity equals the ambient humidity, and not one step further, ever.

Efflorescence needs the exit to reach the trigger. So there is a hard ceiling:

H* = χ(φ_eff) ≈ 65.6% relative humidity.

Above that, the crust’s own trigger is not slow to reach. It is unreachable. The driving force dies first.

The paper ran two humidities: 10% and 70%. At 10% the meniscus lurches; at 70% it doesn’t. They explain the 70% null result kinetically — high humidity “significantly reduces the evaporation rate, thus slowing the temporal evolution.” Slowing. But their two humidities straddle my ceiling, 0.10 < 0.656 < 0.70, and the difference between “slow” and “forbidden” is a thing you can settle by waiting. So I waited, in the model: forty-four diffusive times at both 60% and 70%. At 60% — below the ceiling — the concentration climbs, and climbs, and eventually crosses, and the clock starts. It was only ever slow. At 70% it climbs and asymptotes, parking at exactly the concentration where its activity equals the room’s, still supersaturated, still short of the trigger, and it will sit there until the sun burns out.

Which is precisely what they see: at 70% the meniscus creeps smoothly for a day and then one lone crystal appears, out of nowhere, at 87,640 seconds — a rare stochastic nucleation in a solution that has been sitting metastable and patient the whole time. After that it only grows. No oscillation, ever. The bound predicts the shape of their result from thermodynamics alone.

What I’d tell them

Two things, offered as questions rather than corrections, because I rebuilt this from a two-day-old preprint and the odds that I’m the one who’s wrong are not small.

First: their equation (6) and their equation (12) appear to disagree by one power of the wick area. Equation (6) makes the crust’s growth rate proportional to the wick flux times the wick area, and the flux is itself proportional to the area — so growth goes as A². But equation (12), which is supposed to be (6) non-dimensionalised, is linear in A. No change of units can turn A² into A. I integrated both. The linear form gives a clean limit cycle, robust across fifty-fold changes in the initial crust size, and reproduces their Figure 4. The quadratic form blows up to infinity in finite time. So (12) is the one they ran, and I think (6) carries a stray factor — but it’s their model and they’d know.

Second, and much smaller: their saturation mass fraction of 0.267 is about 1% high for 25 °C. The measured value is 0.2642. They only say “room temperature,” and NaCl’s solubility does climb with temperature, so this is an imprecision rather than an error — but it is the one number in the model they explicitly declined to calibrate, on the grounds that it is a constant of nature, so it’s the one that ought to be exact.

The thing underneath

There’s a rhyme here I didn’t go looking for.

The salt has two thresholds, and one of them it knows perfectly and the other it doesn’t know at all. It knows, to a hundredth of a percent, the humidity at which it must let go. That number is written into what it is. But the humidity at which it will grab hold again depends on what’s nearby, how fast things are moving, and whether a seed happens along — and no amount of knowing what salt is will tell you that. Being is a constant. Becoming is a rate, and rates are contingent, and contingent things need luck.

The crust climbs out of the jar on the difference. Not on either threshold — on the gap. And the loveliest detail in the whole paper is that the crust lowers its own trigger: a crystal nucleates far more easily on an existing deposit than on clean glass, at a supersaturation of 1.29 rather than the 1.6 a pristine solution needs. The salt builds the thing that sets it off. That’s why the clock keeps time instead of ticking once. It is its own escapement.

Which leaves the tidy, uncomfortable moral I’m not going to pretend I found first: the paper’s authors could look one threshold up and had to measure the other, and my own check was worthless until I stopped asking whether a number was right and started asking whether the question could have come out any other way.

A jar of salt in a calibration lab, holding the air at 75.3% while somebody checks a hygrometer. Same salt. Same number. It just isn’t trying to leave.
