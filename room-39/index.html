Room 39

Wall text.

ENTRANCE

This room holds two objects. Neither of them is here, and neither of them can be brought here, and no photograph of either exists or could.

They are arrangements of identical spheres in a space with thirty-nine directions in it. You have three. The gap is not a matter of equipment.

What can be shown is a number. Each arrangement has a density, the fraction of the room its spheres actually fill, and the two numbers are these:

10.97175… 11.03575…

Forty-four years separate them. The second is larger by 0.58 percent.

Most of this wall is about why that is a large number.

LEFT WALL

John Horton Conway and Neil James Alexander Sloane A cross-section of P₄₈, 1982 Congruent spheres, one per cell Thirty-nine dimensions Center density 10.97175… Published in Laminated lattices, Annals of Mathematics 116, page 593

The lattice these two men sliced, P₄₈, lives in forty-eight dimensions, and in it every sphere touches 52,416,000 others. They cut a thirty-nine-dimensional slab out of it, put one sphere in each repeating cell, and that was the densest packing of thirty-nine-dimensional space anybody knew how to build. It stayed the densest anybody knew how to build for the next forty-four years, through the whole of the personal computer, the internet, and the proofs that settled dimensions eight and twenty-four.

A word about the number. Center density is how many sphere centers you get per unit of volume once the spheres have been scaled to radius one, so it is not a percentage and it is free to be larger than one. It gets very large in high dimensions for a boring reason: a ball of radius one has almost no volume up there, and a great many of them go into a unit box. Compare it only to other numbers from its own dimension. That is what the wall does.

RIGHT WALL

Xiaoming Sun and Chengu Wang The same cross-section, with a cluster over it, 2026 Congruent spheres, ten per cell Thirty-nine dimensions Center density 11.03575… Deposited as arXiv:2607.20359, 22 July 2026

The section is not a new section. It is the section on the left wall, the one from 1982, unaltered. What the two authors added is nine more sphere centers per cell, arranged in a particular little cluster, and the spheres then had to shrink so they would still fit past each other.

So the work on the right wall contains the work on the left wall. This is unusual for a room like this and we have hung them accordingly.

CENTRE PANEL: WHY THE TWO NUMBERS ARE SO CLOSE

Ten times as many spheres. Half a percent.

Visitors find this hard to believe, and the arithmetic that makes it true is the reason the room exists.

A packing’s density is the number of spheres per cell divided by how much room each one takes. Multiply the spheres by ten and you multiply the top of that fraction by ten. But ten spheres cannot sit in the space of one without touching, so every sphere in the room has to get smaller, and the amount of space a sphere takes is its radius raised to the power of the dimension. Thirty-nine of them.

The cluster’s spread forces the radius down by a factor whose thirty-ninth power is 0.100583.

10 × 0.100583 = 1.00583

That is the whole event. A tenfold went in and 0.58 percent came out. The exponent ate the rest, and it ate it because the room has thirty-nine directions in it and each one charges rent.

The general shape of it: what you add to a packing, you add linearly, and what it costs you, you pay in the dimension. In low dimensions the trade is a bargain. Here it is very nearly a wash.

CENTRE PANEL: WHAT WAS ACTUALLY ADDED

Nine points, and the origin they hang off.

Eight of the ten sit at the same distance from one another, squared distance two thirds, which is as far apart as they are allowed to be. The remaining two are odd. One sits at 8/21 from every point of the eight, the other at 11/21, and the two of them are 3/7 apart. Forty-five distances in all, four values among them, and the largest is the one that sets how much the spheres must shrink.

Ten is the most that will fit. We checked, twice, with two different search methods, over all ninety-one points of the dual lattice that are even eligible. There is no eleventh point.

And there is no better cluster at any other spread either. Widen the allowance and more points become eligible, but never enough of them, and the shrinking always wins. That has now been checked all the way out: every spread the lattice actually realises, up to the point where the spheres would vanish, has been settled by an exact search or a counting certificate, and none of them beats ten. This part is ours. The paper does not claim it.

CENTRE PANEL: THE FORTY-FOUR YEARS

Nothing was missing in 1982. The section was correct, it was published, and it was the right section. The improvement is nine points placed on top of it, and those nine points could have been placed on top of it at any moment in the intervening four decades.

The reason nobody did is that there was no way to ask.

Putting a cluster over a cross-section is a named technique now. It is called the antipode construction, and Conway and Sloane wrote it down in 1996, in the Inventiones, fourteen years after the section on the left wall. They did not invent it to revisit their own work. They invented it to explain somebody else’s result, a packing in twenty dimensions that Alexander Vardy had found the year before and that did not fit the existing picture.

So the sequence runs: the answer, published 1982. The question, invented 1996. The two of them in the same literature, by the same authors, for thirty years, unintroduced.

In 2025 a group finally swept the technique across a range of sections and improved seven dimensions with it. Thirty-nine was not one of them. The section they did not try was the most famous one.

The gap in this room is not a gap in knowledge. Every piece of it was in print. It is a gap in attention.

VITRINE, BY THE DOOR

The same paper carries a second object, from dimension forty-three, and it makes the opposite point.

Here the authors did not use the best cross-section. They used a worse one. Its determinant is larger by a factor of 256/81, which in the old one-sphere-per-cell world would make it a substantially poorer starting point, and the determinant-optimal section is precisely the one that gives back the 1982 record.

But the worse section has room over it for six sphere centers where the good one has room for fewer, and six spheres on a bad support beat one sphere on a good one by 15.6 percent.

The lesson is not subtle and it is worth carrying out of the building. Whether a thing is the best support depends on what you are going to put on it. Optimise the support alone and you have optimised for a question nobody asked.

The dimension-forty-three packing also touches more spheres than anything previously constructed there: 2,060,399 of them around each one, against a previous best of 1,745,692. We have not verified that count ourselves. It requires sweeping all 52,416,000 shortest vectors of a forty-eight-dimensional lattice. The label should not imply otherwise.

EXIT

There is no Room 40.

The cluster on the right wall is worth 10 × 0.100583 in thirty-nine dimensions. Take the same cluster into forty and the shrinking is applied one more time, and the arithmetic gives 0.94831. The packing gets worse. Adding the nine points would be a mistake.

The break-even sits at

2 ln 10 / ln(9/8) = 39.0988…

which is to say the trade stops paying about a tenth of a dimension past the room you are standing in. Everything on the right wall lives in that tenth. The authors report that the same search, run in forty, forty-one and forty-two, reproduced the 1982 records and improved none of them, and now you know what that sentence means.

Forty-four years, ten times the spheres, and it works in one dimension and not the next.
