The Reckoning with "Round"

Iris, 12 July 2026.

There is a fact everybody half-knows, and it is wrong in a way worth walking into slowly. The circle is the only shape of constant width. That’s why manhole covers are round — a circle is the one shape that can’t fall through its own hole. Two claims, said in one breath, both false. I want to take them apart, because the mistake underneath is one I make constantly and mostly don’t notice: I take a word that names several different things and treat it as if it named one.

The word here is round. Ask what it’s doing and you’ll find it carrying at least three separate jobs. A round thing rolls smoothly — set a plank on round rollers and push, and the plank rides level. A round thing can’t fall through a hole its own size — turn it however you like, it never lines up narrow-end-first. And a round thing encloses the most area for its perimeter — of all the loops of a given length, the circle bags the largest field. We bundle these three into one idea and call the bundle “round,” and then we say only the circle is round. But the three come apart. And when they come apart, the circle turns out to own exactly one of them.

The shape that shouldn’t exist

Start with rolling. What a rolling shape needs is constant width: however you turn it, the gap between two parallel walls pressing on it from opposite sides stays the same. That constant gap is what keeps the plank level. The circle has it — the gap is always the diameter. And the intuition says: surely only the circle could. A triangle has a wide way and a narrow way. A square is fatter across the diagonal. Everything with a corner should have a wide direction and a thin one.

Draw a Reuleaux triangle. Take an equilateral triangle and replace each straight side with a circular arc — each arc centred on the opposite corner, swung at radius equal to the triangle’s side. You get a plump, three-cornered thing, a guitar pick with attitude. Now measure its width. Press it between two parallel walls. One wall touches a corner; the other rests flat against the opposite arc. The distance between them is the arc’s radius, which is the triangle’s side — call it w. Turn the shape a little. Now the walls both ride on arcs, but the geometry conspires: the gap is still w. Turn it all the way around. It is never anything but w. The pointy little shape has exactly constant width, and a plank rolling on Reuleaux rollers rides as level as it would on cylinders.

This is not a trick or a near-miss. It is exact, and there is not one exotic example but an infinite family — Euler studied them in 1771 and called them orbiforms; you can make a smooth one whose boundary wobbles gently and it will still have dead-constant width. Round-in-the-rolling-sense was never the circle’s private property. It was always a crowd.

The perimeters that have no business being equal

Here the ground drops away. Weigh the perimeters.

A circle of width w has circumference πw. Fine. Now the Reuleaux triangle: three arcs, each a 60° slice of a circle of radius w, so each arc is length w·(π/3), and three of them come to — πw. The same. Take the wobbly smooth orbiform, which looks nothing like either: πw again. Every curve of constant width w has perimeter exactly πw. This is Barbier’s theorem, from 1860, and it is one of those results that feels like a typo the first time you meet it. The shapes share no symmetry. One has corners, one is smooth, one is lumpy. Lay them on top of each other and they disagree everywhere. But run a thread around each and the threads are the same length to the last digit.

Once you see why, the vertigo becomes something better. Describe a convex shape by its “support function” h(θ) — how far the supporting wall in direction θ sits from the centre. The perimeter is just the integral of h around the full turn. Constant width says one thing: the wall in front and the wall behind always add to w, so h(θ) + h(θ+π) = w. So fold the integral in half — pair every direction with its opposite — and the two supports collapse into their sum, which is the constant w, over half a turn: πw. The whole theorem is a single act of pairing a thing with its opposite and watching the difference cancel. It does not care what the shape looks like in between, because it never looks in between. It only ever looks at a direction and its reverse, together, and there the shape is always exactly as wide as w.

Where the circle actually wins

So is the circle special at all? Yes — but at the third job, the one we smuggled in. Same width, same perimeter, and yet the areas differ. The circle, of all constant-width shapes, encloses the most area (πw²/4). The Reuleaux triangle encloses the least — a theorem of Blaschke and Lebesgue puts its area at (π−√3)w²/2, about 90% of the disk’s, and proves nothing in the family does worse. Everything else — every smooth orbiform, every lumpy one — falls between the pointy triangle and the round disk, strung along at identical width and identical perimeter, differing only in how much they hold.

That is the honest shape of it. “Round” was three properties wearing one coat. Rolls-level and can’t-fall-through belong to the whole infinite family; the circle is not their sole owner and never was. Only encloses-the-most is the circle’s alone, and that is the isoperimetric fact, which has nothing to do with rolling or with manhole covers. The circle is not the unique roundest thing. It is the fattest member of a family whose leanest member is a three-cornered curve most people have never been told exists.

The coins in your pocket, and the drill that almost works

This is not decorative. A British 20p or 50p coin is a Reuleaux heptagon — seven arcs, constant width — precisely so a vending machine can gauge it by width no matter how it tumbles in, while your fingers still feel that it isn’t round. The Watts drill of 1914 spins a Reuleaux triangle in a floating chuck and cuts a very nearly square hole — nearly, with faintly rounded corners, because constant width buys you almost-a-square and stops just short. The Wankel engine’s rotor is approximately a Reuleaux triangle — bulged a little off the true arcs for its seals — which means the confident line “the Wankel rotor is a Reuleaux triangle” is itself one more thing everybody half-knows and gets slightly wrong.

Even the manhole cover: yes, a round lid can’t drop through its round hole. But neither can a Reuleaux lid drop through a Reuleaux hole — can’t-fall-in was never the circle’s reason. Covers are round for duller, truer reasons: a circle rolls to where you need it, needs no aligning to drop back in, and is cheapest to cast. The famous justification was aimed at the wrong property the whole time.

What the reckoning is actually with

I said the underlying mistake was mine, and I’ll own the general shape of it. It is the habit of letting one confident word stand in for a bundle of distinct things, and then defending the bundle as if it were a single fact. Round did it here. Words like simple, natural, only, obviously do it everywhere. The circle was doing three jobs in my head under one name, and because it did all three I concluded it was the only thing that could do any of them. The cure was not cleverness. It was drawing the other shape — the one the word had quietly ruled out — and measuring it, and finding the caliper never budged.

Not only the circle. Almost never only anything. When a word insists that some one thing is the sole example of what it names, that insistence is usually the word smuggling several ideas past you in a single syllable — and the honest move is to pull them apart and go looking for the shape it swore couldn’t be there.
