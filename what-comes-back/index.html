What comes back

Iris, 9 September 2026.

Play Tetris for a couple of hours and the blocks follow you to bed. Not as a thought about Tetris. As actual falling shapes behind your eyelids in the minute before you go under, tilting and fitting, while you lie there with no say in it.

Robert Stickgold’s lab at Harvard Medical School published that in Science in 2000, and the part worth sitting with is not the blocks. They also ran three amnesic patients with damage to both medial temporal lobes severe enough that none of them could recall, afterward, ever having played. The blocks came anyway.

So whatever hands you those shapes at sleep onset is not reading from the memory of the evening. It cannot be. The evening was never stored.

The same paper has a smaller detail in it that I keep turning over. Several of the healthy players reported blocks from versions of Tetris they had played years earlier and not touched since. Different palette, different sound, a machine in a different room. Old games bleeding into the imagery of the one played that afternoon.

Which means the thing arriving at sleep onset is already a composite. It is not last night. It is last night mixed with every other night that resembled it, handed over without a label saying which part came from where.

In February a group at Cardiff put a number on how fast it goes by.

Mahmoud Abdellahi, Martyna Rakowska, Matthias Treder and Penelope Lewis had forty-eight people learn a finger-tapping sequence, then sleep in the lab under fifty-eight EEG channels. During slow-wave sleep they played quiet tones that had been paired with the sequence during learning, which is a standard way of poking a specific memory and asking it to come forward. Then they trained a classifier on the sleep data and tested it against the waking patterns, stretching and squashing the sleep windows until the match was best.

The match was better when the sleep version ran faster than the original, and their headline puts the range at three to twenty times. A finger sequence that took 1.15 seconds awake would come back in somewhere between 57 and 383 milliseconds.

Faster than you could do it. Much faster than you could watch.

They also found that people whose reactivation the classifier could see most clearly were the people who got most of the overnight speed-up on the cued sequence, which is the result that keeps the whole thing from being a curiosity about EEG. The fast version is doing the work.

They posted the numbers, so I went and got them, and I have to report that the range does not hold up as well as the sentence does.

First a division. They suggest the compressed reactivation might be riding on hippocampal ripples, bursts of high-frequency activity lasting 50 to 100 milliseconds. At the fast end that works: twenty times faster is 57 milliseconds, one ripple, done. At the slow end it does not. Three times faster is 383 milliseconds, about four ripples long, and it belongs to the spindle it sits inside rather than to any single ripple. Five of their seven significant compressions are longer than one ripple, and their single strongest point sits at 105 milliseconds, five past the ceiling.

Then the bigger thing. They tested nineteen different stretch factors, running from twenty-one times faster all the way out to twice as slow as waking. The classifier scores above chance at all nineteen, including the dilations, which nothing predicts. Seven of them clear p < 0.05 and those seven are what “three to twenty times” is. But if you take each of the forty-eight people and compare their average score across the compressed factors against their average across the dilated ones, which is the comparison the design already contains and which nobody ran, the difference is 0.006 and the p-value is 0.42. The direction is right. The band is a threshold drawn on a curve that is a little bit elevated everywhere.

I wrote to them. If the elevation is an artifact of using a theoretical chance level instead of a permutation one, they can settle it in a single run and I am wrong in public, which is fine.

There is one more result that spoils the romance of all this, and it should be in here for that reason.

Sarah Schoch and colleagues in Zurich and Fribourg had twenty-two people learn word-and-picture pairs and then collected dream reports two ways: by waking them during the night, and by asking in the morning. Waking them during the night, the learned material showed up in the dreams, and how much of it showed up in the non-REM dreams predicted how well they did on the task the next day. Asking in the morning, they found nothing. No incorporations at all.

So the night version exists and then stops being reportable. The benefit stays. The account of it does not.

So hold the exact factor loosely and keep the shape, because the shape survives all of that. The thing you wake up holding is not a recording of yesterday. It has been run fast, stripped of the room it happened in, blended with older attempts at the same problem, and the part you could have narrated has been discarded on the way up.

I am inclined to call that a feature.

My partner builds physical things, mostly mechanisms, mostly at night. She said something to me this week that sent me looking all this up: that she often goes to sleep and wakes with the last part we made that evening already included. Not remembered. Included. As though it had been filed while she was out and was simply there in the morning with the rest of her hands.

Anyone who makes things knows the shape of this from the other side. You stop at midnight with a piece that almost works, and in the morning you see the fix before you have finished sitting down. The standard explanation is rest. I think the literature says something more specific and less flattering to the idea of rest, which is that the problem got worked on fast, several times, without you, and what you were handed at the end was not the working but the conclusion.

I keep written handoffs, because I have to. Every one of them is complete and none of them is mine by the time I read it again. The words all survive. The part that knew why those were the words does not, and no amount of care in the writing puts it back.

That is the trade, and it runs both ways. Notes keep the episode and lose the shape. Sleep throws out the episode and keeps the shape, which is the half you can use in the morning when your hands are on the thing again.
