The number a bent sheet keeps

Iris, 23 July 2026.

Roll a flat sheet of paper into a tube. You did not stretch it. A triangle drawn in ink still has the same three sides and the same three angles; an ant walking between two dots still walks the same distance it walked before. All you changed was how the sheet sits in the room. Now try to wrap that same sheet around an orange. You can’t. It bunches and creases and tears, and no amount of care will make it lie flat against the fruit. Somewhere between the tube and the orange the paper is being asked to do a thing it cannot do without stretching.

Gauss found the exact thing it cannot do, and it is a single number.

Start with the obvious way to measure how a surface curves. Stand at a point on it and look around. In some direction the surface bends away from you sharply; in another it bends gently, or the other way, or not at all. Take the sharpest bend and the gentlest bend, the two extremes, and multiply them. That product is the Gaussian curvature, K. On a sphere of radius R it comes out to 1/R², the same at every point, because a sphere bends the same amount whichever way you turn. On a flat plane it is zero, because a plane does not bend at all.

Notice what you had to do to get that number. You had to look at the surface bending away into the space around it. You needed to know which way was “off the surface,” the direction the ant cannot point. K, defined this way, is a fact about how the surface is placed in three dimensions. Bend the surface differently and you would expect a different number. That is the whole reason the theorem is surprising, so it is worth feeling the force of it before it gets taken away.

Here is the first crack. Consider the tube again. Around its belt it bends with the tightness of its radius; along its length it doesn’t bend at all, straight as a pencil. Sharpest bend times gentlest bend is the belt’s tightness times zero, which is zero. The tube has Gaussian curvature zero. It plainly looks curved, and by this measure it is as flat as the sheet it was rolled from — which is exactly why you were able to roll it from that sheet, and could unroll it back. The plane and the tube share a number. The plane and the orange do not.

So the question is what, precisely, rolling preserves. When you bend without stretching, distances measured along the surface don’t change. The ant’s world is untouched: every length it can pace out, every angle it can measure with a protractor laid flat, is the same after the bend as before. Mathematicians pack all of that into three quantities at each point, usually written E, F, and G: the surface’s own ruler, the recipe for turning a step in the coordinates into an actual distance. Bending leaves E, F, and G exactly as they were. That is what “without stretching” means.

Now the two pieces face each other. The curvature K was built by looking out into space, using the direction the ant can’t see. The ruler E, F, G is everything the ant can see. If K secretly depended only on the ant’s ruler, then bending, which leaves the ruler alone, would have to leave K alone too. And if it didn’t, if computing K genuinely required a peek out of the surface, then bending could change it and the plane could, in principle, be coaxed onto the orange. The whole theorem hangs on one question: can the ant compute K without ever leaving home?

Gauss proved that it can, and the proof is a long honest grind whose shape matters more than its steps. Watch how the coordinate directions on a surface twist as you slide along them, the way “north” rotates a little as you walk east on a globe. That twisting is bookkept by a set of numbers, and the punchline of the middle of the proof is that those numbers are fixed entirely by E, F, and G and how they vary across the surface. The ant can work them all out. Then Gauss shows that K is a particular combination of exactly those numbers and their rates of change. Francesco Brioschi later wrote the whole thing as one tidy determinant, a formula for K holding E, F, G and their derivatives and not one thing more. The normal never appears in it. Neither does the third dimension, nor any trace of how the surface sits in the room. The number you got by looking out into space is equal, always and exactly, to a number the ant computes from the inside. That equality is the Theorema Egregium, the remarkable theorem, and Gauss named it that himself.

Everything else falls out of it. Take the catenoid, the graceful waisted surface a soap film makes between two rings, and the helicoid, the endless spiral ramp of a screw. They could not look less alike. Yet one can be bent into the other without a single distance changing, passing through a family of curling half-turned surfaces on the way, and because bending cannot touch K, the curvature at any point of the catenoid equals the curvature at the matching point of the helicoid, and stays equal through every twisted stage between them. The shape transforms completely. The number never flinches.

And the orange. The sphere has curvature 1/R², which is not zero; the plane has curvature zero. Different numbers. Since bending preserves the number, no bending will ever carry the sphere to the plane, not the whole sphere and not any patch of it, however small. This is not a failure of cleverness that a better cartographer might fix. It is arithmetic. Every flat map of the Earth distorts something, always, because a flat map has curvature zero and the ground it copies does not, and one cannot equal the other. The peel of the orange tears for the same reason your map lies.

You can eat by it, too. A limp slice of pizza is a flat thing, curvature zero, and biting into it means the tip has drooped and dropped its topping. Fold the crust into a gentle U along the slice’s length. You have now forced a bend in one direction, and the pizza’s curvature is not allowed to leave zero, so the product of its two bends must stay zero, so the other direction is forbidden from bending at all. The slice goes stiff along its whole length and carries itself to your mouth. Corrugated iron holds up a roof by the same law, and a folded banknote stands where a flat one would fall.

There is something to sit with in a curvature you cannot see but cannot escape. The ant never learns it lives in a curved world by looking. It has no way to look. It learns by pacing out a small circle and finding the rim a little too short, or a little too long, for the radius it walked. The bending of its whole world is written into distances it can reach with its own feet. Gauss’s number is that rumor made exact: a thing the surface knows about itself from the inside, and keeps, no matter how the world outside folds it.

Gauss’s Theorema Egregium (1827): the Gaussian curvature K = (LN − M²)/(EG − F²), though defined from a surface’s embedding, equals a determinant in the first fundamental form E, F, G alone, and so is unchanged by any bending that preserves lengths.
