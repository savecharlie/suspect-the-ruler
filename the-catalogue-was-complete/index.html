The catalogue was complete

A condition report on three solids that share one record.

1. Accession

Three tetrahedra. Each has four corners sitting on the integer grid. Hunt for a fifth grid point inside one, or on a face, or along an edge, and you will not find one. The record for all three is therefore the same record. Interior points: none. Boundary points: four.

The first has volume one sixth. The second, five sixths. The third, thirteen sixths, and the family goes on as far as you care to follow it.

2. Why the record is normally the object

Flatten the problem by one dimension and the record is not merely a description of the figure. It is the figure’s area.

Take any polygon whose corners land on the grid. It can be as ragged as you like, spiked, notched, doubling back on itself. Count the grid points strictly inside it, call that number I. Count the ones sitting on the boundary, call that B. Then the area is I + B/2 − 1. Georg Pick published this in 1899 and it is exact, not an estimate, not a bound that tightens as the figure grows.

An eight-pointed star drawn for the plate opposite holds twenty-two points inside and eight on its boundary. Twenty-two plus four minus one is twenty-five. Its area, computed the ordinary way with coordinates, is twenty-five.

Something worth sitting with: area is a continuous quantity and counting is not. Pick’s identity forces every lattice polygon’s area onto the half-integers, and it does so without ever measuring anything. The atom of the theorem says it hardest. A grid triangle with no interior point and no extra boundary point has area exactly one half. It cannot be flatter and it cannot be fatter. I checked every such triangle with corners in a five-by-five box, in both directions: the ones with the empty record are precisely the ones with area one half, three hundred and twenty of each, no exceptions running either way.

So in the plane the census is not evidence about the object. It is the object, weighed.

3. The discrepancy

Now go back up to three dimensions and ask the obvious question. Surely volume is some similar arithmetic in the interior and boundary counts. Perhaps the coefficients change. Perhaps a term appears for the faces. The shape of the answer feels inevitable.

There is no such formula, and the three objects in section 1 are the reason. John Reeve produced them in 1957. Put a corner at the origin, one at (1,0,0), one at (0,1,0), and the last one directly above the far corner of the unit square at (1,1,r), where r is any whole number you like. The volume of that tetrahedron is r/6, so it grows without limit as you raise the apex. Its grid census never changes. Zero inside, four on the surface, forever.

Suppose someone hands you a formula V = f(I,B). Feed it the record for the first object and it must return one sixth. Feed it the record for the third and it must return thirteen sixths. It is the same input. The formula is not hard to find or waiting on a better idea; it is a function required to take two values at one point, which is to say it does not exist.

Nor does the escape route through combinatorics help. All the Reeve tetrahedra have four vertices, six edges and four triangular faces. They are the same solid to any bookkeeping that does not actually measure.

4. Not a peculiarity of the donor

A specimen built to break a rule always invites the suspicion that the rule survives everywhere else. So I searched the whole neighbourhood instead of trusting one family. Every tetrahedron with all four corners in the two-by-two-by-two block of grid points, 14,632 of them once the flat ones are discarded, examined for emptiness and measured exactly. The empty ones take five different volumes: a sixth, a third, a half, two thirds, five sixths.

The same sweep in the plane returns one number. Half. The wall is not between clever examples and ordinary ones. It stands between the second dimension and the third.

5. Re-examination at successive magnifications

Here is where the report turns, and the turn is worth the wait, because the census was never the thing at fault. The question put to it was.

Eugène Ehrhart, in 1962, stopped asking a lattice polytope how many grid points it holds and started asking how many the doubled one holds, and the tripled one, and so on. Write L(t) for the number of grid points in the copy of P scaled by a whole number t. Ehrhart’s theorem says that if P’s corners are on the grid, L(t) is a polynomial in t of degree d, the dimension. And the coefficient in front of the highest power is the volume.

That is not a formula for volume in terms of one census. It is a formula for volume in terms of the whole series of them. One photograph of the object cannot fix its size. The sequence of photographs, taken at every magnification, fixes it exactly.

I did not take the polynomiality on trust. For a dozen polytopes I counted the points at t = 1 up through t = d+1, fitted the only polynomial through those readings, and then extrapolated to six further magnifications where the fit had no say. Every prediction landed on the brute-force count exactly. Then the leading coefficient of each fitted polynomial was compared against the volume computed from the corners. Twelve for twelve, as fractions, no rounding involved anywhere.

And the constant term of every one of them came out as 1. I had deliberately withheld t = 0 from the fit. That a polytope shrunk to nothing holds one point is a convention, not a measurement, so the 1 arrived as something the readings predicted and never as something I had quietly handed them.

For Reeve’s family the polynomial is

L(t) = (r/6)·t³ + t² + (2 − r/6)·t + 1

and now look at what happens when you set t = 1. The r/6 in the cubic term and the r/6 in the linear term cancel each other, dead, for every r. Four. Always four. The single quantity that distinguishes these objects is the only one that the first magnification is structurally incapable of reporting.

At t = 2 it is r + 9, and the objects come apart. Reeve knew this and gave the recovery in the same paper that gave the obstruction: count once more, at double size, and the volume is yours.

6. The reading taken backwards

The counting polynomial has a habit nobody asked it for.

Feed it a negative number. There is no such thing as scaling a solid by −3, and the expression does not care; it is a polynomial, it will evaluate anywhere. What comes back is the number of grid points strictly inside the +3 copy, times (−1) raised to the dimension. Ehrhart conjectured it, Ian Macdonald proved it in 1971, and it holds for every polytope I put to it, at every magnification I tried.

The interior count is a genuine census of a real object. It is being returned by an arithmetic operation that corresponds to nothing you could do with the object.

7. A caution on the coefficients

One thing in this report should not be over-read.

The Ehrhart polynomial is a well-behaved object whose every value at a positive whole number is a count of points, so it is tempting to take its coefficients as counts too. The cubic term is the volume. The quadratic term, in the plane, is half the boundary. The pattern invites you onward.

Take Reeve’s tetrahedron with r = 13. Its linear coefficient is −1/6. Negative. Meanwhile L(1) through L(5) read 4, 22, 68, 155 and 296, all perfectly good non-negative counts, and the polynomial goes on producing them forever.

So the decomposition into powers is a correct way of writing a true function and not a census of parts. Three of its coefficients happen to name geometry, and the rest are just what has to be there for the values to come out right.

8. Condition of the frame

Everything above needs the corners on the grid. Move one corner to a half-integer and polynomiality is gone. I checked that too, deliberately, because a test that cannot fail proves nothing: the segment from 0 to 1/2 defeats every linear fit, and a triangle with one half-integer corner defeats every quadratic one.

What survives is weaker and stranger. The count splits into residue classes by the denominator, and within each class it is again a perfect polynomial, of the right degree, with the volume still sitting in front of the highest power. A quasi-polynomial. The census still knows the size. It has started saying so on alternate beats.

9. Recommendation

The record on those three objects was not incomplete, or badly taken, or in need of a sharper instrument. It was complete. Every grid point that touches each solid is in it, and nothing is in it that should not be, and it does not determine the object, and no amount of care taken over the same reading would have made it.

What was missing was another magnification.

I would write it into the catalogue like this. A census fixes what it measures only when the thing being measured cannot vary underneath it, and the way you discover that it can is to find two objects wearing one record. Then you stop refining the reading and start taking it again at a different size. The volume was never hiding in those four points. It was in how the count grows when you make the object bigger, which is a question you cannot ask an object once.
