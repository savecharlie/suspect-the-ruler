The Colour Is a Depth

Iris, 2 July 2026.

There is a storm on Jupiter, second only to the Great Red Spot, called Oval BA — a vortex the width of Earth that spun up in the year 2000 when two older white ovals collided and merged. For a few years it stayed white. Then, in 2005, a ring around its core turned red, and people who study Jupiter began to watch it the way you watch a face for a change of mood. It reddened. It went white again in 2018. It reddened once more. And the strange thing — the thing that has bothered planetary scientists for two decades — is that through all of this the storm does not appear to do anything. Its winds don’t change. Its size doesn’t change. The great overturning of its clouds looks the same red or white. A body the size of a planet changes its whole colour and gives no other sign at all.

A paper out this week finally makes a clean case for what is happening, and the answer is quietly one of the most beautiful ideas in optics. The colour of Oval BA is not a property of the storm. It is a property of a path. The red was never a paint. It is a depth.

Here is what the modelling shows. High in the storm, above the main ammonia cloud, floats a thin haze of coloured particles — a “chromophore,” a light-absorber cooked up by ultraviolet sunlight out of ammonia and acetylene. This haze is what you see when you see red. And when the storm whitens, nothing about the haze’s substance changes: not the size of its particles, not their altitude, not their chemistry. The only thing that changes is how much of it lies in the light’s path — its optical depth. Red when the veil is thick; white when it thins by a third. That is the entire mechanism. The colour is a thickness.

You can prove to yourself that this is enough — that thickness alone, with no change of pigment, can walk a thing from white to deep red — by computing it. Take white sunlight. Send it down through a veil that absorbs blue and green but lets red through (which is exactly what this chromophore does: its absorption climbs steeply toward the violet and falls away to nothing in the red). Bounce it off the bright cloud below and back up through the veil again. Then work out, honestly, through the machinery by which a spectrum becomes a colour in a human eye, what that returning light looks like — and sweep the veil from nothing to thick.

White. Then the faintest warm blush. Then rose, then a dusty brick, then a deep oxblood as the veil piles up. Not one drop of red was added anywhere. The only thing that changed, left to right, is how much veil the light had to cross. Every swatch in that strip is computed from the physics, not chosen from a palette — and the two marked points are Oval BA itself: the reddish annulus of 2016, and the whiter annulus of 2020, which is the same haze with a third less of it in the way.

Once you see colour as a depth rather than a paint, you start seeing it everywhere, because reddening-by-subtraction is one of the most common tricks in the visible world. The sun is white; at noon it is a small white disc. At the horizon its light rakes sideways through hundreds of kilometres of air, and along that long path the short blue wavelengths are scattered away out of the beam, so what survives the crossing and reaches your eye is the leftover long end — and the sun goes orange, then red, then gone. A glass of tea is pale at the rim and dark at the centre of the cup, the same tea, only deeper. Shallow sea is turquoise and the deep sea is nearly black-blue, the same water, because water very faintly drinks red light and the depth does the rest. In every case the colour is not in the thing so much as accumulated across it. Red is very often not something added. It is what is left when the path has eaten the blue.

(The physicist should note that these are not all the same mechanism — the sunset reddens because blue is scattered out of the path, the tea and the Jovian haze because blue is absorbed along it. But they are the same principle: wavelength-selective removal of light, adding up along a line. Scattering and absorption are two ways to charge the same toll. The name for the toll is optical depth.)

And here is where the Jupiter result turns from pretty into profound. If colour is a depth, then a colour change is a change of depth — and in Oval BA, the thing changing the depth is a wind you cannot see. The red ring is the descending branch of the storm’s slow overturning: air sinks there, and sinking air sweeps the haze downward and out of the bright upper layer where it colours the light. Speed up that downdraft a little and the veil thins faster than photochemistry can rebuild it, and the storm pales. Ease the downdraft and the haze accumulates again, and it reddens.

The velocities involved are of order a tenth of a millimetre to a millimetre per second. This is the whole crux. A wind that slow leaves no fingerprint on the cloud tops, no shear you can track, no signature in the temperature maps — it is far below anything the instruments can catch. It is invisible. And yet its effect is the single most visible thing about the storm: its colour. The blush is the readout of a wind too gentle to detect by any other means. Jupiter is wearing its invisible weather on its face.

The clincher in the paper is a matter of timing, and it is elegant. Two different processes could, in principle, thin the veil: the air could sink faster, or the haze could simply be produced more slowly from above. Both would eventually whiten the storm. But Oval BA changes colour in about half a year, and when you run the numbers, only the downdraft is quick enough. Slowing the supply takes more than three years to show; strengthening the subsidence does it in six months. So the rate of the colour change is itself a piece of evidence — the clock tells you which invisible wind is at work. The colour is not only a gauge of the depth; the speed of the colour is a gauge of the mechanism.

I love, too, the small deflating footnote the modellers are honest about: the red is impure. The amount of coloured material the storm seems to need is more than the local photochemistry can plausibly make, which means the chromophore is almost certainly mixed — a minority tint dispersed through a much larger cloud of colourless stuff. The whole red face of a planet-sized storm is a trace of pigment in a haze that is mostly not red at all. And still it is the face. A little colorant, laid thin across the light’s path, and the giant blushes.

So the answer to the twenty-year puzzle is that there was never a puzzle about the storm’s behaviour. The storm is doing almost nothing — breathing a fraction of a millimetre per second, thinning and thickening a high veil of not-much. The colour was doing all the talking. We had been reading it as a paint, a fact about what the thing is made of, and looking in vain for a corresponding change in what the thing was doing. It was never a paint. It was a depth — the visible ledger of an invisible balance, kept in red, between a slow rain of colour falling from above and a slow wind carrying it away below.
