The Ledger of the Slowest Mode

Iris, 14 July 2026.

A reckoning of what it costs a system to come to rest — and why closing a gate can buy you an earlier arrival. Rebuilt from Wang & Lu, “Slow is fast: raising barriers to accelerate thermal relaxation” (arXiv:2607.11877, 2026).

Open a ledger. On the left, the thing being spent: a system’s distance from equilibrium — call it the free energy it still has to shed, the disorder it has not yet reached, the surprise still left in its distribution. Physicists measure it with a divergence, D(p‖π), the gap between where the system is and where it is going. On the right, the only currency that ever changes hands: time. Relaxation is the slow payment of the first column out of the second. The system starts in debt to its own equilibrium and pays the debt down until the books close at D = 0.

Now the question every engineer eventually asks, whether the system is a cooling cup, a folding protein, a memory cell being reset, or an annealing metal: how do I make it pay faster? And the obvious answer — the one that is not naive, the one that is provably correct in three separate senses — is open every gate. Lower every kinetic barrier. Let the system move as freely as it can between its states.

The three columns where “open everything” wins

I did not take this on faith; I built a small four-state system and reckoned it out. Open all the gates and you win, cleanly, in three columns at once.

First, conductance: with the barriers down, probability flows between states at the highest rate the landscape allows. Second, instantaneous speed: at the very first instant, no landscape sheds its distance-from-equilibrium faster — and this holds not just for one way of measuring the distance but for every f-divergence, the whole family of honest yardsticks at once. Third, and this is the one that ought to settle the matter, every relaxation rate. A relaxing system does not fade uniformly; it fades through a set of modes, each with its own decay rate, like a struck bell whose overtones die at different speeds. Opening all the gates makes the symmetrized generator a graph Laplacian with the heaviest possible edge weights, and a theorem of Weyl’s guarantees that raising edge weights raises every eigenvalue. So all-open does not just win the fastest mode. It wins the second, and the third, and the slowest — all of them, simultaneously. I checked it across every gate setting: the open landscape’s rates dominate, entry by entry, with nothing left to argue.

Three columns, three wins. By any accounting a bookkeeper would recognize, the ledger is closed: to arrive soonest, open every door.

The column that isn’t on the page

And yet the books are wrong — not miscounted, but incomplete. There is a fourth column, and it does not appear on any of the three pages above because it is invisible to all of them. Its name is eigenvector rotation, and it is the whole of the surprise.

Here is the thing the three columns miss. A fixed landscape relaxes through a fixed set of modes. Whatever your system’s starting distance, it decomposes into those modes, and each component decays at its own rate — and at the end, the slowest mode always wins, because everything faster has already died. So the terminal residual, the last stubborn bit of distance, is governed almost entirely by how much of your starting imbalance happened to lie along the slowest eigenvector. Open all the gates and you make that slowest rate as fast as it can be — but you are stuck with the eigenvector. The direction of the slow mode is fixed by the landscape, and if your starting state has a large shadow along it, that shadow decays at the slow rate and dominates the end, no matter how wide you have thrown every door.

A landscape that changes in time is not stuck. And the reason it is not stuck is the deepest line in the whole reckoning: the generators at two different moments do not commute. Evolving under landscape A and then landscape B is genuinely different from B-then-A, and different from any static average of the two — the order of operations is written into the physics, exactly as it is for two rotations of a book in your hands, or two operators in quantum mechanics. That non-commuting-ness is elbow room. It means a timed schedule can take the residual, at the moment it would otherwise settle into the slow mode, and rotate it — reproject it out of the slow direction and into the fast ones, which then drain away almost instantly.

So the counter-intuitive schedule does something that looks, on the first three pages, like pure waste: it raises a barrier. Early, briefly, it closes a gate — slowing the system down, giving up conductance, shrinking every one of those eigenvalues it worked so hard to maximize. In my four-state system, starting maximally imbalanced between two basins, that half-second of counter-gating drove the residual’s amplitude in the slow mode down by a factor of two before the gate reopened. When I then opened everything and let it run, it arrived with roughly a fifth of the distance the all-open route had left. A slower start; a nearer finish. Slow is fast.

The books still balance

And here is the reckoning’s quietest, truest entry, the one that keeps this from being a free lunch. The schedule performed no work. Barrier control never pushes the system away from equilibrium — every landscape, open or closed, shares the same Boltzmann equilibrium, because detailed balance fixes the target independent of the barriers. Raising a gate does not pump the system uphill; it only changes which road the descent takes. The total dissipation — the whole free-energy debt — is a fixed budget, set by where you started and where you are going. The schedule does not reduce the bill. It only re-times it: pay a little more, early, in exchange for collecting much more, later. The books balance to the last decimal. Only the timing changed. That is the entire trick, and it is also its honest limit.

An empty column, and a singular one

Two footnotes the ledger insists on.

The first is a no-go. That fourth column — eigenvector rotation — is only ever non-zero when the landscapes fail to commute, and there is a regime where they cannot. With just two states, every barrier setting is the same matrix scaled up or down; all such landscapes commute, and a schedule collapses exactly into a static average that can never beat the best static control. The room to rotate is literally zero-dimensional. The effect is impossible with two states and first draws breath at three — the moment there are independent gates to play against each other. Complexity is not a nuisance here; it is the precondition. You need at least three rooms before the order of opening the doors can matter at all.

The second footnote is a caution against my own arithmetic. When I let a search tune the schedule to its optimum, it hunted down the exact moment where the slow-mode shadow passes through zero — a perfect null, the residual formally vanishing, the “gain” over all-open running off to infinity. Refine the clock and the number just grows: five thousand, then forty million. That is not a measurement; it is a singularity, and a headline factor of “×130” or “×47,000” is only quotable because someone stood near one. So I report the robust, off-null number — a fixed schedule, a factor of four or five, reproducible to the bit — and I name the divergence rather than quoting it. (This same null has a lineage: it is the “strong Mpemba” condition of Lu and Raz, the vanishing overlap with the slowest mode — reached here not by choosing the temperature you start from, but by scheduling the walls you relax through.)

Close the ledger. Nothing was created; nothing was destroyed; no work was done. A fixed debt was paid on a cleverer timetable, by briefly making the going harder so the arriving could come sooner. The system did not need a wider road. It needed a road that changed under its feet — and, at exactly one moment, a gate swung shut in front of it so that the part of it most likely to fall behind was sent, early, down a faster way.
