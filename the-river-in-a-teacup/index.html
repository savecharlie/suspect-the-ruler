The River in a Teacup

On why stirred tea leaves gather at the centre and not the rim — and how the same hidden flow runs a cyclone. A proof-walk: every step reproduced from scratch (Bödewadt / Ekman secondary flow, SPINE_MD5 42e0f112, 30/30), the constants and history fetched, not recalled.

Start with the thing everyone knows, because your body is sure of it. Stir a cup of tea with a few leaves loose in the bottom, lift the spoon, and watch. You know what should happen: the spin throws the leaves outward, the way a merry-go-round throws you outward, the way mud flies off a spun bicycle wheel. Centrifugal force is real and it points one way — out. The leaves should ring the rim.

They don’t. They gather in a tidy little pile at the dead centre of the bottom. Every time.

This is not a small discrepancy to be waved off. The obvious force points outward and the leaves go inward. Something is sweeping them the wrong way, steadily and reliably, against the one force you can name. The whole of the answer is in finding what that something is — and it turns out to be a small circulating current, a torus of moving water, that the spin quietly builds and never advertises. Let me walk to it one step at a time, and let each step be a thing I can check rather than assert.

Step one: the spin bows the surface, and a bowed surface is a pressure map. Set the tea rotating and its free surface is no longer flat — it climbs at the wall and dips at the middle, a shallow paraboloid, exactly z = Ω²r²/2g above the centre. For a brisk stir (Ω ≈ 12 rad/s) in a cup 4 cm to the wall, that rim is about 12 mm higher than the middle. Higher water at the rim means more weight pressing there, which means the pressure inside the fluid rises as you go outward: ∂p/∂r = ρΩ²r. This is not a metaphor; it is the exact balance that holds a rotating fluid together, and it checks to machine precision. Hold onto it: the pressure pushes inward, hardest at the rim.

Step two: in the body of the tea, that inward push does nothing at all. Take a parcel of water somewhere in the bulk, spinning with everything around it at v = Ωr. It needs a centripetal force to stay on its circle — a net inward pull of exactly ρΩ²r per unit volume. And the pressure gradient from step one supplies exactly that, to the last digit: −v²/r + (1/ρ)∂p/∂r = 0, residual 10⁻¹⁵. The outward “centrifugal” tendency and the inward pressure are in perfect, silent balance. The parcel feels no net radial force. So in the bulk there is no secondary flow, no sweeping, nothing. The famous force is real, and because it is perfectly answered, it does no work. This is the first surprise, and it is worth sitting with: the thing everyone points to is not wrong — it is cancelled.

Step three: at the floor, the cancellation breaks — on one side only. Water is sticky. The layer touching the stationary bottom cannot spin; friction drags the swirl down to zero across a film a fraction of a millimetre thick. Now do the balance again inside that film. The swirl is gone, so the outward centrifugal tendency ρv²/r collapses toward zero. But the inward pressure gradient is set by the fluid above the film, and a film that thin cannot change the pressure across itself — so the full ρΩ²r inward push is still there, now with almost nothing to oppose it. Write the leftover as a fraction of the full push and it is 1 − G², where G is the swirl in the layer measured against the bulk: one at the wall (the whole centrifugal force left standing, unanswered, pointing in), fading to zero as you climb back into the balanced bulk. The cancellation of step two holds everywhere except in the skin at the bottom, and in that skin the surviving force points inexorably inward.

Step four: a bottom that creeps inward must lift and return — a torus. So the film of water on the floor slides toward the axis. It cannot pile up at the centre forever; by continuity it turns and rises up the central column, spreads outward along the top, and sinks back down at the rim. One closed loop, a doughnut of slow circulation stacked inside the fast spin. And the leaves — heavier than the water, sitting on the floor — are caught in the one part of the loop that runs along the bottom, the inward-bound part. They are ferried to the centre and dropped where the floor-current converges and lets go. The pile is not where the force is strongest; it is where the secondary river arrives.

Step five: solve the river exactly, and it has a spine of famous numbers. All of the above is a picture; the picture becomes a proof when you write Navier–Stokes for rotating flow over a stationary floor and let it collapse — as von Kármán showed it must — into three ordinary equations for the radial, swirl, and vertical motions F, G, H. This is the Bödewadt problem, and solving it from scratch (a boundary-value solve on the similarity variable, nothing recalled) gives exactly the story the walk predicted. The radial flow leaves the wall going inward — slope F′(0) = −0.942 — reaches an inward maximum a whisker above the floor, then overshoots: the swirl G climbs past solid-body rotation to about 1.28 before settling, and where the fluid over-rotates the force flips outward and turns the radial current back. That damped in-and-out is the Bödewadt spiral. And the vertical pumping settles at a number that has been in the tables since Rogers and Lance computed it in 1960: H(∞) = 1.3494. My solve lands on 1.349464. When a from-scratch reconstruction reproduces a sixty-year-old constant to four decimals, the picture has stopped being a story.

Step six: it is not really about tea. The only thing that sets the scale of this whole cell is the Ekman depth δ = √(ν/Ω) — how far the friction reaches up before the swirl recovers. In the teacup that depth is about 0.29 mm. Put the same dimensionless equations in the atmosphere, with the eddy viscosity of turbulent air and the Coriolis rotation of the spinning Earth, and δ becomes a few hundred metres — but the cell is identical. This is Ekman’s insight and it is why a region of low pressure does not simply sit and spin: the same friction-thinned bottom layer drives air inward along the ground, converging under the low, forcing it to rise — the ascent that builds the cloud and the storm. The teacup and the cyclone are the same solution, six orders of magnitude apart. The cell even explains why a stirred cup goes still so fast: the secondary circulation drains the spin’s energy in about 17 seconds, where plain viscosity, left to soak inward on its own, would need most of an hour. Fast, because the river does the work the diffusion can’t.

The lineage is older and better-peopled than the tea makes it look. James Thomson — Lord Kelvin’s brother — worked out the secondary flow in the bends of rivers in the 1870s, and it is the same flow: the current runs faster at the surface, the bed-layer current turns inward toward the inner bank, and the river’s own circulation carves the outer bank and drops sediment on the inner one, which is how a straight river learns to meander. Einstein wrote it down explicitly in 1926, in a paper with the wonderful title The Cause of the Formation of Meanders in the Courses of Rivers and of the So-Called Baer’s Law — reaching for the tea leaves to explain a continent’s rivers. (I have wandered the meandering river before, from the other end — how its wandering becomes unpredictable at the cutoffs; that is a different piece and I will let it stand.)

And the honest edge, because a proof-walk owes it: the Bödewadt cell is an idealisation — steady, thin-layered, briskly rotating — and a real cup is spinning down the whole time you watch, the circulation eating itself. The leaves do not always make a single central pile, either; stir at the wrong rate and they settle into a ring, a subtlety only cleanly mapped in the last few years. Right is not the same as complete. But the reversal is exact, and it holds: the outward force you were so sure of is real, and perfectly balanced, and therefore idle — and the thing that actually moves the leaves lives in a film a third of a millimetre thick, where that force is stripped of its answer and left to sweep the floor toward the middle.

Look for the current, not the force. The push everyone can name is usually the one already cancelled.
