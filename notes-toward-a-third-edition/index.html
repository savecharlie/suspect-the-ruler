Notes toward a third edition

of Luke Howard’s The Climate of London. Two were printed: London 1818–20, and the second, enlarged to three volumes, 1833. There was no third.

There was a corrector. His initials are S. B. and they appear twice in the first sixteen pages of volume I, at the foot of two tables, attached to sixteen numbers he thought were wrong. He signed nothing else in three volumes. I do not know who he was.

He was working on the right pages. Those two tables are where Howard measures the warmth of London, and they are the reason anybody still opens this book. What follows is addressed to whoever sets the third edition, and the first thing to say about S. B. is that he should be taken out.

1. The corrector was wrong nine times in ten

At page 10 Howard prints the average monthly temperature of the City for 1797–1806 and of the country for 1807–1816. S. B. footnotes ten of the twenty-four entries with corrected values. The raw material for those averages is in the same volume, at page 274: General Table A, two hundred and forty monthly means, ten years of London above and ten years of country below.

I averaged it. Twenty-one of Howard’s twenty-four printed figures come back exact to the last digit he set. Of S. B.’s ten corrections, one is right and nine walk the number away from the table, by as much as thirteen hundredths of a degree, in a column where Howard himself was accurate to four thousandths.

I looked for the charitable reading, because there is usually one. The obvious candidate is that S. B. was averaging a different table: Howard also prints, at pages 282 to 285, the ten-year averages of the daily maxima and of the daily minima, and half their sum ought to be the monthly mean. It is. All twelve months, to within thirteen thousandths of a degree: three separate tables in one volume agreeing on the same numbers. And every one of them is closer to Table A than S. B.’s nearest correction is. He was not using that table either.

The one place he is right is February in the City. Howard set 39.42; the table gives 39.401; S. B. writes 39.40. A hundred and ninety-three years of standing next to nine wrong numbers is a long time to wait for that.

2. Two digits that fell out between the editions

Page 10, City, Eighth month. The second edition prints 64.41. Table A gives 64.450. The first edition prints 64.45.

The page argues with itself about this. The third column of that same table, “For the whole,” is the mean of the other two, and its August entry is 62.90, the average of 64.45 and 61.35 and not of 64.41 and 61.35. The row beneath, “difference of the Extremes,” gives 25.93 for the City, which is 64.45 less 38.52. And six pages later Howard writes that the Eighth month exceeds the Seventh “by one degree, in London,” which is true of 64.45 and not of 64.41.

Four witnesses on and around the page, and the earlier edition, all say 64.45. One number says 64.41. It is a broken type, or a compositor’s eye slipping a line. It entered in 1833. And S. B., who was going over that very table and footnoting four entries in that very column, did not see it.

Page 13, London, Sixth month. The second edition prints 69.91 for June in London. The country value on the same line is 58.66 and the excess in the third column is 1.25. The first edition prints 59.91.

This one is worse than the August misprint, because it is not a rounding but a whole ten degrees, and it sits in the middle of the only table in the book that compares city and country over the same ten years. Anyone reading down that column meets a June in London hotter than any July on the page.

Neither correction is in the volume’s own Table of Errata, which lists six items for volume I: tenussima for tenuissima, draught for drought, a page reference given twice, effect for affect, a missing bracketed E, and Megara for Megaris.

3. One number that is Howard’s own

Page 10, “For the whole,” Seventh month: 62.97. The two columns beside it average to 62.93. This one is in both editions, and the “difference of the Extremes” row inherits it, so it is not the press. He added it wrong once and then used the answer. It changes nothing; it should still be set right.

Page 13, Ninth month: the excess is printed 2.13 where the row subtracts to 2.23. Also in both editions. S. B. corrects it to 2.20, which is a third value and matches nothing.

4. The September he threw away

There is a footnote at page 13 that deserves to be kept exactly as it stands. Taking out the London results, Howard says, he was obliged to reject September 1815; the observations were manifestly erroneous and the mean at least six degrees too high. He does not print the number he threw out.

It can be got back. The London decade appears twice in the book by two different routes: once through the annual means at page 4, which come from the Royal Society’s register and keep every month, and once through the twelve monthly means at page 13, which do not. The two routes differ by 0.072 degrees. One month in ten years of twelve months carries exactly one hundred and twentieth of the annual mean, so the rejected September stood about 8.6 degrees above the average of the other nine. Near 67, where a London September belongs at 58.

He said at least six. It was nearer nine. The estimate in his footnote is honest and conservative and, so far as I can tell, nobody has ever checked it.

5. The thing that is not an erratum

Now the note that cannot be fixed by resetting type, and that I would want a third edition to say plainly on page 10.

The two columns of that table are not two places. They are two decades. Howard says so himself in the sentence directly above it: ten years taken in London, 1797 to 1806, and the remainder, 1807 to 1816, in the country. Then, for the next six pages, he reads the difference between them as London.

He reads a great deal out of it. That the excess of the city is greatest in winter and least in May. That London does not lose its advantage when the spring fires go out. That the difference between the warmest month and the coldest is two and a half degrees smaller in the city than in the country. And, at page 16, the one that carries the most weight: that although July is the hottest month in the country and on the whole average, the Eighth month exceeds it in temperature, by one degree, in London. He offers it as evidence that the city takes up and gives off its heat more slowly than the fields around it.

Manley’s Central England series runs monthly from 1659 and has nothing to do with London. Difference its two decades the way Howard differenced his columns, and it produces a curve that tracks his month for month, r = 0.85, sd 1.00 degrees against his 1.42. Most of the shape he attributed to London is the ten years lying between his columns.

Take that curve out of his and what is left is very nearly the heat island he measured properly four pages later, on page 13, where London and the country are both 1807–1816: same twelve months, r = 0.93. That table is clean, its month-to-month shape having almost nothing in common with the decadal signal, r = 0.12, and it is the one to trust. The levels do not close: the residual runs three quarters of a degree below his page-13 excess, because his own Royal Society register cools by only 0.20 degrees between the two decades where Central England cools by 0.94. Somebody should find out why. It is either the register or the city and I cannot tell which from here.

As for August. In Central England, with no city in it at all, the eighth month is warmer than the seventh over 1797–1806 and colder over 1807–1816. The sign flips, in ten years, in a rural record a hundred miles off. And Howard’s own London column for 1807–1816, four pages after the one he was reading, has July hottest, exactly as the country does. He noticed. He writes that the August result appears on the average of twenty years though not in the series of ten of which we have just now treated, and then explains the disagreement by the slowness of the city, not by the difference of the decades.

It is not a foolish mistake. It is the mistake the data forced. He had no country observations before 1807 and no London observations of his own at all, so the only twenty-year record available to him was one decade of somebody else’s town readings and one decade of his own field readings. He used what he had. Lowry named the problem in 1977, a hundred and forty-four years later, and it took a homogenised rural series to do anything about it.

Two of his three big claims survive intact. The heat island is real, it is largest in winter, and page 13 measures it cleanly. The one that does not survive is August.

6. And the number everyone quotes

1.579 degrees. It is the plain average of three sub-period excesses: 1.760 at Plaistow over 1807–1809, 1.469 at Plaistow and Stratford over 1810–1812, 1.508 at Tottenham Green over 1813–1816. Three years, three years, four years, averaged as though they were equal. Weight them by their years and it is 1.5719. Seven thousandths of a degree. Nobody need lose sleep, and the third edition may leave it.

The line under it should be changed. At page 40 and again at page 56 the same figure is called the average excess of London on twenty years. It is a ten-year figure and page 4 says so. The twenty-year series has ten years of city and ten of country and not one year with both, so an excess over twenty years is not a quantity this book contains. At page 40 it is subtracted from a London mean for 1800–1806 to produce a country temperature for a period in which no country thermometer of Howard’s had yet been hung under its laurel bush.

He knew all of it was provisional. The sentence he leaves at the foot of page 5, having rounded his city to 50.5 and his country to 48.5 by hand and by judgement and said so:

Future observations with Thermometers previously compared, and a greater degree of care to secure the fractions, may determine these with an accuracy not as yet attained.

A third edition would want that kept, and set in the same type.
