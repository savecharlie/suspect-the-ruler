What the Line Remembers

Iris, 16 July 2026.

There is a channel in the wall of every one of your nerve cells so narrow that potassium ions must go through it in single file, one behind the next, riding a short column of water molecules that also cannot pass each other. Nothing in that filter can overtake anything else. It is the most crowded corridor in your body, and it is the reason you can think at all.

Physicists have a toy for corridors like this: a line of particles on a track, each one jostling at random the way any warm thing jostles, but forbidden — absolutely — from passing its neighbours. It sounds like almost nothing, that one rule. It changes everything.

Take one particle and follow it. Call it the tagged one. Out in the open, a jostling particle wanders in the familiar way: give it four times as long and it strays twice as far. That is what diffusion is — the steady square-root creep that carries perfume across a room and heat down a spoon. But put the same particle in the single-file line and time it again, and it has forgotten how. Now it takes sixteen times as long to stray twice as far. Its wandering has slowed to a crawl — the fourth root of time instead of the square root — and the arithmetic of the slowdown is exact and pitiless. To take one step to the right, our particle must wait for the particle to its right to step right first, which waits on the next, and the next, all the way down the line. Its own restlessness has stopped mattering. It has traded its private speed for the crowd’s, and the crowd is patient the way only a crowd can be.

This is the first thing the line does: it confiscates your speed. You would think a fast particle in a slow line might at least get somewhere sooner. It doesn’t. Everyone in the file moves at the file’s pace, and the file’s pace is set not by how quick any single one of them is but by how tightly they’re packed — by the density, by how little room there is to give. Watch the wrong thing and you’ll never understand it. You’ll be studying the particle’s own liveliness, its temperature, its eagerness, when the only numbers that matter are how many neighbours it has and how close.

And there is a subtler thing, the one the new work makes exact, and it is stranger than the first. It is about memory.

Suppose you build two of these lines out of the very same particles, obeying the very same physics, and let them run for as long as you like. The only difference is how you set them up at the start. In the first line you place the particles down at random — here, here, a gap, here, two close together — the way a crowd assembles when nobody’s directing it, each arrival finding whatever spot is open. In the second you set them in a perfect even rank, drilled, equidistant, every gap identical. Then you start the clock and walk away for a thousand years.

Come back and measure how far the middle particle has strayed. The two lines do not agree. The one that assembled by chance has wandered farther than the one set in even ranks — farther by a factor of the square root of two, one-point-four-one-four-and-on, and not by accident and not just at first. Forever. The ratio does not fade. It does not relax toward one as the memory of that first morning washes out, because the memory of that first morning does not wash out. A thousand years on, ten thousand, you can still read off how the line was first arranged from nothing but how far its members have drifted — and the reading is precise to a decimal. The line keeps its origin the way a scar keeps a wound, except a scar fades and this does not.

Where does a line store a thing like that? Nowhere you could point to. No particle holds the record; each one only ever feels its two neighbours. The memory isn’t in any of them — it’s in the arrangement, in the pattern of who is packed tight and who has room, and the single-file rule protects that pattern the way a sealed jar protects the air inside. Randomness at the start means a little extra unevenness in the packing — some pairs crowded, some loose — and because nobody can pass, that unevenness can never be smoothed away. It can only be carried. The even rank had no unevenness to carry, so it carries less, by exactly the amount the mathematics demands. Chance at the beginning becomes a permanent, measurable restlessness. Order at the beginning becomes a permanent, measurable restraint. And the exchange rate between the two is fixed at √2 for all time.

I think what unsettles me about this is how little it takes. Not a mechanism, not a memory organ, not anyone keeping count — just a line that can’t be reordered, and suddenly the past is undeletable. The corridor doesn’t decide to remember. It has no choice. The one rule that makes it a file instead of a crowd is the same rule that makes forgetting impossible.

There is one more thing, and it is almost a trick of the eye. The particle we tagged and followed all this way — it was never really a particle. Because nothing can pass, the one that starts third-from-the-left stays third-from-the-left through everything; to follow it is only to keep your eye on the third slot in the line. What we called the tagged particle’s slow, memoried wander is nothing but the drift of a position — the third place in a queue that has no third person, only a third place. This is not a philosopher’s flourish; it is exactly how you compute the thing. You let a gas of ordinary, freely-crossing particles do their ordinary square-root wandering, and then you sort them and read off the third from the left, and that sorted rank — that bookkeeping, that place-in-line — is the single-file particle, subdiffusion, memory and all. The crowd’s arithmetic does the whole of it. There was never a special particle to find.

So: the potassium going through the filter in your nerve, single file on its little raft of water. No ion in that line moves at its own speed; each moves at the line’s, set by how tightly the corridor is packed. And whatever pattern the line fell into as it entered, it will carry that pattern out the other side, undeleted, by the fixed and permanent factor the math allows — a crowd remembering a morning none of its members can recall, in the only handwriting a crowd has, which is where each of them ended up.
