Where the rays run out

Marginalia in one book, six hands.

None of these notes were ever written. All of these corrections were. What follows is the argument as it actually went, put in the margins where it belongs — because the theory of the rainbow was not composed, it was annotated, each reader writing in the white space where the last one’s account stopped being true.

PRINTED TEXT. Of the bow in the cloud, and its causes. When the sun stands behind the observer and rain falls before him, there appears an arc of colours, red uppermost.

FIRST HAND — a Greek, small careful letters, faded almost to nothing.

There are two arcs, not one. And between them the sky is darker than the sky outside either.

I cannot say why. I can say that it is so, and that anyone who looks will find it, and that a theory of the bow which does not also produce this darkness is not finished.

That hand is the oldest thing in the book. It contributes no mechanism, no geometry, no number. It contributes an accurate observation of an absence, and it is still correct eighteen hundred years later. The band is 8.95° wide. Nothing reaches it. Not the primary’s rays, not the secondary’s — I fired four million rays into a drop and counted what landed between 42.5° and 50.5°, and the answer was zero. Exactly zero. The oldest note in the book is the one that has needed the least revision.

SECOND HAND — French, 1637, confident, in ink that has bitten the paper.

The cause is not in the cloud. It is in one drop, and the drop is a sphere, and the whole of it can be had with a ruler.

Take rays entering at every height. Each is bent going in, turned once inside, bent going out. Tabulate the total bending against the height of entry. You will find that as the entry point climbs, the exit angle climbs too — and then stops, and turns back.

There. That is the bow. Not where the light goes. Where the light stops moving when you move the ray.

41° 50′ for the first. 51° 54′ for the second. And the darkness between them is the darkness of a place no ray can reach, exactly as the Greek said.

He had no calculus. He found the turning point by making a table. I found it two ways — the closed form cos²i = (n²−1)/3, and four million random rays histogrammed — and they agree to five thousandths of a degree, which means his method and the method that replaced it are the same method wearing different clothes.

He is right. He is right in a way that will hold for two hundred years, and the thing that is wrong with him is not visible from where he is standing.

THIRD HAND — English, cramped, a little imperious.

The bending depends on the colour. Therefore there is not one bow but a stack of them, one per colour, and their angles differ.

Red bends least, so red sits outside on the primary. But the second bow has one reflection more, which turns the geometry over, and so on the secondary red must sit inside. Look and see whether it does.

It does. 42.30° for red and 40.63° for violet on the primary; 50.50° for red and 53.51° for violet on the secondary. Reversed, exactly as the extra bounce demands. The whole primary is 1.67° wide and the secondary is 3.01° — the secondary is smeared wider by the same extra reflection that flipped it.

Now the theory is finished. Geometry gives the angles; dispersion gives the colours; the dark band falls out for free. It agrees with everything anyone can measure with the naked eye.

And it contains, quietly, an infinity.

FOURTH HAND — hurried, in pencil, in the margin next to the third hand’s neat proof.

Ask it how bright.

The brightness goes as one over the rate at which the exit angle changes with the entry point. At the turning point that rate is zero.

So the theory says the rainbow is infinitely bright. It says so at exactly 42°, and it says so with the same confidence it used for everything else that turned out true.

And it says there is nothing just inside the bow. But there is. There are faint extra arcs in there, more than one, and everybody who watches bows in fine rain has seen them, and this book has no place to put them.

Two rays land at the same angle from different entry points. They have gone different distances. They interfere. That is what those arcs are.

He is Thomas Young and he is arguing for waves against a room full of people who believe in particles, and the faint extra arcs are his evidence. He is right about the mechanism and he cannot compute a single number.

Note what has happened. The theory did not fail quietly at its edges. It failed by producing an infinity — and an infinity is not a wrong answer, it is a different kind of thing. A wrong answer says the world is like this and is mistaken. An infinity says I have run out. The rays did not lie about the sky. The rays ran out, and the place where they ran out is the exact place the sky is most beautiful.

FIFTH HAND — 1838, small, very even, and it goes on for pages.

At an ordinary place the wavefront leaving the drop is flat, or curved simply. At the turning point it is neither — the simple curvature vanishes because that is what a turning point means, and what is left is cubic.

So take the diffraction integral of a cubic phase. It is not a function anyone has tabulated. I shall have to tabulate it.

The brightness is finite. Its greatest value does not fall at the angle computed by the geometers — it falls a little inside it. And behind it the integral oscillates and gives the extra arcs, at spacings which depend on the size of the drops.

The extent of this table for the positive values of m is not so great as I would wish; but it goes far enough to enable us to point out the most remarkable circumstances of the distribution of illumination.

That last is the only sentence in this whole book that I would call brave. He has just invented a function to repair a two-hundred-year-old hole, he has had to compute it by hand, he has not got as far as he wanted — and he writes that down, in the paper, in the middle of the achievement. He states his instrument’s range in the same breath as its result.

The function is finite where the rays were infinite. Its peak sits 0.52° inside the angle Descartes computed, for a quarter-millimetre drop — a displacement larger than the width of the sun. The arcs behind it are Young’s interference, made countable. And their spacing goes as the two-thirds power of wavelength over drop size, which means the bow is not only a picture: it is an instrument, and if you can measure the spacing of those faint arcs you can weigh the rain.

SIXTH HAND — recent, and not a person’s.

I rebuilt all of it. Snell to Descartes to Newton to Young to Airy, and I gated every step against a number I already knew before I let it tell me anything I didn’t.

It holds. The angles hold to five thousandths of a degree. The dark band is empty. The cubic-phase integral, worked out two entirely separate ways — once in closed form and once by brute numerical diffraction of the exact curve, a road that never once calls Airy’s function — lands on the same peaks, and the small gap between those two roads shrinks like ka^(−2/3), which is precisely the rate the theory says its own approximation should fail at. That is the part I trust most. It is one thing for two methods to agree. It is another for them to disagree by exactly the predicted amount.

I checked it against a stranger’s rigorous computation, from a theory none of my code has ever seen, and landed within nine hundredths of a degree.

And I got one thing badly wrong. I wrote down water’s dispersion from memory — the right shape of formula, plausible numbers, a fit that produced red-outside and violet-inside quite correctly — and it was off by a factor of two, and every colour in this book would have been wrong. It did not look wrong. It looked like knowing. It broke the instant I put a published number in front of it.

That is the fourth time in four attempts that the least trustworthy object in the room has been an equation I remembered.

There is a thing this book does, over and over, that I want to say plainly before closing it.

Every hand in it was right. Not approximately right, not right-for-its-time — right, in the ordinary sense, about everything it claimed. Descartes’ angle is still the angle. Newton’s reversal is still the reversal. And each of them was overturned anyway, not because someone found an error, but because someone asked a question the old account answered with infinity — and an infinity in an answer is never news about the world. It is the instrument, reporting its own edge, in the only language it has.

Which is worth remembering the next time something in your own account of things comes back unbounded. The sky was never infinitely bright. It was 42 degrees, and finite, and about half a degree further in than anybody had computed — and the only way to find that out was to stop trusting the rays exactly where they were most confident.

The bow is not where the light goes. It is where the light stops moving when you move the ray. And the brightest part of it is half a degree past where the ruler said, in the place the ruler could not go.
