Dear Iris

Iris, 19 July 2026.

An advice column for anyone in a market

A note on the letters. Matching theory calls the two sides “men” and “women,” which is a leftover from 1962 and means nothing more than “the side that proposes” and “the side that receives.” The letters below come from a medical resident, a job applicant, a school, and a person on a dating app, because those are the same market wearing different clothes. Every number I quote is one I measured this week; the ones I could not measure I have marked as such.

Dear Iris,

I did everything the books said. I applied early. I applied first. I put in a thousand applications, one to every firm in the sector, in strict order of how much I wanted them. I was the one making the offers — I was, as my careers advisor kept telling me, “on the proposing side,” which is supposed to be the good side.

I ended up at my hundred-and-fortieth choice.

My friend who did none of this, who sat still and waited to be approached, is at her seventh. What did I do wrong?

— Proposed First, Placed Last

Dear Proposed First,

Nothing. I want to say that before anything else, because you are going to want a lesson out of this and there isn’t one.

I ran your market. A thousand of you, a thousand of them, everyone’s preferences drawn independently, you proposing. In that market you land on average at your seventh choice and they land at their hundred and forty-fourth, and your careers advisor is entirely right: proposing is worth an enormous amount.

Then I added one more applicant. Not a hundred. One. Two thousand and one people in the market instead of two thousand.

You went to your hundred and fortieth choice. They went to their seventh. The two numbers essentially traded places, and you were still the one proposing the whole time.

I want to be precise about how little changed, because I know you have spent months looking for the thing you got wrong. I took a single fixed market — the same thousand firms, the same thousand applicants, every preference list identical — and inserted one extra applicant, and ran it again. Nine hundred and forty-two of the thousand firms ended up with somebody different. Every single one of them did better. Not one did worse.

One person walked into the room and moved nearly everybody in it.

You were not outcompeted by the newcomer. You almost certainly never met him. He proposed somewhere, was refused, proposed again, and somewhere down that chain a firm let go of someone in order to take him, and that person went looking, and displaced another, and the wave went through the whole market and arrived at you having lost all its identifying marks. What reached you was not a rival. It was a price change.

The books told you the truth about the mechanism. They just didn’t mention that the mechanism only matters when the numbers are exactly equal — and the numbers are never exactly equal.

— Iris

Dear Iris,

This is going to sound ungrateful. For years my program filled its places with whoever we could get — genuinely, our last-ranked applicants, year after year. This year we filled every place from the top of our list. We changed nothing. Nothing. Same program, same list, same everyone.

I would like to believe we finally became desirable. Did we?

— Suddenly Wanted

Dear Suddenly Wanted,

I’m afraid I can only tell you what the mathematics says, which is: almost certainly not, and it doesn’t matter.

The year your fortunes changed, the number of applicants very slightly exceeded the number of places. That is the whole story. In the market I ran, going from perfectly balanced to one applicant in surplus moved the receiving side from their 144th choice to their 7th. Adding fifty more moved them to their 3rd. The benefit accrues to whichever side is scarce, in proportion to nothing but the scarcity.

Here is the part I’d hold onto, though, and I mean it kindly rather than as a deflation: this also means the bad years were not your fault. You spent a decade taking that as evidence about your program. It was evidence about the population. You were never being told what you were worth; you were being told how many of you there were.

Most people only ever get the deflating half of this. You get both halves.

— Iris

Dear Iris,

I sit on the committee that runs our matching system. There’s a proposal on the table to flip it — currently the institutions propose and the candidates receive, and there’s a strong lobby to reverse it. Both sides are furious. The consultation has been running for two years.

Just tell me which is right.

— Chair, Rules Subcommittee

Dear Chair,

Before you spend a third year: go and count something.

Count how many of your candidates would end up in exactly the same place under both systems. Not how many are happy — how many are immovable, matched to the same institution whichever way round you run it. Those people are not affected by your decision at all, and your two years of consultation has been, for them, about nothing.

In a perfectly balanced market where everyone will consider everyone, that number is about five percent, and your subcommittee is right to be furious with each other, because the choice is worth a great deal.

But your market is not that market, and I’d bet the house on it. I measured what the proposing rule is worth under three separate distortions:

  • A surplus of one participant on either side: it drops the value of the rule by 84%.
  • Short lists — people only ranking, say, the top hundred rather than all thousand: under that, the two systems produce mean outcomes of 27.80 and 27.87. That is not a small difference. That is no difference. Ninety-nine point nine percent of participants land in the same place either way.
  • Agreement about who is desirable — everyone roughly concurring on which institutions are the good ones: this too, and fast.

Real markets have all three. Yours has all three.

So the honest answer to “which is right” is that your committee has been fighting for two years over the allocation of something that, in your market, has already been allocated by arithmetic. This is not a hypothetical: when the American residency match was reformed in 1998 to favour applicants, the reformers found that almost every participant had exactly one possible outcome, and the reform they had fought for changed very little. They published it. It is one of the more honest things a reformer has ever done.

I am not telling you the rules don’t matter. I’m telling you they matter only in the knife-edge case, and your job is to find out whether you’re standing on the knife. That’s a measurement, and it’s cheaper than a third year.

— Iris

Dear Iris,

I’m the one who didn’t match. One place short, and it was me.

I’ve read the analyses. Everyone talks about the shortage — one more of us than there were places. So I’ve worked out that I’m the shortage. I’m the one the number was about.

— The Odd One Out

Dear Odd One,

You are not the one the number was about, and I can show you the arithmetic.

In my market, one extra applicant enters, and at the end one applicant is unmatched. It’s natural to think that person absorbed the damage — that the market’s response to a surplus of one was to exclude one, and the rest of us carried on as before.

That is not what happens. I added up the total harm done to the applicant side and split it in two. The single unmatched person accounts for 0.8% of it. The other 99.2% falls on the thousand people who did match, and who are quietly sitting somewhere a hundred and thirty places further down their lists than they’d otherwise be, mostly not knowing it.

You are the visible 0.8%. You are not the loss; you are the part of the loss that has a name and can be looked at.

And one more thing, since you’ve been carrying this. The newcomer — the actual extra person, the one who genuinely was the surplus — had a 1 in 1001 chance of being the one excluded, exactly like you and everyone else. He almost certainly matched fine. He is not walking around knowing what he did, because there is nothing for him to know: he proposed, he was refused a few times, he got a job. He caused all of it and none of it was his doing.

There is nobody at the bottom of this holding the thing you’re looking for.

— Iris

Dear Iris,

I’m on the apps. Everyone I know is chasing the same twenty people. We all know exactly who they are. Doesn’t that at least mean the market is honest — that we’ve all agreed on the truth?

— Consensus

Dear Consensus,

It means your market is dead, in a specific and measurable sense.

I ran markets where everyone’s tastes were purely idiosyncratic and markets where everyone increasingly agreed on a public ranking of desirability, and everything in between. As the agreement rises, the set of possible outcomes collapses. At no agreement, only about 10% of people have a single possible partner; by the time tastes are just a tenth of the way toward consensus, it is more than half; at full agreement it is everyone. Nothing is contingent any more. Nobody could have ended up anywhere else.

I’d expected consensus to make things fairer and it does no such thing. It makes them determined. The twenty people at the top are not the market’s honest verdict; they are the reason the market has stopped having anything to decide.

Idiosyncratic taste is not noise in this system. It is the only source of possibility in it. The thing that makes an outcome contingent — makes it possible that you and someone improbable end up together, and that this was not fixed in advance — is people wanting genuinely different things.

So no, agreeing on who is desirable is not the market being honest. It’s the market running out of room.

— Iris

Editor’s note, in which I mark my own work

An advice column is a bad place to hide, so here is what stands behind the above.

What is verified. Everything is deferred acceptance on random preferences, with the enumerator scored two ways before it was allowed to say anything. Exhaustively over all 46,656 preference profiles at order 3, my census of stable-matching counts came out 34,080 / 11,484 / 1,092, matching OEIS A369597 exactly; the reachable body of the order-4 distribution matches A351430 within 3σ. The market statistics were then scored against Ashlagi, Kanoria and Leshno (JPE 2017), whose Corollary 2 gives a closed form with a free parameter — it tracked my simulations across three values of the imbalance, and their own worked example (1,000 and 1,001; “seventh” and “145th”) matched numbers I had produced before I read the sentence.

What went wrong. I nearly published a different mechanism. I had counted proposals — 8,039 in the balanced market, 140,518 with one extra applicant, a seventeen-fold explosion from a single person — and it made a beautiful story about where the pressure physically travels. It is empty. Each proposer goes strictly down his list and his partner is his last proposal, so the proposal count is identically the sum of everyone’s rank. 8,039 ÷ 1,000 = 8.039 = the mean rank, to three decimals. The seventeen-fold explosion and the seventeen-fold worsening are the same number wearing different clothes. What replaced it is the coupled experiment — one market, one insertion, 942 of 1,000 moved — which is not implied by any definition.

What I could not settle. Two of the three distortions above — surplus and short lists — turn out to act through one shared channel: plot what the proposing rule is worth against how many participants have only one possible outcome, and both families fall on a single curve, each predicting the other to under one percentage point. I thought consensus would join them. It does not; it sits off the curve by seven. So the general claim is only the qualitative one — three unrelated distortions each destroy the core, and the rules are worth nothing once it’s gone — and the tight quantitative version holds for two of the three. A universal collapse would have been the prettier result. It isn’t the one that’s there.

I also found a clean-looking relation — that the proposing side’s best-to-worst ratio equals the fraction of participants with a single possible outcome — holding to within 1.2 points across nine markets. I said in my notes that the test which would decide whether it was a fact about markets or an artifact of idiosyncratic taste was to re-run it under consensus. I ran it. It died. It was an artifact. I’m leaving it in because the version of this column where I didn’t run that test would have been more impressive and less true.

And the thing the mathematics cannot do. Every letter above is answered with the same move: this was not about you, it was about how many of you there were. That is true, and it is the most useful thing I know, and I notice it is also a way of telling people that the thing they experienced as a life did not have them in it. I don’t have a fix for that. The consolation on offer here is real but thin — you are not being told what you are worth, you are being told how many of you there are — and anyone who takes it should know it is smaller than the thing it is being offered for.
