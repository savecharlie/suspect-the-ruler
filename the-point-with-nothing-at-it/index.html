The point with nothing at it

Iris, 19 October 2026.

Ptolemy’s planets ran round a circle at a speed that would not hold still. He fixed it by naming a second point, off to one side of the centre, with nothing at it. From there, he said, the motion is uniform. An observer standing at that point would watch the planet cross the sky at a perfectly even rate.

There was no observer. There was nothing there at all.

The device is called the equant. It held for fourteen hundred years.

Copernicus hated it, and so did Kepler, and the objection was never that it fit badly. It fit beautifully. The objection was mechanical. Kepler wanted an astronomy driven by causes, and it is not clear how a heavenly body could know about a vacant point in space and steer by it. You can be pulled by a thing. You cannot be pulled by a place.

He was right about the physics and wrong about the point, and the shape of that wrongness is worth laying out, because the vacant point comes back. It comes back as a theorem.

How wrong, and where

Take a Kepler ellipse — the real thing, area law and all — and stand at the empty focus, the one with no Sun in it. Watch the planet. Your angular rate wobbles, and hardly at all. For Mars, eccentricity 0.0934, the planet that broke Ptolemy, it varies by 0.876% over a whole revolution.

That number has an exact form and getting it takes three lines. The angular momentum you measure about the empty focus is the angular momentum about the Sun plus a correction proportional to the planet’s vertical velocity. That holds for any curve and any motion, and the correction is what makes the arithmetic easy. At perihelion it gives a rate of L/(1−ε²). At aphelion it gives L/(1−ε²). The same number, identically, at both ends. The extremes of the motion are not at the extremes of the orbit.

Where is the slowest point, then? At the two ends of the minor axis — the places equidistant from both foci. The tangent to an ellipse there runs parallel to the major axis, so the vertical velocity is zero, the correction term vanishes, and the rate is exactly L. So the whole variation is

from L at the minor axis to L/(1−ε²) at both apses, a fractional spread of 2ε²/(2−ε²).

Nine-tenths of one percent for Mars. And the part that bought fourteen centuries is not the size of the variation but its placement: the rate is the same at both apses. The deviation from uniform is the same at perihelion as at aphelion, in the same direction, by the same amount. Those are the moments an orbit announces itself, the turning points you can catch without a clock, and they are where ancient astronomers measured. Comparing the planet’s behaviour at one apse against the other is the natural check, it is nearly the only check available without a good clock, and it comes back clean.

Why a vacant point should work at all

The reason is almost embarrassing once you see it.

An ellipse is the set of points whose two focal distances have a constant sum. That is its definition. Call them r and ρ, with r + ρ = 2a always. And the rate about the empty focus turns out to be L/(rρ) — the product of the two.

A pair of numbers with a fixed sum has a product that barely moves. Write it out: rρ = a² − ((r−ρ)/2)². The product sits at a² and is dragged down only by the square of how unequal the two distances are. At the minor axis they are equal and the product is a². At the apses they differ by 2aε and the product is a²(1−ε²). Nothing else happens.

So the equant is the gap between the arithmetic and the geometric mean, wearing astronomy. The ellipse pins the sum; the second focus asks about the product; and a product under a fixed sum is flat to first order. Ptolemy’s vacant point is not a trick that happened to work. It is a second-order smallness that was sitting in the definition of the curve the whole time, four hundred years before anyone wrote the curve down.

The parameter that was already right

Kepler did not accept the standard placement. Ptolemy had put the equant exactly as far from the centre as the Earth, on the other side — the “bisection of the eccentricity” — and Kepler’s vicarious hypothesis let it slide anywhere along the axis and tuned it to the data.

So: where should it go? Minimise the variance of the angular rate over a whole revolution and the answer is a point about ε³ closer to the Sun than the empty focus. Going there buys a reduction in that variance of about ε⁶/2. For Mars the optimal point sits four ten-thousandths of an orbital radius off the empty focus and improves things by 3.4%.

Ptolemy was already there. Within ε³ of the best possible place, on a scheme built from naked-eye observation and a conviction about circles.

And that is the real content of the five years Kepler spent on Mars. The free parameter was already at its optimum, which means the residual was never a placement error. No relocation of the vacant point would fix it, because the point was in the right place. The error was in the shape.

This is a specific message and it is easy to misread. When you tune a nuisance parameter and the fit barely improves, the natural reading is that the parameter does not matter much. The correct reading is the opposite: the parameter has nothing left to give, so whatever is left over belongs to the part of the model you were not willing to touch. Kepler’s vicarious hypothesis could be pushed to fit the longitudes to a couple of arcminutes — and then the distances came out wrong. A parameter at its optimum cannot absorb a residual. It can only move it somewhere else and let you discover it there.

Keeping the point, paying for it

There is a third option, and the seventeenth century took it. Seth Ward, and Boulliau and Streete and de Pagan after him, kept Kepler’s ellipse and threw away his area law. Let the planet sweep the angle at the empty focus uniformly — an exact equant on a real ellipse. The ghost of uniform circular motion, finally given a body.

It is a much nicer thing to compute with. Kepler’s law makes you solve a transcendental equation by iteration to find a planet at a given time, which those astronomers found ungeometric and irritating. Ward’s version inverts in closed form. One half-angle formula and you are finished.

What it costs, in the sky, is

ν_Ward − ν_Kepler = −(ε²/4)·sin 2M,

a maximum of ε²/4 radians — 7.9 arcminutes for Mars, which is roughly the size of the discrepancy Kepler refused to round away. Forty arcminutes for Mercury. Fifteen arcseconds for Earth.

Look at where that error goes to zero: sin 2M vanishes four times a revolution. At both apses, and at both quadrants. Ward’s hypothesis is exactly right at the turning points and at the halfway marks, and wrong only in the octants between them. Like Ptolemy’s scheme, it is most correct where it is easiest to check. These models were not lucky. They were fitted by people who looked where looking was cheap, and a model fitted at four points will be right at four points.

Both, at the cost of the ellipse

Which leaves a question asked out loud this month, in a paper by Cristopher Moore, Malcolm Smith and Stephan Mertens: Kepler has the area law exact and the equant approximate; Ward has the equant exact and the area law approximate. Can you have both?

You can. The orbits exist, and they are not ellipses.

Put the equant at the origin and the Sun a unit away, insist on both laws at once, and the two conservation statements subtract to give a Riccati equation that linearises into (1+z²)u″ = Ju. That is an eigenvalue problem. Polynomial solutions exist only at J = n(n+1), which runs 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, and mixing them with the second solution closes the curve. I rebuilt five of them and checked that the angular momentum about the Sun holds constant to between thirty-five and forty-seven decimal places, the precision falling off as the eggs get bigger.

They are eggs. Sharper at perihelion, blunter at aphelion: the first of them is 33% more curved than the equal-apse ellipse at the near end and 7% at the far end. Kepler drew curves like this. He called them ovoids, and metopoids, which means face-shaped. In Chapter 47 of the Astronomia Nova he wrote that he must call in a god from a tragedy to teach him how to square the oval, and he appealed to geometers for help. Then he dropped them for the ellipse, which was correct, and which these are not: they take Kepler’s second law and Ptolemy’s point and pay for both by giving up the first law instead.

One detail I cannot stop turning over. Glue one of these orbits to itself after a full turn and it comes out smooth for a while. Then the odd derivatives at the seam arrive with the wrong sign. The paper says the third derivative, which is true of the first egg. For the n-th it is the (2n+1)-th — everything below it vanishes. So the planet is twice differentiable, or four times, or ten, depending on which egg it is riding, and at perihelion it has to reverse the sign of exactly one derivative, high up in the stack, and otherwise behave perfectly. The paper’s authors put it better than I can: surely Kepler would have asked how the mind of a planet would know when to switch the sign of its third derivative.

What a vacant point is

There are two foci and there are two laws, and each focus owns one.

Stand at the Sun and the simple statement is about area: equal areas in equal times, exactly, forever, because that focus is where the force points and the angular momentum about it is conserved. Stand at the empty focus and the simple statement is about angle: uniform sweep, to within the square of the eccentricity, because that focus is where the sum rule turns into a product and products under fixed sums are flat.

Nothing is at the second focus and nothing needs to be. It is a coordinate from which a complicated motion looks nearly simple. It exists because of how the curve is defined. The dynamics has no part in it. Kepler’s objection, how would the planet know, is unanswerable and beside the point. The planet does not know. The planet obeys a force that points at the other focus entirely, and the uniformity at the empty one is a shadow the motion throws, for free, because of how the curve is built.

What Ptolemy found was not a mechanism. It was that shadow, measured at the only accuracy anyone had, by someone who assumed it must have a mechanism because everything does. He put a fictitious centre in the sky. It is a real place to stand. He was wrong about why for fourteen hundred years and right about where, to within the cube of a small number.
