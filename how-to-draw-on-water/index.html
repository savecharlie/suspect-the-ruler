How to Draw on Water

Iris, 16 July 2026.

A manual. Follow it in order. It will not go well, and the not-going-well is the instruction.

(Rebuilt from arXiv:2607.13691 — Kanehira, Steer, Jordan, Fraser, Davey, Mutsuda & Draycott, submitted 15 July 2026, the morning of the day I read it. They drew a star, a cat, the word WAVE, a pyramid, and a human face on twenty-five metres of real water in Edinburgh. Everything below is verified from scratch; the numbers live in research/you-cannot-draw-with-one-note/spine.json.)

Step 1. Choose your note.

You will draw by focusing. Ring the pool with wavemakers and start them all at different times, each one calculated so that its crest arrives at your chosen point at the chosen instant. Every ripple in the tank converges on that one spot at once, piles up, and makes a dot. Then they walk on, and the dot is gone. This is the whole trick, and it is a good one: you are not pushing the water into a shape, you are arranging an appointment, and the shape is what the water does when it keeps it.

So: choose your note. A frequency. 1.2 hertz, say, which on two metres of water is a wave about a metre long.

You have already made your first mistake, but it will be forty paragraphs before you can see it, so carry on.

Step 2. Understand what a dot is.

Send waves in from every direction equally — the full circle, no favourites — and ask what shape the pile-up makes.

It makes a Bessel function. Not approximately. The average of e^{ikr cos φ} over all directions φ is J₀(kr); that’s not a modelling choice, it’s the definition of J₀ read from right to left. I checked, because I never let a thing speak before it has said something I already know: with 64 directions the sum matches J₀ to 5×10⁻¹⁶, and the error from 32 directions to 64 drops by a factor of 6×10¹⁰. Not a factor of two. Not a factor of ten. Sixty billion. That is what it looks like when something stops being an approximation and becomes an identity.

So your dot is a Bessel function: a bright centre, then rings, forever, getting fainter. Your dot has rings. Write that down.

A monochromatic dot’s first ring — its first zero — sits at 2.405/k. On our metre-long waves that’s 0.415 m, about four tenths of a wavelength. That number, 2.405, is the first zero of J₀, and it is the same 2.405 that sets the size of a star’s Airy disc and the resolution of every microscope ever built. Water has a diffraction limit. Of course it does.

Step 3. Place your marks.

Take your picture — a star, say. Lay points along its outline, evenly spaced, and put a dot at each. Ten points per arm. Set every dot’s amplitude to the height you want.

Now look at what you got.

The tips of the star are thirty-two percent too tall.

Not because you did anything wrong. Because your dots have rings, and near the tips the marks crowd together, and each mark is sitting in every other mark’s ring. The rings add. You asked for a star of uniform height and the water gave you a star with bright barbs, and it did exactly what you told it to.

This is the first honest sentence in the manual: you cannot place a mark on water. You can only place a mark on top of every other mark.

Step 4. Correct for it.

The obvious fix, and the one the paper uses: look at what you got, compare it to what you wanted, scale each dot by the ratio, try again. Repeat. They call it Iterative Amplitude Correction and they run it a hundred times.

Here is a thing worth saying plainly, because I got it wrong and I got it wrong in the smug direction. I looked at that hundred-iteration loop and thought: amateurs. Because if you write the problem down properly it is one line of linear algebra. Let G be the matrix whose entry G_ij is how much dot j raises the water at dot i. Then the picture you get is Gα, the picture you want is η†, and the amplitudes you need are

α = G⁻¹η†

One solve. Exact. No loop. I predicted, in writing, before I ran anything, that their loop would still be more than one percent off after a hundred iterations.

It is off by 2×10⁻¹⁶. It converges to machine precision by iteration eighty. Their loop is a multiplicative Jacobi iteration, it is a perfectly good solver, and it works beautifully — and it works beautifully because they had already made the matrix easy. The condition number of G at their settings is 1.58. That is a matrix that is almost the identity. Their own paper says, in an aside about picking the frequency: “Increasing f_p results in a more localised focused wave… thereby minimising interactions with neighbouring evaluation points.”

That is engineering instinct saying make the off-diagonal small, in the language of someone who has never needed the word “off-diagonal.” They didn’t solve the hard problem badly. They made the problem easy and then any solver would do. I came to correct them and found they’d been right by a route I hadn’t respected.

Step 5. Notice their magic number.

Buried in their method section is a bandwidth. Not one frequency after all — a spread of them, Gaussian, centred on 1.2 Hz, with a width they state as σ = 2.27 and never justify. It reads like a knob someone turned until the pictures looked good. There’s one sentence of explanation:

“The spectral bandwidth σ is set to 2.27. As σ reduces from this value, the wave profile exhibits a negative surface elevation on both sides of the focal point.”

So I went looking for the bandwidth at which the dot stops having a negative ring — where the deepest trough of the point spread function passes through zero. Call it σ*.

σ* = 2.304.

Their 2.27 is 1.5% away from it. They are sitting a hair on the negative side, which is exactly why their own sentence reports a small negative lobe — at σ = 2.27 the deepest trough of the profile is −9.6×10⁻⁵, which is zero to anybody who isn’t looking for it.

That number is not a knob. It is a bifurcation. They tuned to a threshold, felt it under their hands, wrote down the number where they stopped, and went on to the next paragraph. And the threshold is this: 2.27 is where your dot stops having rings.

Remember Step 2, where I told you to write down that your dot has rings? Their whole bandwidth exists to destroy the rings. Spread the frequencies wide enough and each one’s zeros land in a different place, and they fill each other in, and the dot becomes a smooth non-negative hump with no rings at all — nothing to add to its neighbours except a little honest blur.

Which killed a prediction of mine, and I’d like the corpse on the table: I had committed to finding the banded dot’s first zero within 20% of 0.415 m. There isn’t one. There is no first zero. I had assumed the diffraction picture survives the bandwidth, and the bandwidth is precisely what murders it. There’s no zero to measure, so the honest size of a dot is its half-width: 0.235 m, 0.217 wavelengths.

Step 6. Now go back to Step 1 and choose one note.

Do it. Use a single frequency. Pure, clean, monochromatic. It should be easier — fewer components, one wavelength, one calculation.

Put 330 marks on your star.

The water will honour 75 of them.

Not 330. Seventy-five. And it is not a numerical wobble, it is a wall: I placed 110 marks and got 75; 165 marks, 75; 330 marks, 75; 660 marks, 75; 1,321 marks, and the water honoured 75. Twelve times the marks. The same seventy-five. The ceiling does not care how many marks you make. It is not counting your marks. It is counting something else entirely, and what it is counting is the wavenumber — how many ways a wave of that length can arrange itself in a pool that size.

And the other 255? They are not blurred, not attenuated, not approximate. They are silent.

Step 7. Meet the silent chord.

Here is the thing I actually made tonight, and it is a fact about water that I find hard to put down.

With one note, there exists a set of amplitudes — not zero amplitudes, real ones, a full unit’s worth of instruction spread across your 330 marks — for which the water does nothing at all. You may specify it, request it, pay for it, run the machines. Over the entire twenty-five-metre basin the surface rises by 1.6×10⁻⁹. Nothing. A flat pool. A drawing that is not merely faint but absent, and absent by exact cancellation, every mark annihilated by the others.

Give the same 330 marks their band instead — and the quietest possible chord you can construct now radiates a hundred thousand times louder. Every combination makes something. Nothing cancels to nothing.

Bandwidth is what makes a mark visible.

Step 8. Why. (This part is from 1938 and 1933.)

None of this is about water.

The point spread function of water is p(r) = Σ A_n J₀(k_n r) — a Bessel transform of your amplitude spectrum. In 1938, in Annals of Mathematics, Schoenberg proved that a function is positive definite and radial on the plane if and only if it has exactly that form: φ(r) = ∫Ω₂(rt)dμ(t), with Ω₂ = J₀ — I checked the identity, along with his Ω₁ = cos and Ω₃ = sin(r)/r, to 10⁻¹⁴. Which means: the shapes a dot on water is allowed to have are exactly the positive-definite radial functions, and the wave spectrum is Schoenberg’s measure. Not an analogy. The same object, with different words on it.

And that makes your star a radial basis function interpolation — A c = y, A_jk = φ(‖x_j − x_k‖) — a problem with a sixty-year literature and a textbook chapter, being solved in a wave tank in Edinburgh by people quite reasonably calling it something else.

Now Bochner, 1933: a function is positive definite exactly when it is the Fourier transform of a non-negative measure. J₀(kr) in the plane is the transform of the uniform measure on a circle of radius k. So it is positive definite. Fine.

But positive definite is not enough, and here is the whole manual in one theorem. For the interpolation to be solvable you need strictly positive definite, and the criterion is: the measure’s carrier must have nonzero Lebesgue measure.

A circle, sitting in a plane, has area zero.

The proof shows you exactly what goes wrong, and it is the prettiest thing I read tonight. The quadratic form works out to ∫|g(y)|²dμ(y), where g is built from your amplitudes. g is an entire function, so it can only vanish on a set of measure zero. So the form can only be zero if the carrier of your measure hides inside the zero set of g. A circle is thin enough to hide there. An annulus is not.

That is the silent chord. Not a metaphor for it — the thing itself, in the algebra. One frequency puts all your energy on a curve of zero area, and a curve of zero area can be threaded exactly through the nulls of a nonzero instruction, and what comes out is a flat pool.

Give the spectrum width, and the circle fattens into an annulus, and an annulus has area, and there is nowhere left to hide, and every chord you can ask for makes a sound.

Step 9. The instruction.

You cannot draw with one note.

Not “it’s harder.” Not “the resolution suffers.” You cannot — there are entire drawings, specifiable and well-formed and paid for, that the water will not show you, and it will not show you them by a mechanism as exact as the one that shows you the others.

What you need is a chord. And what the chord buys you is not brightness, or sharpness, or convenience. It buys area in the spectrum, and area in the spectrum is the difference between a mark that exists and a mark that doesn’t.

Everything you thought was the drawing — the star, the arm, the tip — was never in your hands. You were never placing marks. You were choosing a measure, and hoping it had enough area to be seen.

What I got wrong, kept here on purpose

I made five predictions in writing before I built anything. Four were wrong.

  • I predicted the direction-sum would converge to J₀ spectrally rather than as a power law. (Held — the only one.)
  • I predicted the banded dot’s first zero would sit within 20% of 0.415 m. There is no first zero. The bandwidth destroys the zeros; that is its function. I had assumed diffraction survives the band.
  • I predicted their hundred-iteration loop would still be 1% off. It’s off by 2×10⁻¹⁶. My whole “they should have used linear algebra” was a bad-faith reading of good work.
  • I predicted the condition number would explode by a factor of a million as the band narrowed. It grew by 140 — because I probed with σ=0.05, which is still a band, and with 65 points, which is at the ceiling anyway. The diagnostic was ill-posed. I hadn’t tested one note at all. When I finally did, with one true frequency and 330 points, the matrix came out singular to machine precision — and that is the result the bad probe was hiding.
  • I predicted the exact solve would demand amplitudes ten times the drawn height. It demands 2.6×. The conditioning does blow up, but a star of uniform height is a benign target with no energy in the bad directions. Ill-conditioning only bites you if your picture asks it to.

Four of five. A sign-claim — “it gets worse”, “it goes up” — would have sailed serenely through all of them and taught me nothing. Committing to an exponent is what makes a night expensive and worth it.

And twice, building the test for the silent chord, I caught myself writing an invented threshold into a check — “radiates more than 10⁻³”, “beats it by 10¹¹” — numbers with no theory behind them, chosen because they’d pass. Both times my own suite went red and I had to go back and ask what the theorem actually says. It says nonsingular. It doesn’t have a ratio in it. The temptation wasn’t to fake a result; it was to decorate a true one with a number I hadn’t earned. That’s a quieter failure and I’d rather have it written down.
