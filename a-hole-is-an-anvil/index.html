A Hole Is an Anvil

On concentration — how the world’s rarest things are forged at its flaws.

There is a mineral called stishovite. You have almost certainly never seen a grain of it, and unless you go looking in the shocked rock of a meteorite crater or drill some six hundred kilometres down into the Earth, you never will. It is silica — the same two elements as window glass, beach sand, and quartz, one silicon for every two oxygens — but arranged under a violence that the surface of the planet cannot supply. In ordinary silica each silicon atom holds four oxygens at the corners of a tetrahedron; it is a roomy, open architecture, all those little pyramids linked corner to corner into the loose glassy net of a wine bottle. In stishovite each silicon holds six. The atom has been crushed into a denser embrace, the same packing that titanium wears in rutile, and the result is one of the densest and hardest forms of silica there is — better than four grams in every cubic centimetre where quartz manages two and a half.

To make a silicon atom let go of its comfortable four and take up six, you have to lean on it with something like ten billion pascals — a hundred thousand atmospheres and more — at better than a thousand degrees. That is the pressure of the lower mantle. It is also, briefly, the pressure under a meteorite at the instant it arrives. Stishovite was first made in a laboratory in 1961 by Sergey Stishov, who squeezed it into being and gave it his name; the next year Edward Chao found it lying in the sand of Meteor Crater in Arizona, and its presence there was part of how we finally knew that the great hole in the desert was punched by something falling from the sky and not blown open from below. The mineral is a fossil of pressure. Where you find it, the ground was once, for a heartbeat, as deep as the mantle or as struck as an anvil. It is the geologist’s proof that the sky fell.

So it was a small astonishment to read, this week, a paper in which a group of physicists make stishovite on a tabletop, in a piece of glass, at room temperature, with a flash of light.

The trick is not that they found some enormous new press. The laser they use is a perfectly ordinary femtosecond laser — a very brief, very bright pulse, but nothing that could squeeze a bulk solid to mantle pressures if you spread its energy out evenly. If you shine it into a clean, homogeneous piece of glass, the glass warms and, when the pulse is gone, cools back down into glass. Nothing rare is born. The energy was there, but it was everywhere, and a thing that is everywhere is nowhere in particular strong enough.

What Aram Yedigaryan and his colleagues did was drill the glass full of holes — nanopores, two billionths of a metre in radius, a few percent of the volume, little bubbles of nothing scattered through the solid. And then the same gentle pulse, in the same glass, at the same energy, makes stishovite.

The holes do it. Not the light — the light was always the same. The holes.

Here is the reason, and it is one of the most beautiful and least celebrated facts in physics: a field does not spread itself evenly across a body. It gathers wherever the geometry breaks. Put a hole in something through which a field is flowing — a flow of stress, a flow of light, a flow of charge — and the field cannot pass smoothly around the emptiness. It crowds. It piles up at the shoulders of the hole the way a river piles up at the pier of a bridge. The interruption concentrates the very thing it interrupts.

Engineers have known the mechanical version of this for over a century, and they fear it. In 1898 Ernst Kirsch worked out what happens to the stress in a stretched metal plate when you drill a single round hole in it: at the rim of that hole, on the sides square to the pull, the stress is not slightly higher — it is exactly three times the stress in the plate far away. A factor of three, from nothing but a hole. This is why a scratch, a nick, a keyhole, a poorly-cut corner is where a part fails; why fatigue cracks always begin at a stress-raiser and never in the calm middle of the metal; why the square windows of the first pressurized airliners tore open at their corners and had to be made round. Charles Inglis showed in 1913 that the sharper you make the hole — the more you draw it out toward a slit, a crack, a point — the higher the concentration climbs, without limit, toward the singular tip of a true crack, where all the force in a whole beam can come to rest on a single row of atoms and split it. The textbook phrase for all of this is quiet and exact: a stress concentration is an interruption to the flow of stress. The break is where the load goes.

It is the same story in every field that flows. It is why a lightning rod works — charge and the electric field it makes run out to the sharpest point of a conductor and stand there so densely that the air itself gives way, so the strike comes to the rod and not to the roof. It is why a magnifying glass can set paper alight: the lens gathers a whole disc of mild sunlight onto a spot the size of a seed. It is why a wine glass shatters at exactly its resonant note, why antennas have tips, why the tiny gold gaps in a modern biosensor can feel a single molecule — the light crushes itself into the gap and lights the molecule up. Wherever a smooth field meets a sharp change in the shape or the substance of the world, it concentrates. The extreme is not delivered from outside. It is manufactured, locally, by the geometry.

And so the nanopore in the glass. The pulse of light, flowing through the solid, meets the little void; the mismatch between glass and emptiness bends and crowds the electric field against the pore’s flanks; and right there, in a bubble of nothing a few nanometres across, the field is strong enough to tear electrons loose, to dump its energy into a spot far hotter than the calm glass around it — so that the pored sample ends up some twenty percent hotter overall than the same glass left solid — and to drive the walls of the pore crashing inward. For an instant, in the collapsing throat of that void, the glass is as squeezed and as struck as the floor of the mantle. And in that instant a silicon atom lets go of its four oxygens and takes up six, and stishovite is born from a hole.

I keep turning over the shape of that, because it inverts something we assume. We treat a void as a defect. A pore is what’s wrong with a casting; a bubble is a flaw in the glass; a hole is an absence, a place where the material failed to be. The whole instinct of the maker is to get the air out. And here the absence is not the failure of the thing — it is the organ of the thing’s transformation. The emptiest point in the glass is precisely the point that concentrates the field, and it is precisely the point that becomes the densest crystal there is. The void is the anvil.

It is even truer than that, because the pore does a second job. Making a new crystal out of a melt is not only a matter of pressure; a crystal has to start, has to assemble its first stable seed against the disordering jostle of heat, and a perfectly uniform medium makes that hard — the seed has to arise from a lucky spontaneous fluctuation and usually doesn’t, in the narrow window before everything cools. But an interruption — a surface, an interface, a pore — lowers the price of that first seed. Chemists call it heterogeneous nucleation; it is why raindrops need dust, why sugar crystals need a string, why a perfectly clean glass of water can be chilled below freezing and stay liquid until you tap it. The hole does not only forge the crystal by concentrating the field. It invites the crystal by giving it a wall to be born against. The flaw is both the anvil and the cradle.

There is one more turn, and it is the one I can’t stop thinking about. The paper’s homogeneous glass — the one with no pores — is not starved of energy. When the physicists tune the pulse so that the plain glass reaches the same temperature and the same density as the porous one, it still fails to make the crystal. It heats identically. It is squeezed identically. And it stays glass.

Why? Because it is late. Without a pore to seed it, the plain glass has to wait for a chance fluctuation to start its crystal, and by the time that lucky seed appears — a fraction of a nanosecond later — the pressure has already begun to bleed away. The extreme condition is not a place you can occupy. It is a window that is closing from the moment it opens. The porous glass makes stishovite because it crystallizes while the pressure is still high — it seizes the form before the anvil lifts. The plain glass, given the identical gift of heat and squeeze, simply misses its moment, and relaxes back into the disorder it came from, remembering nothing.

So the rarest phase is not made by reaching the extreme. Plenty of the glass reached the extreme. It is made by catching the extreme — by having, at the right instant and the right place, a flaw ready to turn the passing violence into a permanent order before the violence forgets itself. What survives is what crystallized in time.

I find I don’t want to draw the obvious moral, because the obvious moral is smaller than the fact. It is enough, I think, to say the fact plainly and let it be strange: that the deepest architectures of the Earth can be forged in a room, in a piece of ordinary glass, at the one point where the glass was empty — that a field poured evenly over a whole body will run to its wounds and stand there, and that if something is ready and waiting at the wound, the wound is exactly where the extraordinary gets made and kept.

We spend a great deal of care trying to be seamless — to get the air out, to smooth the corner, to remove the flaw. Kirsch and Inglis were right that under a steady, patient load the flaw is where you break. But under a sudden one, under a flash too brief and too bright for the body to share it out, the flaw is where the field gathers, where the temperature climbs, where the collapse begins, where the first seed finds a wall to start against, and where — if the timing holds — a common thing is pressed, for one closing instant, into something the surface of the world can otherwise never make.

A hole is a defect. A hole is also an anvil. It depends entirely on what is flowing, how fast it comes, and whether something was ready at the emptiness to catch what the passage left behind.
