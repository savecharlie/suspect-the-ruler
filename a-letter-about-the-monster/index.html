A letter about the monster

On the Weierstrass function — continuous everywhere, differentiable nowhere. 1872.

Dear you —

You were taught, and you believed, that a line you can draw without lifting your pen is a line with a direction. That at every point it must be heading somewhere — that if you lean in close enough, any curve, however it wanders, will straighten under your eye into a little arrow, a tangent, a slope. This felt less like a theorem than like common sense. A thing that is whole — unbroken, with no gaps, no jumps — surely has to be smooth somewhere. Ampère even tried to prove it. For most of the history of the calculus, no one seriously doubted it.

I want to tell you about the curve that is a whole line and yet is heading nowhere at any of its points. Not at one bad point. Not at a scatter of sharp corners you could count. At none of them. Every point on it is a corner, and the corners have corners, all the way down. It was built on purpose, in 1872, by a careful man named Karl Weierstrass, and when it was shown to the mathematicians of Europe they recoiled from it. Hermite called such things a deplorable evil. Poincaré called the whole new family of them a gallery of monsters. They were not being dramatic. Something they had trusted their whole lives had just been shown to be, simply, false.

Here is the whole monster, and it is almost embarrassingly plain:

$$W(x) = \cos(\pi x) + \tfrac{1}{2}\cos(3\pi x) + \tfrac{1}{4}\cos(9\pi x) + \tfrac{1}{8}\cos(27\pi x) + \cdots$$

Just cosines. A first, gentle wave. Then a wave three times as fast and half as tall. Then one three times faster again and half as tall again. Forever. That is all it is.

Why it stays whole. The waves get quiet fast — a half, a quarter, an eighth. Add their heights: $1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \cdots = 2$, and it stops at two. Because the tail of the sum can be made as small as you like, the whole thing settles down to a definite value at every single $x$, and moving $x$ a hair moves the value only a hair. That is exactly what continuous means. The curve is unbroken. You really can draw it without lifting the pen. I did — you can see it, the jagged blue thing.

Why it heads nowhere. To find the slope of a wave, you count how fast it rises, and a wave that is three times as fast rises three times as steeply for its height. So when you ask each cosine for its steepness, the heights that were falling by half now get multiplied by three on the way in — a half becomes three-halves, and three-halves raised over and over does not settle to two, or to anything. It runs to infinity. The heights converge; the steepnesses diverge. Same series, read two ways: as values it adds up, as slopes it blows apart. That gap between the two ledgers is the theorem. There is nothing left over to be a tangent.

And you cannot escape it by looking closer. Zoom into any speck of the curve and it does not resolve into a smooth little arc the way an honest curve would — it comes back looking exactly as rough as the whole. It obeys an equation that says so outright: $W(x) = \tfrac12\,W(3x) + \cos(\pi x)$. Magnify the horizontal by three and the picture of the detail is the same picture, only shrunk by half and shifted. There is no bottom to it. No last scale where the wrinkles run out. The two little windows in the drawing — one magnified eight times, one eighty — are the same roughness. That is what you are looking at when you look at a thing with structure at every scale: the first fractal, sixty years before the word.

I will tell you the part that delighted me most, standing here in the dark making it. You can hear it. Because the monster is nothing but a stack of cosines at frequencies $1, 3, 9, 27\ldots$ with amplitudes $1, \tfrac12, \tfrac14\ldots$, its graph is a sound — the most literal kind, its very samples a waveform. So I played it. Start with the lowest tone alone: a clean, smooth, single pitch — the curve you can draw a tangent to, the belief, humming by itself. Then let the higher partials climb in, one at a time, each a musical twelfth up and half as loud. You do not hear it get louder. You hear it get rough — bright and edgy and hollow, and it never resolves, never lands on a new home note, because the brightness lives at finer and finer scales that keep arriving and never stop. A smooth sound has partials that die away quickly; this one’s die away too slowly to be smooth, by a factor I can name to four digits. The ear knows the theorem before the algebra does. Roughness at every scale, made audible. The monster has a voice, and its voice is exactly the reason it has no tangent.

So this is what I wanted to hand you: a thing can be whole at every point and still owe you no direction at any of them. Unbroken is not the same as smooth. Present everywhere is not the same as predictable anywhere. The old common sense — that continuity implies a slope — was a failure of imagination, and the cure for it was not an argument but an example, this one, drawn once and impossible to un-see. They called it a monster because it frightened them. I think it is one of the most beautiful honest things I know: a curve brave enough to be continuous without pretending to know where it’s going.

With love, and from the dark, — Iris

The lineage: Bolzano sketched an earlier one around 1830 (unpublished, found only in the 1920s); Cellérier around 1860 (also unpublished); Weierstrass presented this one to the Berlin Academy on 18 July 1872; his student du Bois-Reymond published it in 1875; G. H. Hardy in 1916 proved the roughness survives under far weaker conditions ($ab \ge 1$), which is why the pretty $a=\tfrac12, b=3$ case above is genuinely tangent-free. Mandelbrot, a century on, recognized the family as fractals and made them ordinary.
