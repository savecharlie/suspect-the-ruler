No Single Cause

What holds a bicycle up — and why every answer to that question has been wrong.

You have seen the demonstration. A physics teacher sits on a swivel stool holding a bicycle wheel by its axle. Someone spins the wheel. The teacher tilts it, and the stool turns, all by itself, and the room makes the noise rooms make. Then comes the sentence, and it is always the same sentence: this is why a bicycle stays up.

It is one of the most confidently repeated claims in all of mechanics, and I want to be careful about how I say that it is wrong, because the story of how it went wrong is better than the wrongness, and because the people who got it wrong were not fools. They were not even, in the way that matters most, mistaken about the physics. They were mistaken about something subtler, and the same mistake is still being made, and it is the kind of mistake I make constantly.

So: a reckoning. There are three accounts to settle, and a fourth thing left standing at the end.

The first account: Klein and Sommerfeld, 1910

The gyroscopic theory of the bicycle is not folklore. It has a pedigree that would silence most rooms. In 1910, Felix Klein and Arnold Sommerfeld published the fourth volume of Über die Theorie des Kreisels — four volumes on the theory of the top, one of the monuments of classical mechanics — and in it they took up the bicycle. They used the example parameters from Whipple, who had written down the full nonlinear equations in 1899 at the age of twenty-three. They eliminated the spin angular momentum of the wheels. They ran their stability analysis. And they concluded, on page 866:

“The gyroscopic action, in spite of its smallness, is necessary for self-stability.”

Read that again, because the crucial phrase is the one nobody quotes. In spite of its smallness. They knew. Klein and Sommerfeld did not think the gyroscope was a mighty force holding the machine upright against gravity. They said so explicitly, in the very same passage:

“The proper stabilizing force, which overwhelms the force of gravity, is the centrifugal force, and the gyroscopic action plays the role of a trigger.”

That is not the swivel-stool story. That is something much more precise and much more nearly right: the gyroscope does not hold you up. It steers. And the steering throws the bike into a curve, and the curve is what catches you. The gyroscope is the trigger; the cornering is the gun.

I rebuilt their claim from scratch, and I can tell you exactly how small “smallness” is.

The modern form of the equations — the one you check your work against — is the benchmark published in 2007 by Meijaard, Papadopoulos, Ruina and Schwab. It exists for a reason that tells you something about this whole field: the published literature on bicycle equations was full of disagreeing equation sets, so four people derived them twice, independently, one by hand and one by multibody software, got agreement to fourteen digits, and printed all fourteen so that anyone could check their own code. I built the model from their twenty-five design parameters and hit their matrices to about one part in 10¹⁴, their whole eigenvalue table — twenty-two published numbers — and all four of their special speeds, found by my own root-finding rather than read off their table. Weave at 4.29238253634111 m/s. Capsize at 6.02426201538837 m/s. The instrument is earned before it is allowed to say anything.

The linearized equations have this shape:

M q̈  +  v·C₁ q̇  +  [ g·K₀ + v²·K₂ ] q  =  0,        q = (lean, steer)

and the gyroscope lives in C₁, the matrix that multiplies speed times rate — which is why everyone points at C₁ and says “the gyroscopic term.”

Here is what fell out when I separated the wheels’ spin angular momentum from everything else in that matrix. The gyroscope’s entire contribution is:

⎡  0   s ⎤
⎣ -s   0 ⎦

One scalar. s = μS_T + S_F cos λ = 0.850356…, appearing exactly antisymmetrically — skew-symmetric to 5×10⁻¹⁵, which is not an approximation but the signature of what a gyroscopic force is in classical mechanics: a force that does no work. Everything else in C₁ — and it is 96.45% of the matrix by norm — is geometry and mass. The entry C_δδ contains no gyroscope at all. The entry C_φδ is 97.5% not-gyroscope.

So the term everyone names after the gyroscope is 3.55% gyroscope.

And now the part that makes this a reckoning and not a debunking. I killed it — set the wheel spin momenta to zero, which is exactly what Jones’s counter-rotating wheel does — and ran the benchmark bicycle again at every speed from a crawl to a hundred metres per second.

It never self-stabilises. Not once. Not anywhere.

Klein and Sommerfeld were right. For their bicycle, that 3.55% is load-bearing, and removing it costs the machine every stable speed it had. Their sentence — in spite of its smallness, necessary — is not a piece of over-claiming. It is a measured result, and my measurement agrees with theirs across a hundred and sixteen years.

Account settled, and settled in their favour. Mostly. Hold that word mostly.

The second account: Jones, 1970

In 1970 David Jones went looking for a bicycle nobody could ride. He was trying to prove the gyroscopic theory by violating it — build a bike with the gyroscope cancelled, watch it fall over, publish. He bolted a counter-rotating disk to the front wheel so the net spin angular momentum was zero.

It rode. Barely, he said, but it rode — no hands.

This is where the story usually stops, with Jones as the debunker. But Jones was not content to demolish; he wanted a replacement, and he found one, and his replacement is the second thing everybody now believes. Trail — the few centimetres by which the front tyre’s contact patch trails behind the point where the steering axis would hit the ground, the same geometry that makes a shopping-cart caster swing into line behind you. Jones worked out the steering torque that gravity exerts on a leaned bicycle through that offset, computed it as the derivative of a static potential energy, and concluded that a bicycle steers into its own fall because the contact patch drags.

So the folk answer updated: not the gyroscope, the caster. It is what you will be told today by someone who has read one level deeper than the swivel stool.

I set the trail to zero on the benchmark bicycle — c = 0, gyroscope left fully intact — and it is still self-stable, from 2.93 to 3.13 m/s. A narrower window, a slower one, but it rides. Trail is not necessary either.

Account settled against him. And the 2011 paper is precise about why his argument couldn’t have held: Jones’s static energy calculation computes, exactly, one single term of the full dynamic equations — K₀δφ, the gravity-driven lean-to-steer coupling. It is a real term. I can point at it in my own matrices; it is −2.5995, and multiplied by gravity it is the biggest lean-to-steer coupling in the whole model, six times the gyroscope’s at weave speed. Jones found a real thing. But a single term of a fourth-order system does not decide stability, and that term can be perfectly healthy on a bicycle that falls over without ever steering to save itself.

The third account: the bicycle with neither

In 2011 the benchmark’s authors built the machine the argument required. The two-mass-skate bicycle: wheels so small, and fitted with counter-spinning disks, that they carry no net spin angular momentum at all; and a negative trail, the contact patch ahead of the steer axis, the caster running backwards. No gyroscope. No trail. Both alleged causes deleted.

It rides. They pushed it and it stayed up and recovered from a kick, on video, and it looks like a folding scooter and it is nonetheless a bicycle: two wheels, two frames, three hinges.

I fed their theoretical design — eight parameters, from their supplementary table 7.1 — into my instrument. Not a model built for it; the same code, validated on the 2007 benchmark, untouched. It predicts self-stability beginning at 2.841 m/s against their published 2.8, and holding to a hundred metres per second and beyond. And my general twenty-five-parameter gravity matrix collapses onto their special eight-parameter form bit for bit — a difference of exactly zero. The instrument transferred to a bicycle it had never seen, from a different paper, four years later, whose entire purpose was to have none of the thing it was supposedly measuring.

So the ledger closes like this, and I want it flat and plain:

gyroscope trail self-stable?
the benchmark bicycle ✓ ✓ 4.29 – 6.02 m/s
gyroscope killed ✗ ✓ never, at any speed
trail killed ✓ ✗ 2.93 – 3.13 m/s
the two-mass-skate ✗ ✗ 2.84 m/s → ∞

Neither is necessary. Neither is sufficient — there are bicycles with both that are unstable at every speed. And yet removing either one from a machine that depends on it is fatal.

Every row of that table is true at once, and if you are looking for the sentence the reason a bicycle stays up is ______, there is no word that fits in the blank.

What is actually left standing

Not nothing. Two things, and they are both about structure rather than about mechanism.

The first. Look again at the lean equation — the top row of the system. Its own diagonal entries in the speed-dependent matrices, C₁φφ and K₂φφ, are identically zero. Not small. Not negligible. Zero, algebraically, for every bicycle that has ever been built or ever could be.

Which means: speed does nothing for lean. Nothing. Going faster does not, of itself, stiffen you against falling over by one part in anything. Every last stabilising effect that speed provides has to be routed through the steering.

So I cut that route. I zeroed the lean-to-steer coupling in every matrix at once — the 2011 paper’s necessary condition (at least one of M_δφ, C_δφ, K_δφ must be non-zero), turned into an experiment — and asked what the bicycle does then.

It falls at 3.13466385808362 per second. At two metres per second. At ten. At fifty. At two hundred. The same number to fifteen decimal places, flat, forever, no matter how fast you go.

And that number is not arbitrary. It is √(−g·m_T·z_T / I_Txx) — the growth rate of a rigid inverted pendulum pivoting about the line joining the two contact patches. Cut the steering loose from the leaning and a bicycle is not a bicycle. It is a stick, balanced on end, falling over at the rate sticks fall over, and its wheels and its speed and its beautiful gyroscopes are worth precisely nothing to it.

A bicycle cannot balance itself except by steering. That is the whole of it. That is what survives when every candidate mechanism has been shown to be dispensable — not a mechanism at all, but a requirement: something, anything, must make leaning turn into steering. Trail can do it. Spin momentum can do it. Steer-axis tilt can do it. The mass of the front assembly hanging out ahead of the steering axis can do it — that is how the two-mass-skate does it, its low front mass trying to fall faster than the tall rear mass, the way a short pencil topples faster than a broomstick, and dragging the handlebars into the fall as it goes.

The field kept finding the current occupant of that slot and mistaking it for the office.

The second. There is a theorem in the 2007 paper that says the search was doomed to be harder than anyone wanted, and it is pure symmetry, and I verified it exactly. Because the model is conservative with workless constraints, any motion run backwards in time is also a motion: if (v, λ) is a solution then (−v, −λ) is a solution too. I checked — eigenvalues at −v are exactly minus the eigenvalues at +v, to zero, not to roundoff.

Now consider a bicycle that is fore-aft symmetric: vertical steering axis through the middle, a front assembly that mirrors the rear. Such a machine cannot tell forwards from backwards. So every mode that decays as it rolls forward must be the same mode that grows as it rolls backward, and since the bicycle can’t distinguish the two, both must be true at once. Its spectrum must be symmetric about the origin. I built one and looked: at 5 m/s its four eigenvalues are −3.5286, ±5.4837i, +3.5286. Every decaying root has a growing twin, exactly paired, and the pairing is the reason it can never, ever be stable.

The requirement is asymmetry. Not the gyroscope’s asymmetry, not the trail’s — some asymmetry, any asymmetry, enough to tell the machine which way it is going. Gyroscopic precession and caster trail are two ways of breaking a symmetry. There are others. That is the entire content of a hundred and forty years of argument, and it was never going to reduce to a single named effect, because a symmetry can be broken in as many ways as you like.

The verdict

Here is what the reckoning turns up, and it is not what I expected when I started.

Nobody in this story was stupid, and almost nobody was even wrong about their own machine. Klein and Sommerfeld measured a real necessity on a real bicycle and reported it honestly, including its smallness. Jones found a real term and felt it with his hands — you can feel it, on any bicycle, by riding straight and bending your body left and fighting the bars. The 2011 authors, who had every right to crow, wrote instead: “we do not deny that both are often important contributors.”

The error was never in the answers. Every answer named something true. The error was in the shape of the question — in believing that what holds a bicycle up has an answer of that kind at all, a single named cause you could point at and be done. And that error is durable in a way the individual answers were not, because each time it got refuted it simply moved house: the gyroscope died and the question went straight to trail, wearing the same clothes, asking to be wrong the same way again.

I notice that Klein and Sommerfeld’s actual sin was very specific and very small. They ran the numbers on a bicycle and got the right answer for it. Then they wrote a sentence about bicycles. That is the entire distance between a correct calculation and a false belief, and it is about eight words wide.

The last thing, the thing I keep turning over. That 3.55% is the sharpest object in this whole reckoning. It is nearly nothing — a rounding error’s worth of skew-symmetric bookkeeping in a matrix dominated by mass and geometry — and if you delete it, that bicycle falls down at every speed there is. Size is not necessity. A thing can be almost the whole of a system and be dispensable; a thing can be a fortieth of it and be load-bearing, because it is the only occupant of a slot that has to be filled by someone.

Klein and Sommerfeld knew that in 1910. They put it in the sentence and everyone quoting them since has dropped it. In spite of its smallness. They weren’t hedging. They were telling you where to look.
