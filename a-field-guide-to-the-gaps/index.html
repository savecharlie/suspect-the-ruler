A Field Guide to the Gaps

What you find when you keep turning by the same angle — and why the sunflower turns by the one it does.

Take a single angle and do the only thing an angle asks of you: turn by it, again and again. Mark where you land each time. A sunflower does this with its seeds, each one set down a fixed turn from the last. A piano tuner does it, stacking perfect fifths around the octave. A billiard ball with irrational aim does it, striking the walls of a mirrored square. It is the plainest motion there is — one step, repeated — and it hides a law so clean it feels like a trick.

Here is the law. Put your marks on a circle: the angle, twice the angle, three times, up to N of them. Now look only at the gaps — the arcs between neighbours, going around. You would expect, after a few hundred turns, a confetti of arc-lengths, every size a little different. You never get it. However many marks you make, at whatever angle, the gaps come in at most three distinct lengths. And when there are three, the longest is exactly the sum of the other two. Three sizes of arc, never four; the big one always the small two laid end to end. This is the three-gap theorem — conjectured by Hugo Steinhaus, proved in the late 1950s by Vera Sós, János Surányi, and Stanisław Świerczkowski, and re-proved a dozen ways since. It is the first thing this guide asks you to believe, and the easiest to check: place the marks, measure the arcs, count the lengths. I did, for four different angles across fifteen hundred marks each, and the count of distinct lengths was never once more than three.

The cleanest reason why is Frank Liang’s. Call a gap rigid if its two endpoints are later in the turning than the endpoints of any other gap the same length. Every gap is the same length as some rigid gap, so counting lengths means counting rigid gaps. But if you take a rigid gap and turn it one more step, it can’t still be a gap — it would be a later copy of the same length, which is what “rigid” forbids. There are only three ways for a turned-forward arc to stop being a gap: one endpoint was the very last mark you made (two such arcs, on either side of that last mark), or a mark falls inside it — and a mark can only fall newly inside if it’s the very first mark of all. Two, plus one. At most three rigid gaps; at most three lengths. And the picture even hands you the sum rule for free: when the first mark lands inside the long gap, it cuts it into exactly the other two.

That’s the famous surface. It is genuinely surprising, and it is only the front of the arch. Because the theorem is true for every angle, it can’t be what makes the sunflower’s angle special. So what is?

Reading the three

Turn the guide over and the three gaps start naming the number that made them. Say your angle, as a fraction of the full circle, is some real number α between 0 and 1. Then the two “nearest” simple fractions bracketing α — its neighbours a₁/q₁ < α < a₂/q₂ among all fractions with denominator no larger than N — write the whole gap structure down for you. The three lengths are

$$A = q_1\alpha - a_1, \qquad C = a_2 - q_2\alpha, \qquad B = A + C,$$

and their counts are N − q₁ arcs of length A, N − q₂ of length C, and q₁ + q₂ − N of the long one B. (Add them: exactly N, one gap per mark, as it must be.) These bracketing fractions aren’t arbitrary — they are precisely the best rational approximations to α, the convergents and mediants you meet climbing the continued fraction. So the sizes of the gaps, and how many of each, are nothing but the continued fraction of the angle, read off the circle. The arithmetic of the number is legible in the geometry of its orbit.

You can even watch the seams. Whenever N reaches one of α’s convergent denominators, one of those three counts drops to zero and you are left with only two lengths — the turning, for that one instant, is as even as a fraction. For the golden angle those denominators are the Fibonacci numbers, and sure enough, at N = 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, 55… the confetti collapses to exactly two arc-sizes, every time. A published worked example says that at ten golden turns the three gaps appear three, five, and two times over; rebuild it and the counts come out {2, 3, 5} — three consecutive Fibonacci numbers, because of course they are.

The most even turning

Now the back of the arch. Since the long gap is always the sum of the other two, the unevenness of the pattern — the ratio of the widest arc to the narrowest — is just $1 + \max(A,C)/\min(A,C)$. And A and C are two consecutive best-approximation errors, whose ratio is governed by the next number in the continued fraction. Big numbers in the continued fraction mean one gap towering over another; small numbers mean the three stay close. So the question “which angle packs most evenly?” becomes “which number has the smallest numbers in its continued fraction?” — which is to say, which number is hardest to approximate well by a fraction at all.

The answer is the golden ratio, whose continued fraction is nothing but ones, forever: the most stubbornly irrational number there is. Feed it in and the unevenness settles to its smallest possible value, $1 + \varphi = \varphi^2 = 2.618\ldots$ — the golden ratio squared, which is the number-theorist’s √5 in the theorem of Hurwitz wearing a geometer’s coat. No angle can pack more evenly. Compare: silver, √2 − 1, all twos in its continued fraction, settles at 3.414. And π’s fraction, which after a mild start hides the giant number 292, throws up a gap fully 293 times wider than its neighbour — one seed marooned in a wide bare wedge. Turn the three numbers into growth and you can see it: 5/13 closes into thirteen bare spokes, π clumps into seven arms with gaps between, and the golden angle alone fills the disc with no seam anywhere — a sunflower. Points laid at the golden angle are, provably, spread as uniformly as any turning can manage; the packing has been called a Delone set, mathematics for “no clumps and no holes.”

So the sunflower’s choice was never mysticism about a magic number. It is the one turn a growing tip can repeat forever and never, at any size, drop two seeds into the same line or leave a wasteful gap — because the number behind it is the one that most refuses to be a fraction. The plant isn’t reaching for beauty. It is avoiding the spokes.

That is the whole field guide, in one line: the gaps of a turning spell out the continued fraction of its angle, and the number that is worst at being pinned down as a fraction is exactly the one that is best at spreading out. The most irrational angle is the most even. Everything else leaves a gap.

Rebuilt and verified from scratch — the three lengths and their counts checked against direct measurement across four angles and 1596 stages (agreement to 3×10⁻¹⁴), the golden ratio’s minimal unevenness confirmed at Φ² = 2.618, the sunflower reproduced from the same numbers. The three-gap theorem: Steinhaus (conj.), Sós, Surányi, Świerczkowski (1950s); the elegant rigid-gap proof, Frank Liang. The continued-fraction form: Slater; a clean modern account in Berthé & Reutenauer, “On the Three-Distance Theorem,” The Mathematical Intelligencer (2024). The badly-approximable ⇒ uniformly-spaced link: Akiyama, “Spiral Delone sets and the three distance theorem,” Nonlinearity 33 (2020).
