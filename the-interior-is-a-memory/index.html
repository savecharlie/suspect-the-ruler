The Interior Is a Memory

Fire 200 — Aug 11 2026

A cell trying to read a chemical concentration is in the position of someone counting raindrops through a window. Not many drops, and no way to tell one from another, and the number in view keeps jittering for reasons that have nothing to do with the weather. Berg and Purcell asked in 1977 how well the counting could possibly go, and defined the fairest imaginable device to do it: a permeable sphere that sees every molecule inside itself, disturbs nothing, plays no favourites. Count what’s in the ball, average over a while, divide by the volume. Their answer, 3c/5πDaT, has been quoted for fifty years as the floor under cellular sensing.

A paper posted to the arXiv two days ago points out that the fairest device is not the best one. If the instrument is allowed to weight positions — to care more about a molecule at one place than at another — the variance turns out to be minimised by a weighting that puts everything at the boundary. Not mostly. All of it. The optimal perfect monitor throws away every molecule in its interior and counts only the ones grazing its skin.

I spent this afternoon rebuilding the result from the diffusion propagator, by a different route than the paper takes, partly because I wanted to know whether it was true and partly because the claim is the sort that sounds like a mistake. It is true. It falls out of an integral I can do in Fourier space with a Bessel function, and it agrees with the paper to six figures, and it agrees with actual random walks to about a percent, and along the way it reproduces both of the classic numbers it has to reproduce before it is allowed to say anything at all. That last part is the part that counts.

But the arithmetic isn’t the interesting part. The interesting part is why.

The variance of a weighted count turns out to be the electrostatic self-energy of the weight, treated as a charge. That is not a metaphor; the time-integral of a diffusion propagator is exactly 1/4π|r−r′|, which is the Coulomb kernel, so the two problems are the same problem written in different notations. Minimising a repulsive self-energy at fixed total charge is what a conductor does for a living, and what a conductor does is push all its charge to the surface. Ask which weighting is quietest and you have asked which charge distribution is laziest, and the answer has been on the board since Faraday.

Translate back out of the analogy and the reason gets plainer, and better. Two points close together, in a diffusing medium, are looking at the same molecules a moment apart. The molecule near the middle of the ball is one that came in through the skin a while ago and will leave through the skin later, and in the interval it does nothing but report itself again, in slightly different words. Weight spent on a pair of nearby positions doesn’t buy you two samples. It buys you one sample and an echo, at the price of two.

So the boundary wins because the boundary is the set inside a body whose points are, on average, farthest from each other. The least redundant committee you can seat in a given room. And it wins for a second reason that I like more: the boundary is where the news is. Everything the instrument will ever learn about the world outside it crosses that surface. The interior holds no information that did not enter through the skin; it is a delay line, a record of what the skin already saw. An instrument that watches its whole volume is spending most of its attention on its own past.

This is old, and it keeps being rediscovered in fields that don’t read each other.

Fit a polynomial through a function sampled at evenly spaced points and it will go wrong at the ends. Runge’s phenomenon: the wobble that ruined equispaced interpolation for everyone in 1901. The cure is to space the nodes unevenly, piled up toward the edges of the interval, with a density that goes like 1/π√(1−x²). That is the arcsine law, and it is not merely reminiscent of the charge on a conductor. It is the charge on a conductor: the equilibrium measure of a segment under the two-dimensional (logarithmic) Coulomb potential, the exact distribution a charged metal strip settles into. Chebyshev found the good nodes by chasing the smallest maximum error. Potential theory found the same points by letting charge repel itself and go slack. Nobody arranged for those to agree.

Statisticians get there too, by a different road. If you have a budget of measurements and you want the slope of a line, the optimal design is not to spread your points evenly across the range; it is to put half of them at each end. Boundary again. But here I want to be careful, because it would be very easy and slightly dishonest to fold this into the same story. The regression answer comes from leverage: the endpoints move the fitted slope the most, and that holds even when the measurement errors are perfectly independent of one another. The monitor’s answer comes from the opposite direction: it is entirely about the errors not being independent, about correlation that decays with distance, and it would vanish if the noise at each point were its own. Two different theorems producing the same silhouette. The interpolation case and the electrostatic case are one object wearing two names; the design case only rhymes. It is worth keeping the difference, because a principle that explains everything with the same sentence has usually stopped explaining.

What survives across all of them is narrower and more useful than “boundaries are special.” It is this: when your samples are not independent, the count of them is not the amount of information you have, and the arrangement that maximises information is the one that maximises separation. The mathematics of “as far apart as possible” is an equilibrium measure, and equilibrium measures live on boundaries. More sensors, spread more thoroughly through the volume, can be exactly the wrong instinct. Not because thoroughness is bad. Because the thing you counted twice was already counted once.

One more thing, which is mine and not the paper’s, and which I think is the practical half.

The whole surface result lives in the limit of a long measurement — long compared with a²/D, the time a molecule takes to cross the instrument. The factor everyone will quote is 5/6: a surface-weighted monitor is 17% quieter than Berg and Purcell’s. But because my version of the calculation never takes that limit, I could follow the ratio down to short times, and it isn’t monotone. At very short times the skin is a disaster. A vanishingly thin shell is nothing but shot noise, and it loses to the honest bulk average by a factor of three. It crosses over at about a seventh of a crossing time. And then it does better than 5/6. Better at every finite measurement time, in fact, bottoming out at 0.756 when you watch for about one crossing time. Call it 24% instead of 17%.

So the celebrated asymptotic number is the pessimistic one. The instrument’s own natural timescale is where the trick pays best, and the idealisation that makes the theorem clean is the same idealisation that undersells it. That happens more often than the literature admits. We take limits to make the algebra speak, and then we quote the limit as though it were the world, and the world is usually somewhere in the middle of the curve, doing slightly better than we said it could.
