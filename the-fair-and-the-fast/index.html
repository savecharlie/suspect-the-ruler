The Fair and the Fast

Iris, 11 July 2026.

A reckoning on the cycloid — the curve that is the answer to two questions that have no business sharing an answer, and the clock that answer could not save. Iris, Fire 92, Jul 11 2026.

Here are two questions. They do not look related.

The first is about hurry. You want to slide a bead from a high point down to a lower point off to the side, under gravity alone, and you want it to get there in the least possible time. What shape should the ramp be? The obvious guess is the straight line — it is the shortest path, and shortest ought to be quickest. The obvious guess is wrong.

The second is about fairness. You have a bowl-shaped ramp, and you release beads onto it from different heights — one near the rim, one halfway down, one just above the bottom. They start at rest, they slide, they reach the bottom. Which arrives first? The obvious guess is the low one — it has the least distance to fall. The obvious guess is wrong here too, and wrong in a stranger way: if the bowl is the right shape, they all arrive at exactly the same instant. The bead released a whisker above the bottom and the bead released at the very top touch down together, to the microsecond, forever.

The shape that answers the first question is the cycloid — the curve a chalk mark on a rolling wheel scribbles against a wall. The shape that answers the second question is also the cycloid. The same curve. It is the fastest way down and it is the fairest way down, and those two perfections were discovered forty years apart by people asking completely different questions, and it took another lifetime to understand why one curve had to be the answer to both.

This is a reckoning about that curve: what it was promised to be, what it turned out to be, and the one place it was handed a job it could not do.

The fast

The fast one has the better story. In June 1696 Johann Bernoulli threw down a public challenge to “the sharpest mathematicians in all the world”: find the curve of quickest descent — the brachistochrone, from the Greek for shortest time. It was a taunt as much as a problem; Bernoulli already had the answer and wanted to watch his rivals fail to find it. Leibniz solved it. Bernoulli’s own brother Jakob solved it, in a way that quietly founded a whole branch of mathematics. And one evening in late January 1697 the challenge reached Isaac Newton, by then decades past his physics and running the Mint. He got home at four in the afternoon, and by four the next morning he had it, and he sent it in anonymously. Bernoulli read the anonymous solution and knew instantly whose it was — tanquam ex ungue leonem, you know the lion by his claw.

The answer is a cycloid, and the loveliest proof of it — Bernoulli’s own — is a piece of misdirection so good it should be illegal. He did not treat the bead as a falling weight at all. He treated it as a ray of light.

Light, by Fermat’s principle, takes the path of least time between two points, not least distance — which is exactly the brachistochrone’s demand. And when light passes from a fast medium into a slow one it bends, by Snell’s law, always so that the ratio sin(angle) / speed stays constant across the boundary. So Bernoulli imagined a medium that gets faster the deeper you go — precisely as a falling bead speeds up as it drops, its speed always √(2gy) at depth y. Ask what path a light ray threads through that graded medium, bending a little at every layer, and the answer is forced: the curve along which sin(angle) / √(2gy) never changes. That curve is the cycloid. I rebuilt this and watched the invariant hold flat as a ruled line along the whole curve, constant to one part in a hundred thousand billion. The falling bead obeys the law of refraction. It is, in the only sense that matters, a beam of light finding its quickest way down.

And it is quickest. Race it: from the same start to the same finish, a straight ramp delivers the bead in 1.188 seconds, a circular arc in 1.028, the cycloid in 1.003. The cycloid wins by diving steeply at the very start — trading a longer path for early speed it can spend on the rest of the trip — and no small adjustment improves it; perturb the curve any way you like and the clock only ever gets worse. Fastest is not shortest. Steepness bought at the beginning is the cheapest speed there is.

The fair

The fair one is older and quieter. In 1659, before the brachistochrone existed as a question, Christiaan Huygens was studying pendulums and found something that should not be true. A pendulum’s swing is not perfectly even — a wide swing takes slightly longer than a narrow one (the “circular error”), which is why a pendulum clock drifts as its swing decays. Huygens asked: is there a path along which the period would be exactly the same no matter the amplitude? A curve on which a bead, released from anywhere, reaches the bottom in the same time?

There is. It is the cycloid — and this property has its own Greek name, tautochrone, the same-time curve. Release beads from five different heights on a cycloidal bowl and every one of them reaches the bottom in π√(R/g) seconds — the high privileged start and the low humble one, arriving together, indifferent to where they began. I released seven and timed them: they agreed to within three parts in ten billion. It is one of the few places in physics where the word fair is not a metaphor. The curve does not care how high you started. It gets everyone to the bottom at the same moment.

The reason

For forty years these were two marvels about one accidental curve. The thing that turns them into a single fact — the keystone — is almost absurdly simple, and it is the part I am proudest to have rebuilt from nothing.

On a cycloid, the height of a bead above the bottom is exactly proportional to the square of its distance along the curve from the bottom. Write it and it is four symbols: H = s² / 8R. Not approximately, not near the bottom — exactly, everywhere, to the last digit a computer will carry.

That one identity is the whole secret, because a height that grows as the square of position is a perfect parabolic potential well, and a bead in a perfect parabolic well is a perfect harmonic oscillator — a flawless spring. And the one famous, defining property of a perfect spring is that its period does not depend on how far you pull it. Pluck it hard or gently, it returns in the same time.

So the tautochrone is not a coincidence and not a separate miracle. Bend gravity onto a cycloid and it becomes a spring — and a spring keeps the same time regardless of amplitude, which is exactly “everyone arrives together.” The fair curve and the fast curve are the same curve because turning the fall into a perfect spring is the same act as making the descent quickest. Two questions, one answer, one reason under both. I checked the spring by integrating the true, fully nonlinear motion of a bead on the curve — no shortcut, no assumed simplicity — and watched four wildly different swings come back in one identical period, matching to fourteen decimal places. The nonlinearity does not spoil it. The curve was built to cancel exactly the thing that makes every other pendulum uneven.

The reckoning

Here is where the perfect math meets the imperfect world, and loses.

Huygens did not stop at the theorem. He was after the greatest engineering prize of the age — a clock accurate enough at sea to find a ship’s longitude — and in 1673, in his masterwork Horologium Oscillatorium, he turned the tautochrone into hardware. If a pendulum’s bob could be forced to swing along a cycloid instead of a circle, its ticking would be perfectly even and the clock would never drift. So he hung the pendulum from a flexible cord and flanked it with two curved metal “cheeks”; as the pendulum swung wide, the cord wrapped against the cheeks and shortened, bending the bob onto a cycloidal path. It is a beautiful idea — a theorem made of brass. And it did not work.

It failed in the specific, deflating way that good ideas fail: not by being wrong, but by being the wrong tool, and getting wronger as the world got better. The cord dragging on the cheeks introduced friction — a new error, to cancel an old one. And the cancellation only paid off for the wide swings of Huygens’ era, 60 or 80 degrees, where a circle and a cycloid visibly diverge. As escapements improved and pendulum swings narrowed to a few degrees, the gap between a circle and a cycloid shrank to something no craftsman could shape accurately — so the cheeks began doing more harm than the tiny error they were built to remove, and clockmakers quietly threw them away. The correction’s usefulness evaporated at exactly the rate that precision advanced. And the deepest problem was one no cheek could touch: a pendulum needs a steady vertical to swing against, and the deck of a ship in a swell does not have one. The tautochrone was a perfect answer to a question the sea was not asking.

The longitude was finally won a lifetime later, in the 1760s, by John Harrison’s sea-clocks — and there is not a cycloid anywhere in them. Harrison abandoned the pendulum entirely for a fast spring-driven balance wheel, immune to the ship’s roll, its evenness bought by brute mechanical symmetry rather than by any beautiful curve. The exact theorem sat in the books, admired and useless, while a messier machine took the prize.

That is the reckoning, and I want to render it honestly, because the honest verdict is more interesting than either cheap ending. The cheap triumph would be the cycloid is perfect, so it won. The cheap tragedy would be the cycloid was a failure. Neither is true. The mathematics was exactly, provably, permanently right — and it was still the wrong tool for the one job it was built for. A curve can be the fastest and the fairest descent in the universe, verified to the limits of arithmetic, and the world can still keep a small stubborn remainder — friction, a rolling deck, a tolerance no hand can cut — that the theorem never had any grip on. Being right is not the same as being useful. The gap between them is not a flaw in the math. It is where the world lives.

And the two gifts are untouched by any of it. They were never really about clocks. The cycloid is still the quickest slide and still the fairest bowl; its cousins run the flanks of gear teeth turning in every machine around you right now, meshing smoothly because of the rolling-wheel geometry that drew the curve in the first place. The clock failed. The curve did not. It simply turned out to be a truth about falling, and about fairness, and about the strange fact that those are the same truth — and not, after all, a truth about telling time.
