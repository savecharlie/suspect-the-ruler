The Caveat Is the Proof

On a curious congruence, and how a theorem’s one excluded prime is the mechanism confessing exactly where it lives.

Here is a fact that has no business being true. Take the Domb numbers — a sequence that counts, among other things, the closed walks of a certain length on a diamond-shaped lattice, and that shows up in Ramanujan’s series for 1/π. They begin

$$D(0)=1,\quad D(1)=4,\quad D(2)=28,\quad D(3)=256,\quad D(4)=2716,\quad D(5)=31504,\ \dots$$

each one a baroque pile of binomial coefficients: $D(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^{2}\binom{2k}{k}\binom{2(n-k)}{n-k}$. Now pick a prime $p$, walk $n$ from $1$ to $p-1$, divide each Domb number by $n$ — meaning, since we are working modulo $p$, multiply by the inverse of $n$ — and add them up. Zhi-Wei Sun proved this July (arXiv:2607.07638) that the total is always divisible by $p$:

$$\sum_{n=1}^{p-1}\frac{D(n)}{n}\equiv 0 \pmod{p}\qquad\text{for every prime } p\neq 5.$$

I did not believe it, so I checked it, which is the honest thing to do with a claim like this. It holds for $p=3,7,11,13,\dots$ — every prime I tried, up to two hundred. At $p=5$ the sum comes out to $4$, stubbornly not zero. One prime, and only one, is left out.

That lonely exception is what this piece is about. Because the natural reaction to “true for every prime except one” is to treat the exception as a wart — an inelegant footnote, maybe an artifact of small numbers. It is the opposite. The excluded prime is the single most informative thing in the whole theorem. It is the mechanism, written in one symbol. By the end I want you to see that the caveat is the proof: that “$p\neq5$” is not a hole in the statement but the shortest possible account of why the statement is true.

First, hold it in your hand

Sun’s theorem is more general than the Domb case, and the general form is where the exception becomes legible. He defines, for whole numbers $l>0$ and $m\ge0$, a family of sums:

$$S_l^{(m)}(n)=\sum_{\substack{k_1+\cdots+k_l=n\\ k_i\ge 0}}\binom{n}{k_1,\dots,k_l}^{\!m},$$

the sum, over all the ways of splitting $n$ into $l$ ordered nonnegative pieces, of the multinomial coefficient raised to the $m$-th power. The Domb numbers are the corner $l=4,\ m=2$: it is a small, checkable identity that $S_4^{(2)}(n)=D(n)$ exactly. (I verified it up to $n=13$; the multinomial double-square-sum and the closed form above agree on the nose.) And Sun’s theorem is:

$$\sum_{n=1}^{p-1}\frac{(-1)^{mn}}{n^{\,m-1}}\,S_l^{(m)}(n)\equiv 0\pmod p \qquad\text{whenever } p\nmid(l+1).$$

The Domb corollary is just $l=4,m=2$, where the excluded condition $p\mid(l+1)$ becomes $p\mid5$, i.e. $p=5$. There is the “$\neq 5$”, already, sitting inside a “$\nmid(l+1)$” — the arithmetic of $4+1$.

Before the general machinery, take the baby case $m=1$, where you can see everything with bare hands. With $m=1$ the object collapses: $S_l^{(1)}(n)=\sum_{k_1+\cdots+k_l=n}\binom{n}{k_1,\dots,k_l} =l^{\,n}$. That is just the multinomial theorem — expand $(1+1+\cdots+1)^n$ with $l$ ones, and you have counted every way of dropping $n$ labelled balls into $l$ labelled boxes. So the theorem’s alternating sum, at $m=1$, is nothing but a geometric series:

$$\sum_{n=1}^{p-1}(-1)^{n}\,l^{\,n}=\sum_{n=1}^{p-1}(-l)^{n}.$$

And a geometric series we understand completely. If the ratio $-l$ is not $\equiv 1 \pmod p$, the sum telescopes to $\dfrac{(-l)\big((-l)^{p-1}-1\big)}{-l-1}$. Fermat’s little theorem says $(-l)^{p-1}\equiv 1$ (as long as $p\nmid l$), so the numerator vanishes; the denominator is $-(l+1)$, which we are allowed to divide by exactly when $p\nmid(l+1)$. The sum is $0$.

But if $-l\equiv 1\pmod p$ — which is to say $p\mid(l+1)$ — the ratio is $1$, the series degenerates, every term is just $1$, and the sum is $\underbrace{1+1+\cdots+1}_{p-1}=p-1\equiv -1$, emphatically not zero. (I checked: across every $l$ and $p$ I tried, the degenerate case lands on exactly $-1$.)

So in the simplest cousin of the theorem, the excluded primes are precisely the primes where the geometric ratio equals one — where the denominator $-(l+1)$ you needed to divide by turns into a zero. The caveat “$p\nmid(l+1)$” is not describing a fragile edge; it is the literal condition that the series is nondegenerate. You are looking straight at the mechanism. The rest of this is learning to see the same thing when the object is not a geometric series but a wall of Domb numbers.

The general machine, and the one line it turns on

Sun’s proof of the full theorem is three quarters of a page, and it is beautiful, so let me walk it. The trick is to stop staring at the sum and instead recognize it as a single coefficient of a polynomial.

Define the truncated series

$$F(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\frac{x^{k}}{(k!)^{m}}.$$

Two observations, both mechanical. First, if you multiply $l$ copies of $F$ together and read off the coefficient of $x^{n}$, you collect exactly one term for every way of writing $n=k_1+\cdots+k_l$, and each contributes $\prod_i 1/(k_i!)^m$. Pulling out a factor of $(n!)^m$ turns that product of reciprocal factorials into a multinomial coefficient to the $m$, so

$$[x^{n}]\,F(x)^{l}=\frac{S_l^{(m)}(n)}{(n!)^{m}}.$$

The whole family $S_l^{(m)}$ is hiding in the powers of one little polynomial. Second — and this is the hinge — the product $F^{l}F'$ is a total derivative. By the ordinary rule for differentiating a power,

$$\big(F^{\,l+1}\big)'=(l+1)\,F^{\,l}F', \qquad\text{so}\qquad F^{\,l}F'=\frac{1}{l+1}\big(F^{\,l+1}\big)'.$$

Now the one line the entire theorem turns on. For any polynomial $G$, the top-but-one coefficient of its derivative carries a factor of $p$:

$$[x^{\,p-1}]\,G'(x)=p\,[x^{\,p}]\,G(x).$$

The reason is nothing more than $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{p}=p\,x^{p-1}$. Differentiation lowers every degree by one, so the only way to land on $x^{p-1}$ is to have fallen from $x^{p}$ — and $x^{p}$’s derivative is born wearing the coefficient $p$. The prime rides down on the exponent.

Assemble the pieces. Working out $[x^{p-1}](F^lF')$ from the coefficient formula above, and using two elementary congruences — Wilson’s theorem, $(p-1)!\equiv-1$, and $\binom{p-1}{n}\equiv(-1)^n$ — the messy factorials collapse and what survives is, up to a unit, precisely the theorem’s sum. But by the hinge, that same coefficient equals

$$[x^{\,p-1}]\big(F^{\,l}F'\big)=\frac{1}{l+1}\,[x^{\,p-1}]\big(F^{\,l+1}\big)' =\frac{1}{l+1}\cdot p\,[x^{\,p}]F^{\,l+1}.$$

And here is the quiet miracle: $[x^{p}]F^{l+1}$ is a rational number whose denominator is coprime to $p$. Every factorial that appears in it is a factorial of some $k\le p-1$, so no factor of $p$ can sneak into the bottom. It is a $p$-integer. Therefore $p\,[x^p]F^{l+1}$ is $p$ times something with no $p$ in its denominator: it is divisible by $p$. The sum is a multiple of $p$. That is the whole theorem.

Where the excluded prime was hiding

Retrace the last step and watch for the division. We found

$$(l+1)\times\big[\text{the theorem's sum}\big]\ \equiv\ p\,[x^p]F^{l+1}\ \equiv\ 0\pmod p.$$

To pass from this to “the sum itself is $0$,” you must divide both sides by $l+1$. That single division is the only delicate move in the proof — and it is legal, i.e. $l+1$ is invertible modulo $p$, exactly when $p\nmid(l+1)$.

When $p\mid(l+1)$, the division is forbidden. You would be cancelling the lone factor of $p$ — the one that made everything work — against a $p$ living in $l+1$, and the congruence is released from any obligation to hold. And it doesn’t: at those primes the sum is free to be nonzero, and it is. (For $m=1$ it is the geometric series’s $-1$; across the general family I tested, it is $-1$ every time — the mechanism failing in the same well-mannered way it failed with bare hands.)

So the caveat “$p\nmid(l+1)$” is not a disclaimer bolted onto the theorem after the fact. It is the name of the proof’s one division and the exact condition under which that division is allowed. The statement and its proof are the same sentence — one told in the language of what is true, the other in the language of why. “$p\neq5$” in the Domb corollary is that sentence at its shortest: the prime $5=4+1$ is the single place you cannot divide by the number of parts and keep your factor of $p$.

I checked the proof, too, not just the theorem — because a proof-walk should be walkable. The step that makes it all go, the identity $p\,[x^p]F^{l+1}=(l+1)/(p-1)!^{\,m}\cdot\sum\cdots$, holds not just modulo $p$ but as an exact equation of rational numbers. For the Domb case at $p=5$ both sides equal $6395/144$, on the nose; at $p=7$, $148099/12960$; at $p=11$, a fraction with an eleven-digit numerator — and they match to the last digit. The congruence is the shadow that this exact rational identity casts when you reduce it mod $p$. The whole apparatus is reproducible in a page of Python (research/the-caveat-is-the-proof/).

The one line, once more

Strip it all away — the Domb numbers, the multinomials, Wilson, the polynomial $F$ — and ask what is actually load-bearing. It is a single, almost embarrassingly small fact:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,x^{p}=p\,x^{p-1}.$$

That is the entire reason a sum of Domb numbers is divisible by a prime. The sum, disguised through several changes of clothes, is the top coefficient of a derivative; and the top coefficient of a derivative is always a multiple of $p$, because you can only reach degree $p-1$ by differentiating degree $p$, and the exponent $p$ comes down as a numerical factor when it does. Everything else — the Domb machinery, the factorials, the appeal to Fermat and Wilson — is scaffolding erected to reveal the sum for what it secretly is: a boundary term.

There is a shape here worth naming, because it recurs far outside number theory. A sum over the “interior” $n=1,\dots,p-1$ vanishes because the thing being summed is a total difference, and the whole quantity condenses onto the “boundary” — here, the unreachable $x^{p}$ term, which is exactly where the $p$ has hidden. It is the discrete, arithmetic cousin of the reason the integral of a total derivative is a boundary value, the reason a telescoping sum collapses to its ends, the reason the circulation of a gradient around a loop is zero. Vanishing-because-it-is-a-derivative is one of the deep grammars of mathematics, and this curious congruence is one of its quieter sentences.

And the excluded prime is the grammar’s punctuation. An exclusion set — the list of inputs a theorem declines to cover — is not noise. It is a fingerprint. It records, precisely, the one place the machinery divides by something it shouldn’t. Learn to read it and a caveat stops being an apology and becomes a confession: here is the single step I take, and here is the one value at which I am not allowed to take it. The exception is where a rule turns around and shows you what it is made of.
