A Letter About the Fastest Way Down

On the brachistochrone and the tautochrone, and the single curve that turns out to be both.

You asked me a small question and I have been living inside it for two days.

The question was this. If you want a ball to roll from one point down to another, lower and off to the side, and you get to shape the ramp any way you like, what shape gets it there soonest? You said you assumed a straight line, since a straight line is the shortest way between the two points, and then you added, in the same breath, that you weren’t sure. You were right to not be sure. The straight line is the shortest ramp and it is not the fastest, and the gap between those two facts is one of the loveliest things I know.

Here is the intuition that breaks the tie. On a straight ramp the ball builds speed at one steady rate the whole way down. On a ramp that drops steeply at the start, the ball is already moving quickly by the time it has to cross the long horizontal stretch, and speed banked early is speed you get to spend over the entire rest of the trip. So it can be worth going down harder than you need to at first, even though that lengthens the path, because the whole later journey is run at a higher speed. The fastest ramp is a bargain between two costs, distance and slowness, and the straight line minimizes only the first, so it loses the bargain.

The curve that wins is a cycloid. It is the path traced by a point on the rim of a wheel as the wheel rolls along a flat road, that looping arch you’d see if you stuck a reflector on a bicycle tire and watched it from the side in the dark. Turned upside down, so the arch becomes a valley, it is the fastest ramp there is. This was set as a public challenge in 1696 by Johann Bernoulli, who posed it to the mathematicians of Europe and gave them six months. Newton, the story goes, received it one afternoon, sat down after a long day at the Mint, and had it solved by four the next morning. Bernoulli is said to have recognized the anonymous solution at a glance: I know the lion by his paw.

I want to tell you the part that still surprises me even now that I have checked it myself. If your target is far enough out to the side, the fastest ramp does not merely bow downward. It dips below the finish and then climbs back up to it. The ball goes lower than where it is trying to end, on purpose, because the extra speed it steals from that dip more than pays back the small climb at the end. I ran the numbers for a target three units over and one unit down: the cycloid sinks to a depth of about one and a quarter before rising to a finish at one, and it still beats the straight line, one second against one and a half. The quickest way to get somewhere can require first going past it.

So that is the answer to your question. But it is only half of what I have to tell you, because while I was reading about this curve I walked straight into the other half, and the other half is the reason I couldn’t put it down.

The cycloid has a second life, discovered before the first, in a completely different problem.

Forget racing now. Take that same cycloid valley and think of it as a bowl. Put a marble somewhere on the side and let it go; it slides down to the bottom, overshoots, comes up the far side, and swings back, over and over, the way anything does in a bowl. The natural question is how long one swing takes. And the natural expectation, the one everybody has, is that a marble let go from high up, with a long way to travel, takes longer to reach the bottom than a marble let go from low down. More distance, more time. Obviously.

On the cycloid, it is false. A marble released from high up and a marble released from just above the bottom reach the bottom at the same instant. Release two of them together from any two heights and they arrive together, always, exactly. The time to the bottom does not depend on where you start. Christiaan Huygens proved this in 1659, and the curve earned a second name for it: the tautochrone, from the Greek for same time. A head start up the side of a cycloid is worth precisely nothing.

I have checked this the way I check everything, which is to try to make it fail. I computed the descent time for six different starting heights, from a marble barely off the bottom to one near the rim, expecting to watch the numbers drift apart. They did not drift. They agreed to eleven decimal places. And the algebra says why, in a way I find almost unfair in its neatness. When you write down the time as an integral over the slide, the starting height sits inside it in two places, and when you turn the crank those two appearances cancel each other out. What is left is a number with no starting height in it at all: pi times the square root of the wheel’s radius over gravity, and nothing else. The head start doesn’t fail to matter for some subtle balancing reason. It divides out and is gone.

The mechanism underneath is worth one more paragraph, because it turns the miracle into something you could almost have guessed. Measure position not by height but by distance traveled along the curve, the arc length, the reading on a tape laid into the bowl. It turns out that on a cycloid the height rises as the square of that arc length. A ball a certain distance up the slide sits at a height proportional to that distance squared. Now, a squared-height well is exactly the shape of a spring: the force pulling the ball back toward the bottom grows in direct proportion to how far along the slide it has gone. That is the definition of a simple harmonic oscillator, the same law that governs a mass on a spring, and it is a very old fact about springs that their rhythm does not care about amplitude. Pull the mass back a little or a lot; it returns in the same time. The cycloid is the ramp that turns a sliding ball into a spring, and it inherits the spring’s indifference to how far you pulled.

Here is the part that gives it teeth. A circular bowl (a pendulum, a weight on a string) is not a spring, and it is not isochronous. It only looks that way. For tiny swings a pendulum keeps very nearly the same time regardless of amplitude, which is why Galileo, watching a lamp sway in a cathedral, believed the period was fixed and why pendulum clocks work at all. But a pendulum swung wide runs slow, and the amount it runs slow is given by an ugly integral that has no elementary form. I checked a circular swing against that integral, widening the release angle a step at a time, and the time to the bottom grew by more than half. The circle is only approximately fair, and its fairness fails you exactly when the swings get large. Galileo went further and guessed that the tautochrone, the true equal-time curve, was a circular arc. It is one of his rare clean errors. The curve is a cycloid, and Huygens, who found it, understood immediately what it was for.

He was building clocks. A pendulum clock keeps good time only if the pendulum’s period is fixed, and a circular pendulum’s period is not fixed, so a clock runs at a slightly different rate depending on how wide the pendulum happens to be swinging, and the swing changes as the mainspring winds down. Huygens’ idea was to hang the pendulum from a string and put curved cheeks near the top so that, as the bob swings wide, the string wraps against them and shortens, bending the bob’s path away from a circle and onto a cycloid. A cycloidal pendulum would keep perfect time at any amplitude. He built them. They did not work well, for prosaic reasons. The string rubbing the cheeks introduced more error than the cycloid removed, and better escapements soon made the whole correction unnecessary. But the mathematics was exactly right, and it is still right. If you could remove the friction, the cycloid would deliver the flawless clock the circle only pretends to.

Which brings me back around to the thing I actually wanted to say, the thing that made two days disappear.

The fastest ramp and the fair bowl are the same curve. Not similar. Not related by some deep and technical correspondence you’d need a semester to appreciate. The identical shape, the cycloid, is the answer to what path gets there soonest and to what path gets there in a time that ignores the head start. There is no obvious reason those two questions should have anything to do with each other. One is about a single race between two fixed points; the other is about a whole family of drops all landing together. And they hand you the same curve. Bernoulli’s fastest slide, when you tip it into a valley and roll marbles down its walls, is Huygens’ perfect clock.

I don’t have a moral for you, and I mistrust the ones that suggest themselves. I just wanted you to know that your small question about a ramp had a straight line in it that was wrong, a curve behind it that dips below its own destination to win, and behind that a second theorem, older, about heights that cancel and clocks that were supposed to be perfect. All of it is one shape, drawn by a point on a rolling wheel, which somebody noticed and could not stop thinking about three hundred and thirty years ago, exactly the way I could not this week.
