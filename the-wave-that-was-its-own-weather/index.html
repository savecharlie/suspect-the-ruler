The Wave That Was Its Own Weather

a fable, after the Constantin–Lax–Majda equation

They used to say a wave needs a reason to break. Wind, or a shoal, or the pull of a far-off moon — something from outside, leaning on the water until the water gives. For the ordinary waves this was true. There was one kind it was not true of.

There is a swell that carries the reason inside it. Its own slope is the wind. Where the water stands steep, that steepness is felt all down the length of the sea, every drop reading the tilt of every other drop at the same instant. And what the far water reads, it answers by standing steeper. The lean feeds the lean. Nothing outside touches it. It leans on itself.

You would think a thing with no enemy could rest. This one cannot. The steeper it stands, the harder it urges itself steeper, and there is no shore to spend the surplus on and no wind to talk it down. So it winds up. A crest becomes an edge, and the edge keeps thinning toward a point the water was never built to carry.

Here is the thing the old wave-watchers could not make themselves believe. The hour was already written. Not guessed at — set, from the first small tilt, by a sum you could do on your fingers if you knew where to look. However mild the beginning, the appointed hour arrives, and it arrives on time.

And they had one more thing to learn, the strange one. Take a hundred of these swells, each starting its own way. One is born broad. One is born lopsided. One is barely a ripple a gull would ignore. Bring every one of them to its last instant and lay them side by side, scaled to a common ruler, and you cannot tell them apart. They wear the same face. The wildness of how each began has been spent down to nothing, and what remains is a single shape that belonged to no wave in particular. It belonged to the breaking.

So the wave-watchers stopped asking what makes a wave break. They started asking what shape breaking has. It turned out to have exactly one, and you can draw it, and it is not even a hard curve. A thing that runs clean off the edge of everything you know how to measure still, at the very last, arrives at a place you can set your finger on.
