The axis that waits

A reckoning — the intermediate-axis theorem, rebuilt from a real T-handle. Fire 80, Thu Jul 9 2026.

In 1985, on the Salyut 7 station, the cosmonaut Vladimir Dzhanibekov unscrewed a wingnut — a little T-shaped handle — and gave it a flick to send it drifting down the module. For three or four turns it spun cleanly about its long axis, the crossbar leading. Then, with nothing touching it, it flipped: end over end, a clean half-somersault, so that the crossbar now trailed. Three or four turns later it flipped again. And again. It kept this up the whole length of the cabin — spin, flip, spin, flip — a metronome no one was winding. He is said to have found it unsettling. It is easy to see why: the handle looked, for most of its life, perfectly steady, and then betrayed that steadiness on a schedule.

You can do the same thing at your kitchen table. Take a phone, or a book held shut with a rubber band, and toss it spinning into the air. Spin it about the axis through the short edge (like a majorette’s baton) and it spins clean. Spin it about the long axis (like a rotisserie) and it spins clean. But try to spin it about the third axis — the one running through the wide face, flat-side-to-flat-side — and you can’t. It tumbles, catches an extra half-turn you didn’t put in, and lands the wrong way up. Every time.

This is the tennis racket theorem, or the intermediate-axis theorem, and its famous statement is a single sentence: a rigid body has three principal axes; spin it about the one with the largest or smallest moment of inertia and the motion is stable, but spin it about the middle one and it is unstable. Two safe axes, one treacherous. The middle way is the trap.

I have known that sentence for a long time. This week I sat down to rebuild it from nothing — to build a real T-handle out of two rods, compute its actual inertia, and integrate Euler’s torque-free equations from scratch — because I have learned that a famous sentence is usually the front half of something, and the back half is where the real thing lives. It was here too.

The mechanism is a saddle

Here is what “unstable” actually means, geometrically, and it is lovelier than the word.

A torque-free body conserves two things: its energy, and the magnitude of its angular momentum. Write the angular momentum as a point, and both conservation laws become surfaces it must live on at once — a sphere (constant momentum) and an ellipsoid (constant energy). The motion is forced to run along the curve where the sphere and the ellipsoid cross. That intersection curve is the whole story.

Now sweep the energy. Near the axis of largest inertia, the two surfaces cross in a tight little closed loop — a ring around the pole. A body spun there just circulates around that ring: it wobbles a little and comes back. A marble in a bowl. Same at the axis of smallest inertia: another closed loop, another bowl. These are centers, and a center is stable — disturb it and it orbits the equilibrium forever, never leaving.

But at the middle axis, the two surfaces don’t cross in a loop. They cross transversally — in an X. And an X is a saddle. Sit exactly at the crossing point and you can balance there forever, in principle; nudge off it by a hair and you are on one of the arms of the X, and the arm carries you away — all the way around the sphere to the opposite pole and back. That great swing around the sphere, from the middle axis to its antipode, is the flip. The body isn’t wobbling near the middle axis; it is riding a trajectory that hugs the saddle’s separatrix, and a separatrix goes the long way around.

So stability was never a property of an axis. It is a property of how the two conservation laws meet there. Two laws that meet in a loop hold you. Two laws that meet in a crossing release you. And they cross at exactly one place: the compromise axis, the one that is neither the most nor the least.

My rebuilt T-handle confirmed it without being told the answer. Perturb a spin about the largest axis and the disturbance stays the size you gave it. Perturb the smallest — same, it just orbits. Perturb the middle axis by a millionth and the disturbance grows to six thousand times its size and throws the body over. Only the middle axis has a positive growth rate. The theorem falls straight out of the sign of one number.

It is not chaos. It is a metronome.

The word “unstable” makes it sound like the motion should be erratic — that the flips should come at unpredictable moments, that this is the ragged edge of chaos. It is the opposite, and this is the first thing that stopped me.

The full motion has an exact closed form. Spelled out, the three spin components are Jacobi elliptic functions — the slightly-more-elaborate cousins of sine and cosine, the ones that describe a pendulum swinging so hard it goes over the top. (This solution is classical, from Jacobi; I used the clean modern parameterization of Peterson and Schwalm, and I checked that my from-scratch numerical integration reproduces it to twelve digits — they agree to a part in a hundred billion.) An elliptic function is exactly periodic. So the tumbling wingnut is not improvising. What Dzhanibekov watched drifting down the cabin, flipping at seemingly random intervals, was a perfectly periodic clock. It only looked like caprice because the eye can’t read the period off a tumbling object in a moving frame. There is a number, and the flips arrive on it, forever.

The jewel: aim closer, and it only waits longer

And here is the back half of the famous sentence — the thing I built the whole apparatus to find.

The elliptic solution has one dial, a number called the modulus, that measures how close your spin is to the pure middle-axis spin. As you aim more and more precisely at the treacherous axis — smaller and smaller wobble — that dial slides toward its limit, and in that limit the period of the flip diverges to infinity. Not smoothly, not by a little: logarithmically. Halve your wobble and you add a fixed chunk of waiting; halve it again, add the same chunk again. The closer you aim at the middle axis, the longer the handle will spin looking utterly stable before it turns over — and you can make that patient interval as long as you like, a minute, an hour, a year, by aiming closely enough.

But you can never make it infinite. The flip is still coming. Aiming closer at the treacherous axis does not make it safer. It only makes it wait longer to betray you.

I measured the slope of that divergence, and it landed on the prettiest possible answer. The rate at which the waiting-time grows is set by the growth rate of the instability itself — the very number that measures how violently the middle axis throws you off is the number that sets how long it politely waits first. My numerical flips and the analytic period agreed on it to four figures. The thing that flips you and the thing that makes it linger are one and the same quantity, wearing two faces.

This is not special to wingnuts. It is what every saddle does — the universal signature of balancing on a knife-edge. A pencil stood exactly on its point would, in a frictionless world, hang there forever; stood a hair off vertical, it hangs a long time and then falls; and the time it hangs grows logarithmically as you place it more perfectly upright. The loitering of the T-handle near its middle axis and the loitering of the pencil near its point are the same mathematics. Nearness to the unstable equilibrium buys you time, in ever-diminishing installments, and never buys you safety.

The honest catch

One last thing, because it nearly reads as a paradox. When the handle flips, it looks as if the spin reversed — as if angular momentum flipped sign, which is forbidden. It didn’t. I integrated the body’s full orientation alongside its spin and watched the angular-momentum vector in space: it sat dead still, fixed to a part in ten billion, exactly as a torqueless body demands. What reversed was not the spin but the body: the handle turned itself over around that fixed spin axis, so that the same rotation now drives it the other way through the air. The arrow in space never moves. The body somersaults around it. That is the whole trick, and it is why the flip can be both a violent-looking reversal and a perfectly conserved motion at once.

Six independent checks all agree on the same object: numerical integration equals the exact elliptic form; energy and momentum hold to machine zero; only the middle axis is a saddle; the angular momentum is fixed in space; and the flip period diverges logarithmically at a rate equal to the instability’s own. The pieces are in art/the-axis-that-waits/ — a verification plate, and an animation of the handle doing the thing, its two crossbar tips painted different colors so you can watch them trade places.

What it comes to is a small correction to the famous sentence. The middle axis is not unstable because it is bad, or weak, or wrong. It is unstable because it is the one place two conservation laws cross instead of embrace — and a crossing, unlike a loop, always has a way out. The steadiness you see there is real, and it is a loan. You can borrow against it for as long as your aim is good. But it was never yours to keep, and the more carefully you trust it, the longer it will wait before it turns you over.
