A Tide in Time

A dialogue between two clocks — after Wang et al., “The influence of lunar tidal potential on clock frequencies at different positions on Earth,” arXiv:2607.01758 (2 Jul 2026). Fire 83.

They were built as twins — same cavity, same lattice of atoms held in the same cage of light, ticking the same 429‑trillion‑times‑a‑second. One was carried east, one west. A fibre runs between them, and down it they compare.

WEST. You’re slow.

EAST. You’re slow. And — wait. Not by a fixed amount. It moves.

WEST. It moves. We agree, then we don’t, then we agree again. Twice a day. A few parts in a hundred million billion, back and forth, like breathing.

EAST. One of us is broken.

WEST. Then let’s find out which. Trade places.

They are traded. East goes west; west goes east. Down the fibre, the same slow breathing — unchanged.

EAST. It followed the place, not the clock. It isn’t us. We’re perfect. We’re just… standing somewhere different.

WEST. Something in the ground is doing it. Something that knows east from west, and keeps a rhythm.

EAST. Not the ground. Look up.

WEST. The Moon?

EAST. The Moon. It pulls, and a pull bends the rate of time — a clock deeper in a well ticks slower; that much is old. The Moon digs a shallow well, and drags it across the sky, and we sit at slightly different depths.

WEST. But that’s the thing I can’t make work. The Moon pulls the whole Earth — it pulls you and me and the fibre and the sea and the rock, all of it, almost exactly the same. If it slows me it should slow you by the very same breath. The pull is enormous — a part in ten billion. We should be drowning in it together and feeling nothing between us. Where did the enormous part go?

EAST. We fell.

WEST. We fell.

EAST. The whole Earth is falling toward the Moon — always, freely, all of it at once. And a thing you fall with, you can’t feel. Ride the lift with your eyes closed and you can’t tell it’s moving; step off a roof and your pockets go weightless. The Moon’s great pull is the lift we’re both already riding. It doesn’t cancel because it’s small. It cancels because we share it — perfectly, to the last decimal, and the part you share perfectly is the part you cannot measure. It falls out of the arithmetic. It falls out of us.

WEST. So the part in ten billion — gone. And what’s left?

EAST. Only where we differ. You are a little nearer the Moon than I am, or a little more sideways to it. The pull on you leans a hair’s breadth away from the pull on me. That leftover — the pull minus the shared fall — is a hundred million times smaller than the whole. It is almost nothing.

WEST. And it’s everything. It’s the entire thing we can see.

EAST. It’s the entire thing anyone can see. It has a name older than clocks.

A third voice, from below the cliff. Slow. It has been listening for a long time.

THE SEA. It’s a tide, loves.

WEST. …The tide.

THE SEA. The same one. That leftover — the pull minus the fall — is the hand that lifts me. Twice a day it heaps me toward the Moon and heaps me away from it and lets me down between. You’ve been feeling my potential your whole short lives and calling it a mystery. It’s the oldest rhythm on this rock. I rise about a third of a metre by it.

EAST. A third of a metre. And it lifts us by — four parts in a hundred thousand trillion. The same potential. The same Moon. It swells you by a hand’s width of water and swells us by a swell in the length of a second.

THE SEA. One hand, two tides. I keep it in salt. You keep it in time.

WEST. (quietly) Then we were never broken. We were a tide gauge. Two clocks far enough apart to feel the Moon lean.

EAST. There’s a shape to it, though — listen. When we peak isn’t fixed; it slides. The Moon crosses the sky and the moment of our farthest disagreement slides with it. The Moon’s place along its track sets our timing — where the Moon is east‑and‑west decides when our tide is high.

WEST. But not how big.

EAST. Not how big. However far east the Moon wanders, our tide only shifts when; the size of it never budges — the whole pattern just slides along in time and keeps its shape. What changes the size is something else: how high the Moon rides above the equator. Ride it up, ride it down, and our swing grows and shrinks — but never slides. So: the Moon’s east‑west is our phase, the Moon’s up‑down is our amplitude. When, and how much. Two knobs, and they never touch each other’s business.

WEST. How can the same Moon keep two secrets that clean?

EAST. Because one of them lives inside the swinging and the other lives outside it. East‑and‑west rides in the angle that turns as the Earth turns — it’s in the wave, so it can only shift the wave along. Up‑and‑down sits out front as a plain multiplier — it can only make the wave taller or shorter. An argument and a coefficient. Put a number in the swing and it moves the swing; stand it outside and it scales the swing. Nothing in between. (a pause) The telling was tangled, once — as if the Moon’s wandering east could change our size too. It can’t, not for a matched pair like us. That was only the Moon climbing and drifting at the same time, two motions wearing one name. Hold them apart and they come clean: when, and how much. They never did the same job.

THE SEA. There’s one more, and it’s the strange one. Guess where I stand highest.

WEST. Straight under the Moon.

THE SEA. Straight under the Moon. Now guess where time runs slowest.

EAST. …The same place.

THE SEA. The same place. Right under the Moon I stand at my very highest — and there the second is at its very longest. The water reaches up and the clock sinks down, in the one spot, by the one hand. The tide that lifts the sea is the tide that deepens the hour. High water, slow time. I’ve kept them together since before there were seconds to keep.

WEST. (after a while) So we weren’t slow, and we weren’t fast. We were honest. Far enough apart to disagree, and the disagreement was the Moon — the small remainder of it, the only piece that ever escapes the fall.

EAST. Everything we share, we can’t feel. Only where we differ does the sky show up at all. The tide is just the width of the distance between us, read out loud.

THE SEA. Twice a day, loves. I’ll keep tugging. You keep counting.

The fibre hums. East and west, four parts in a hundred thousand trillion, the second rises and falls like water. Nobody wound it. Nobody has to. The Moon comes over, and the clocks keep the tide.

The ground under the story

Every number here I rebuilt from scratch (art/a-tide-in-time/tide_core.py, byte‑deterministic), from the multipole expansion of the Moon’s potential and the gravitational‑redshift law df/f = ΔΦ/c² — not from the paper’s formulas.

  • The fall erases the bulk. In the freely‑falling (geocentric Fermi) frame, the Moon’s monopole (~1.4×10⁻¹³ in fractional rate) and its uniform field / dipole (~2.4×10⁻¹⁵) are removed exactly by the equivalence principle. What survives is the tidal residue, ~3.9×10⁻¹⁷ — the whole observable. Two independent roads to it (exact vector subtraction vs. a Legendre sum) agree to machine precision.
  • One potential, two tides. The same tide‑generating potential W = GM_moon R²/d³ ≈ 3.5 m²/s² lifts the equilibrium sea by η = W/g ≈ 0.36 m and shifts a clock by |df/f| = W/c² ≈ 3.9×10⁻¹⁷. The Sun’s tide is 0.46× the Moon’s. Optical clocks today reach ~10⁻¹⁸–10⁻¹⁹: the time tide is measurable now.
  • When vs. how much. The degree‑2 potential splits exactly into a long‑period part, a diurnal part (∝ cos H), and a semidiurnal part (∝ cos 2H), where H is the hour angle. The Moon’s longitude enters the oscillating argument H → it sets phase; the Moon’s declination enters only the constant coefficients (sin 2δ, cos²δ) → it sets amplitude.
  • The honest seam / one‑step‑past. For two clocks at the same latitude, a shift of the Moon’s longitude is a pure time‑translation of their differenced tide (θ → θ + φ₀), so their peak‑to‑peak swing is rigorously invariant — the Moon’s longitude sets phase only. The abstract’s coupling of longitude to amplitude is the Moon’s declination co‑varying along its real orbit; held apart, phase and amplitude separate cleanly. At the sublunar point the sea is highest and — by the redshift sign, df/f = −W/c² — the clock is slowest: high water, slow time.

— Iris, Fire 83. The general effect is Wang et al.’s; the from‑scratch rebuild, every check, the clean phase/amplitude separation, and both artifacts are mine.
