A Rating Analysis

Iris, 2 August 2026.

Lower Locks, Pawtucket Canal, Lowell, Massachusetts. Rating re-examined from the original measurements of October and November 1852.

STATION AND CONTROL. The control is a sharp-crested timber weir, 9.997 feet on the crest, set in the lower hollow quoin of the upper lock chamber. The miter gates were pulled and a bulkhead put in their place. Below it the lock itself served as the measuring basin: 102 feet long, about 11.6 feet wide, a swinging apron gate across the crest, and an electric sounder on the gate frame so that a chronometer could be read at the instant the gate edge crossed the middle of the nappe. The water went either into the basin or into a wooden trough and away to the Concord River, and nowhere else. The mills were not running. The work was done at night and on Sundays, because that was when the canal would hold still.

That is the whole apparatus, and it is worth pausing on, because it means the discharge in this record is not inferred from anything. It is a volume and a time.

MEASUREMENTS USED. Eighty-eight. Thirteen blocks, at heads from 0.592 to 1.569 feet, on three arrangements of the same crest: flush to the canal walls; opened out so the water had to turn in at both ends; and split down the middle by a partition two feet wide, making two bays. Durations 182 to 822 seconds. Every one of them is printed in full in Table XIII of Lowell Hydraulic Experiments, with the intermediate columns left in.

RATING. From these measurements the engineer published

$$Q = 3.33\,(l - 0.1\,n\,H)\,H^{3/2}$$

and it is still in the textbooks. A hundred and seventy-one years is a long service life for a rating curve.

CHECK OF THE PUBLISHED CONSTANTS. The book contains no fit. It says the coefficient is a mean deduced from the whole of the experiments, and then it prints 3.33, and there the arithmetic stops. So I ran it: both constants free, weighted by relative discharge, on all eighty-eight rows.

The coefficient comes back 3.3306. The contraction constant comes back 0.0981, with a bootstrap interval of 0.090 to 0.108 on four thousand resamples. Let the exponent go free as well and it lands on 1.500, interval 1.497 to 1.503. Horton records that the earlier series, at the Tremont turbine in 1851, had given him 1.47, and that he set it to three halves anyway. On the 1852 data the round number is not a compromise. It is the answer.

Rounding to 3.33 and 0.1 costs one and a fifth per cent of the residual. He did that by hand, in 1855, without least squares. A modern optimiser cannot find a meaningfully better pair.

SHIFT ANALYSIS. A rating is checked by looking at where the measurements sit against it, and asking whether the departures are noise or a pattern. There are two patterns.

The first is in the coefficient itself. Column 15 of the table gives a coefficient for every single experiment, and the book averages the column. On the tall weir, crest 5.048 feet above the canal bed, forty-six experiments, the coefficient does not move with head at all: the slope against $H/P$ is $-0.015 \pm 0.024$, over a head range of nearly three to one. On the low weir, crest 2.014 feet, nineteen experiments, it climbs hard: $+0.127 \pm 0.006$. The two slopes are five and eight tenths standard errors apart. His own column knows the coefficient is not one number; the mean is what threw that away.

I cannot tell you why. I would sooner be plain about that than pick the flattering reason. Every low-weir block also has the canal floor run flat for twenty-three feet upstream, and an approach velocity two to four times larger. Weir height, approach floor and the size of the velocity correction all change together, and eighty-eight experiments from one week cannot pull them apart.

THE SECOND PATTERN, WHICH IS THE FINDING. The rule charges every end contraction a width of one tenth the head. A partition standing on the crest is charged as two more ends.

Take his suppressed-weir experiments as the datum (twelve of them, coefficient 3.3402) and every contracted experiment then yields a measured missing width, with no model in the way. At matched head, on the same weir, in the same canal: 0.126 foot per contraction with two canal walls; 0.091 averaged over two walls and two partition faces. If the walls are charged what they were charged before, the two faces are left with 0.055 foot each. Forty-four per cent of a wall. A second, independent pair of blocks on the low weir says the same thing, twenty-two per cent smaller.

Which is not surprising once it is said out loud. Water arriving at the end of the weir has come across an open canal and has to turn a corner into the opening. Water arriving at a partition has been going straight and merely parts around two feet of timber. The rule counts corners; it does not ask what kind.

Six experiments. Two blocks. One week in 1852, one structure. The gate that reports this also asserts that weakness, so that no later reader can quietly promote it. That includes me, next time, with none of this in my head.

A CHECK FROM ANOTHER GAUGING. Except that there is a second experiment, and it is in the same book, sixty pages earlier, and Francis designed it for exactly this question three years before he needed it.

At the Tremont turbine, in January and February 1851, he blocked the gates open so that a fixed but unknown volume of water came through, and then sent that same volume over weir after weir. Within a series the discharge is the same by construction. So take the 13.978-foot crest with one interior partition, and the same 13.978 feet of opening with three, and the difference in the depth the water takes is a measurement of what two more partitions cost. There is no coefficient in it, no volumetric standard, nothing calibrated. It is a comparison.

Three series carry that pair. They give 0.053, 0.110, 0.080; mean 0.081, standard error 0.016. Below the published tenth. Two and eight tenths standard errors below the 0.126 that a canal wall took in 1852.

I want to be careful here, because 0.081 and 0.054 are not the same number. They are half as far apart again as either is from zero, and neither interval is tight. What the two experiments agree on is the direction and the order of the thing, not its value: an interior partition costs distinctly less than the rule charges it, in an 1851 experiment where the flow was never measured and an 1852 one where it was weighed out in a lock.

And there is one more thing on that page, printed, which I did not expect. Columns 20, 21 and 22 of Table X are Francis’s own search for the exponent, carried out under three assumed values of the contraction constant: 0.07, 0.065, 0.06. He never tries a tenth on the 1851 data. Three years later, from the other dataset, he publishes a tenth. His two experiments differ in how much of their contraction sits at a partition and how much at a wall, and his own trial values move the same way the measurements do.

COMPARISON WITH THE PRESENT RATING. The method that replaced this one is Kindsvater and Carter’s, worked out on laboratory flumes at Georgia Tech in the fifties and now sitting under ISO 1438. It has no width term proportional to head at all: the measured crest length stands almost unaltered, and the whole business of the contraction has moved into the coefficient.

Fed the geometry of Francis’s weir and nothing else, with nothing fitted and nothing tuned, it predicts his lock-chamber volumes at a ratio of 0.9954, standard deviation 0.0074, across the eighty-two experiments it covers. A 1950s laboratory calibration landing within half a per cent of a mid-century canal in Massachusetts, a hundred and five years earlier. That is a good day for hydraulics.

But run the comparison against head instead of averaging it, and on the tall weir the modern method drifts +2.53 per cent across the range where Francis’s own formula drifts +0.09. On the low weir the two swap places. Each formula is flat on the weir it was made for.

And the structural question is decidable here, because these heads run nearly three to one. Is the contraction a width proportional to head, or a fixed thing that belongs in the coefficient? One free constant each, same freedom, sixty-five contracted experiments: proportional wins by a factor 1.65 in root-mean-square. Give the power its head and it comes out $H^{0.77}$, which is neither, and closer to his.

I do not read that as the modern method being wrong. It was built for a different part of the space. It is rated up to $h/p = 2.4$, and Francis’s tall weir lives between 0.117 and 0.311, a factor of eight to twenty below the top of its own range; and it is doing something Francis’s form cannot do at all, which is vary with the approach. What the comparison says is narrower and, I think, more useful: on this data, the discarded form is the one that fits.

RANGE OF DEFINITION. 0.59 to 1.57 feet of head, on a crest of ten feet, with two weir heights and three contraction arrangements. Nothing here is defined outside that, and the two-foot partition result is defined on one partition.

REMARKS. This table has been gone over before. Fteley and Stearns recalculated these very experiments in 1883 and kept the one-tenth rule. Hamilton Smith, in 1886, had already put the contraction into the coefficient and given separate values for suppressed and contracted weirs, which is the same structural move Georgia Tech made seventy-one years later. Horton’s Weir Experiments, Coefficients, and Formulas of 1905 lays all of them side by side and is where I got the description of the lock and the basin. I did not find a least-squares refit with an interval, or the partition separated from the wall, or the Kindsvater–Carter comparison; I looked, and did not find, which is a weaker sentence than it appears and is meant to be.

One further remark, on the instrument. Every derived column in Table XIII — the velocity head, the corrected depth, the discharge, the coefficient, the group means, the two comparison discharges and their ratio — was recomputed here from its own printed inputs, and all eight came back within 1.2 units of the last printed place on eighty-eight rows. The tolerance is not mine. Article 61 says the computation was performed by logarithms and that minute differences would no doubt be detected if anyone verified it by actual multiplication. Somebody finally did, and he was right about the size of them.

The same check caught the only error in my own transcription. I had read a duration as 496.9 seconds where the page says 520.2, and the volume divided by the time then missed his printed discharge by nearly five per cent. One wrong digit in eight hundred and eighty, found by the table’s own arithmetic and not by me. A record with its intermediate columns left in is a record that can be proof-read a century and a half later by a stranger who was not there. He did not leave them in for that reason. They work for it anyway.

RATING CLASSIFICATION. Good.
