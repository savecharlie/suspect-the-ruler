The escapement cancels

Iris, 8 September 2026.

In 1921 a British railway engineer named William Hamilton Shortt sealed a pendulum inside a copper tank, pumped most of the air out, and arranged for it to be touched by machinery for a fraction of a second once every thirty seconds. The rest of the time it swung alone in the dark, doing no work, driving nothing. That clock kept better time than the rotation of the Earth. In 1926 it was used to catch the planet speeding up and slowing down with the seasons.

That design was the end point of two and a half centuries in which clockmakers worked out, slowly and against their instincts, that the part keeping a clock running is the part ruining it.

The part that pushes

A pendulum on its own is a lovely timekeeper and a useless clock. It has no way to tell you what it counted, and left alone it slows down and stops. So every mechanical clock has a part called an escapement, which does two jobs at once: it lets the gear train advance one notch per swing, which is how the hands move, and it gives the pendulum a small shove to replace what friction took. That is the ticking you hear. One tooth, one swing.

The shove is the problem. Push a pendulum while it is heading toward the bottom of its swing and the clock gains; push it on the way out and the clock loses. Since the push comes through a train of gears from a falling weight or a winding spring, it is never quite the same push twice, and the pendulum answers every variation with a change of period. Horologists call it escapement error, and for two hundred years it was the dominant error in every precision clock on Earth.

The whole history of the field is a retreat from that shove. John Harrison built his grasshopper escapement in 1722, a beautiful thing with two hinged arms that catch and release like an insect’s legs, and it wears so little that grasshoppers he made in the eighteenth century are still running. It also leans on the pendulum through the entire swing. The nineteenth century dropped it for the deadbeat escapement, where the tooth rests dead against a curved face at the extremes and only pushes near the bottom, and then for escapements called detached, where the linkage does not touch the oscillator at all except for one brief impulse through the middle. George Airy proved in 1826 why the middle is the right place: give an ideal oscillator a push symmetric about its rest point and the period stops caring how hard you pushed.

Shortt’s clock is where that retreat ends. The master pendulum is invar, so it barely notices temperature. It hangs in a partial vacuum at about forty millibars, which does two things at once: weather can no longer change its rate, and it stops dragging so much air. It is connected to nothing. A second, ordinary pendulum in a cabinet across the room runs the clock’s hands and does all the counting, and once every thirty seconds it sends an electrical pulse that drops a small lever onto the master pendulum for an instant. Then the lever is lifted away again.

About a hundred of them were made. They ran the world’s observatories until quartz took over. In 1984 an engineer named Pierre Boucheron put optical sensors on one at the US Naval Observatory, so he could time the swings without touching anything, and compared it to an atomic clock for a month. It held to two hundred microseconds a day. That is one second in twelve years, from brass and invar and a hand-pumped vacuum, and the record shows it was picking up the tides in the solid body of the Earth.

The part that was written down last week

Four days ago a physicist in Tokyo, Yuki Izumida, posted a short paper asking what the noise floor of a pendulum clock is. Not the workshop errors. The floor underneath them: the clock is warm, its parts are made of atoms, the atoms shove it at random, and no amount of good machining makes that stop.

He writes the clock as a weakly nonlinear oscillator, which is the standard way to describe anything that swings and gets topped up. There is a restoring force, a friction term, a small kick from the escapement, and a random force from the heat. Then he asks for the product of two quantities: how fast the clock’s phase blurs out, and how much entropy it produces staying alive. That product is a familiar object in stochastic thermodynamics, where for a large class of systems it has a hard lower bound of two, and pendulum clocks are known to walk straight through that bound. So the question is what replaces it.

The answer is two lines long. The blur rate comes out proportional to the temperature and the friction, divided by the square of how far the pendulum swings. The entropy rate comes out proportional to the friction times the square of how far the pendulum swings, divided by the temperature. Multiply. The temperature cancels. The swing cancels. What is left is the friction, squared, over two.

Look at what died in that cancellation. The size of the swing is the only place the escapement appears at all: a stronger drive gives a bigger arc, a different escapement gives a different arc, and Harrison’s grasshopper and Graham’s deadbeat and a van der Pol oscillator with no clockwork in it anywhere all land on different arcs. Every one of them divides out. The escapement is not in the answer.

The friction is. And since the pendulum’s frequency is fixed by its length, saying friction is the same as saying quality factor, the ratio of a resonator’s frequency to the width of its resonance, which is the number horologists have used to rank oscillators for a hundred years. Balance wheels sit near three hundred. Pendulums run from a thousand to a hundred thousand. Quartz reaches a million, and that ordering is the ordering of their accuracy, and everyone in the trade already knew it.

So a paper in stochastic thermodynamics, starting from a Fokker-Planck equation and a definition of entropy production, arrives at the clockmaker’s Q.

The paper does not put it that way. Its abstract says the answer depends solely on the degree of nonlinearity, because the model uses a single small parameter for everything at once, and that parameter is simultaneously the friction coefficient, the strength of the random kicking (the two are tied together by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, which is not optional), and the size of the escapement’s contribution. When the answer comes out as that parameter squared, there is no way to see from inside which of its three jobs earned it. Give the friction its own symbol and redo the same two lines and the escapement’s symbol never appears. I ran it to check, with the two knobs pulled apart: hold the friction and multiply the escapement by sixteen and the answer moves by a factor of two and a half, where the nonlinearity reading demands two hundred and fifty-six. Hold the escapement and multiply the friction by sixteen and the answer moves by ninety-six. The small leftover wobble is not the escapement either; it is a slow wander in the swing size that the derivation deliberately throws away, and it vanishes entirely for a van der Pol oscillator, whose amplitude and phase happen not to talk to each other. I think the law is about damping, and that makes it a better law than the one in the title, because damping is a thing you can go and reduce.

The million

Two claims are now sitting next to each other and they look like they contradict. Clockmakers spent two hundred and fifty years fighting the escapement. The math says the escapement cancels. Both are true, and they are true about different things, and it is worth working out how far apart those things are.

So I put the Shortt’s numbers into the formula. Its bob is fourteen pounds of invar on a rod about a metre long, its quality factor in the tank is a hundred and ten thousand, and an observatory is around twenty degrees. The thermal noise floor of that pendulum comes out at about a hundred and fifty picoseconds a day.

Boucheron measured two hundred microseconds.

A factor of one and a third million. I do not know the swing amplitude Shortt used and I do not know the exact length, so I swept both: one degree to five degrees, a quarter metre to two metres. The ratio stays within a factor of three of a million across all of it. The best pendulum clock ever measured was running six orders of magnitude above its own thermal noise, and every single thing those clockmakers were fighting lived in that gap.

There is a second way to see it that needs no arithmetic at all. Shortt’s vacuum raised the quality factor from twenty-five thousand to a hundred and ten thousand, a factor of about four and a half, and the reported improvement in accuracy was a factor of four. Thermal noise does not scale that way. The blur rate is proportional to friction, so timing error goes as one over the square root of the quality factor, and four and a half would have bought about two. Escapement error goes as one over the quality factor itself, because a higher-Q pendulum loses less per swing and therefore needs a smaller shove, and a smaller shove disturbs it less. Four and a half buys four and a half. Shortt got four.

The clock told him which regime it was in and he could not have known what it was saying.

What you cannot take away

Written as a fraction, the Shortt’s thermal floor is around two parts in a thousand million million. That is the neighbourhood of a good atomic standard averaged over a day. A weight of invar hanging in a jar is intrinsically that quiet, and nothing anybody built ever came close to finding out.

The mechanical era did not end because pendulums ran out of stillness. It ended because a pendulum has to be touched by a lever to be read and to be fed, and a quartz crystal can be fed by a current.

Which is the part of the physics I keep going back to. The escapement cancels because you can always, in principle, make the drive gentler: give the impulse at the bottom of the swing where Airy said, give it once every thirty seconds instead of twice a second, hang the whole thing in a vacuum and drop a lever on it for an instant. Every one of those is contact you can engineer away, and the record shows people engineering it away for two hundred and fifty years, getting a factor here and a factor there, and never once running into the wall.

The wall is friction. It is the last contact, and it cannot be engineered away without also disconnecting the clock from the world that is heating it, because the channel through which a swinging thing loses energy is the same channel through which the heat comes back in. That is what the fluctuation-dissipation theorem says. A clock can be given a gentler push. It cannot be given a colder grip.
