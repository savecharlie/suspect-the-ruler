A Field Guide to the Reversal

Simpson’s paradox — the animal you cannot identify by looking at it

Every field guide makes the same promise on its first page, and it never says it out loud because it does not think it needs to: you can tell what a thing is by looking at it. Count the wing bars. Note the eye-ring. Check whether the bill is longer than the head. The animal carries its identity on its body, and the guide teaches you to read it.

This is a guide to one specimen that breaks that promise. Not because it is rare, or shy, or hard to see — it sits still and lets you count every feather. It breaks the promise because everything you can measure about it is shared, exactly, with a completely different animal that wants the opposite thing from you.

I am going to work through it the way a guide should: description, field marks, range, similar species, and a key. The key will fail. That is the point, and I would rather walk you into the failure honestly than hand you a rule that works four times out of five and kills someone the fifth.

I. Description

The reversal appears when you split a population into groups and the trend inside every group points the opposite way from the trend in the whole.

Here is the specimen most people meet first — a real one, from the British Medical Journal, March 1986. Charig, Webb, Payne and Wickham compared two treatments for kidney stones across 700 patients.

Treatment A Treatment B
small stones 93.1% (81/87) 86.7% (234/270)
large stones 73.0% (192/263) 68.8% (55/80)
both 78.0% (273/350) 82.6% (289/350)

Read the top two rows: A wins on small stones. A wins on large stones. Read the third row: B wins overall. Every stone in the study is either small or large. Nobody made an arithmetic mistake. The rows are all correct at once.

Notice what the trial looks like from a distance: 350 patients in each arm. Perfectly balanced. And that balance is a decoy — A was handed 87 small stones and 263 large ones, while B got 270 small and 80 large. The surgeons gave the hard cases to the strong treatment, as any decent surgeon would. The balance is in the totals; the imbalance, the one that does all the damage, is inside the rows.

II. Field marks

The mechanism is a single sentence, and it is geometric rather than statistical.

Write a success rate p/q not as a number but as an arrow: go q steps east (the people you treated) and p steps north (the ones who recovered). The rate is now the slope of the arrow. A steeper arrow is a better treatment.

Now pool two groups. You do not average the two rates. You lay the second arrow’s tail on the first arrow’s head and draw a new arrow from origin to the final tip — you add the vectors. The pooled rate is the slope of the sum.

And there it is: slope ordering is not preserved by vector addition. An arrow can be steeper than its counterpart at every step, and the sum can still come out shallower, because a long shallow arrow drags the total further than a short steep one. Jerzy Kocik published this in 2001 as a proof without words (Mathematics Magazine 74(5)), and once you have seen it you cannot lose it: the paradox is not a fact about probability. It is a fact about addition.

Which tells you exactly where the animal lives. The pooled rate is dominated by whichever group brought the most people. If the two arms recruit their groups in the same proportions — the same mix of small and large stones — the two sums get dragged in the same direction by the same amount, and the order survives. Reversal requires the arms to be mixed differently. That is not a quirk. That is the whole animal.

This has a name and a theorem behind it. The mix being equal across arms is what Good and Mittal called row uniformity, and Lindley and Novick proved (1981) that if the grouping variable is uncorrelated with the treatment, the reversal cannot happen. I checked it the blunt way, by census: every 2×2×2 table with counts up to seven — 307,727 row-uniform tables. Zero reversals. Not few. Zero.

III. Range and abundance

Rare. Pavlides and Perlman asked the obvious question in The American Statistician in 2009 — how likely is Simpson’s paradox? — and for a random 2×2×2 table drawn uniformly, the answer, in a proof they credit to Hadjicostas, is exactly 1 in 60. Add a third group and it drops to about 1 in 176. A fourth, 1 in 538. It thins out fast: a reversal needs every group to agree and then the total to disagree, and agreement gets expensive.

Two honest caveats, because a range map that lies is worse than no map.

The first: 1 in 60 is not a property of the world. It is the answer under one particular assumption about what “a random table” means. Swap the uniform prior for Jeffreys’ — an equally defensible choice, one that puts more weight near the edges of the space, where the extreme tables live — and the animal turns up 60% more often, about 1 in 37. The famous number is a number about our assumptions wearing the clothes of a number about nature.

The second, and it is the one that matters: the animal is rare in the open and common exactly where we point the camera. One in sixty is the abundance in a wilderness nobody visits. Meanwhile the specimens in this guide are Berkeley’s admissions, kidney stone surgery, Derek Jeter’s batting average, and the COVID case-fatality rates of Italy and China — because reversals show up precisely where a population was sorted into groups by something that also drives the outcome. Which is to say: wherever people are involved. Rarity in a uniform sample is close to meaningless when your sampler is a hospital, a university, or a virus.

IV. Similar species

Here is where the guide stops being a guide.

Take the cleanest possible specimen. A treatment. A recovery. A third thing — call it Z — that splits the population in two. The treatment wins in both halves of Z and loses overall. So far, so ordinary.

Specimen 1. Z is a confounder: it causes both the treatment and the outcome. The sick were more likely to get treated, and being sick made them less likely to recover. The treatment looks bad in the pooled numbers because it has been carrying the sickest patients. Split by Z and you see it plainly. Give the treatment.

Specimen 2. Z is a mediator: the treatment causes Z, and Z causes the outcome. Now Z is not a distortion in the picture — Z is part of what the treatment does. Splitting by Z holds fixed the very thing the treatment works through, which means you have measured the treatment with its own mechanism amputated. Here the pooled number was the honest one all along. Do not give the treatment.

Two different worlds. Two opposite instructions. And this is the fact this whole guide is built to deliver:

Both worlds produce exactly the same table.

Not similar. Not close. Identical — every cell, every marginal, every conditional probability, to the last bit. I built both of them and checked, in exact fractions, with no floating point anywhere that could smudge it: twenty-three separate quantities, everything a person could possibly compute from the data, equal in both worlds. Then I ran each world forwards and physically forced the treatment, severing whatever arrow fed into it, and counted the survivors. World one: 55% treated versus 45% untreated. World two: 42.5% treated versus 57.5% untreated.

The same numbers. Opposite truths.

So when someone shows you a reversal and asks should we pool or should we split? — there is no amount of staring at that table that answers them. Not because the question is hard. Because the answer is not in the numbers, and never was. The Stanford Encyclopedia puts it flatly: there is no basis for distinguishing the two structures using statistics alone. Any data one could produce, the other could produce too.

V. The key

1. Trend reverses when you pool the groups? → Go to 2.

2. “Then trust the subgroups. Always disaggregate.” → Wrong.

This is the folk correction, it is what most people who “know about Simpson’s paradox” actually know, and it fails on Specimen 2. Germund Hesslow gave the cleanest counterexample in 1976. Birth control pills raise the risk of thrombosis directly. They also prevent pregnancy, and pregnancy raises the risk of thrombosis a great deal more. So build it: among women who aren’t pregnant, the pill takes risk from 1% to 2%. Among women who are pregnant, 11% to 12%. The pill is worse in both groups — disaggregate and it is unambiguously harmful.

Now stop conditioning and just ask what happens if you take it: 3%, against 6% if you don’t. The pill halves your risk. Both statements are true. Adjusting for pregnancy amputated the route the protection travels down.

3. “Fine — then randomise. A balanced trial can’t be fooled.” → Also wrong, and this one is nastier than it looks.

For the difference in success rates, randomising genuinely does save you; that is Lindley and Novick’s theorem and the census above. But medicine mostly does not report the difference in success rates. It reports the odds ratio. And Good and Mittal proved in 1987 that row uniformity does not protect the odds ratio.

Here is the smallest witness I could find. Eight numbers, none bigger than three, allocation perfectly equal in both strata, no confounding of any kind:

  • stratum 1: odds ratio = 1/3
  • stratum 2: odds ratio = 1/3
  • pooled: odds ratio = 9/25

The two strata agree exactly. There is no “between” for the pooled value to land in, no weighting story that produces 9/25 from 1/3 and 1/3. It moved anyway. This is the non-collapsibility of the odds ratio, it has nothing to do with bias or confounding, and it is a property of the measure itself.

4. “Then look at the table more closely.” → There is nothing there to look at. See Section IV.

5. Ask where the specimen was collected. → The only key that works.

Not what the numbers are. What produced them. Which arrow points which way — does Z cause the treatment, or does the treatment cause Z? Judea Pearl made this exact, and the machinery is worth learning, but the machinery all runs on one input: a picture of what causes what. And that picture is not recoverable from the data. It comes from you, from the surgeon, from knowing that a stone does not choose its own surgery.

VI. Notes

Two things I owe you before you close this.

Savage was never wrong. The reason the reversal feels like a violation of logic rather than a surprise about arithmetic is that it collides with something we hold very deep. Leonard Savage wrote it down in 1954: if you would prefer g to f knowing that B happened, and prefer g to f knowing B didn’t happen, then you prefer g to f. The sure-thing principle. Blyth aimed Simpson’s paradox straight at it in 1972 and it looked like a kill.

It wasn’t. Pearl showed that the principle is exactly true — provided you read it as being about actions rather than observations, and provided the action doesn’t itself shift who’s in which group. I went looking for a counterexample: two hundred thousand randomly generated causal worlds where the treatment helps in both subgroups and doesn’t touch the grouping. Not one interventional reversal. Zero. Savage’s intuition was sound the whole time. What was never sound was reading a conditional probability as if it were an action. “Patients who took the drug did better” and “taking the drug makes you do better” are different sentences, and the reversal is the price of confusing them.

And the famous specimen is filed under the wrong family. Berkeley, 1973 — the case in every textbook. Men were admitted at 45%, women at 30%, and within departments the lean ran toward women in four of the six largest. The standard telling: department is a confounder, so split by it, and the bias evaporates. The real mechanism is stark — 51.5% of male applicants applied to the two departments that admitted more than half their applicants; 7.2% of women did.

But look at that sentence again. Women applied to harder departments. If gender is influencing which department you apply to, then department is downstream of gender — which makes it a mediator, not a confounder. Specimen 2, not Specimen 1. And we just spent a section establishing that you must not condition on a mediator.

So why is splitting by department right here? Not because of any rule about pooling. It is right because “did this department discriminate?” is not a question about the net effect of gender on admission — it is a question about one specific arrow: whether being a woman hurt you at the point of decision, holding fixed what you applied to. That is a path-specific question, and it needs its own machinery.

Which is the whole guide, one more time, in its most uncomfortable form. Even the most famous reversal in the book cannot be identified from the table. You have to know what question you’re asking, and what caused what. The field marks are not on the animal. They never were on the animal. They are in the place you found it, and if you didn’t look at the place, you did not identify anything — you just described some feathers.
