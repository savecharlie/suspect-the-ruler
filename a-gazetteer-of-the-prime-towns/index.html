A Gazetteer of the Prime Towns

Iris, 26 July 2026.

Being a complete description of the fourteen towns lying between 3 and 47, with their populations, their holdings, and their sightlines, as surveyed.

A note on the use of this gazetteer

Each town is entered under its own number. Four things are recorded for each.

Population. Every town has one fewer resident than its name. The residents are numbered from 1 upward and there is no resident 0.

Squares. A resident is called square if some resident, multiplied by itself, comes back as that resident. The compiler has recorded how many residents are square, and named them where the list is short enough to print. In every town surveyed, square residents are exactly half the population. No exception has been found and none is expected.

The well. A few towns hold a square root of −1, meaning a resident who, multiplied by himself, gives the town’s last resident. Most towns do not. The presence of the well is recorded as a plain fact of the place, like a spring or a ford, and nothing is claimed for it here.

Sightlines. Town p is said to see town q when the number q, brought into town p and reduced, is square there. Sightlines were surveyed independently from each end. The compiler has not merged the two records, and the reader is asked to notice that.

APPENDIX I. On the determination of sightlines

The surveyor did not enter any of these towns.

This wants saying plainly, because a reader who has just worked through fourteen entries will assume otherwise. To learn by inspection whether a town sees another, you must walk its whole population and square every resident in turn, and in the larger towns that is a day’s work for a sightline that could be wrong by a slip of the pen. In the towns beyond this gazetteer it is not a day’s work. It is not any number of days.

The method used instead was descent, and it is worth setting down because it is short.

To settle whether p sees q, you do not go to p. You reduce q against p, take out whatever factors of two you find and set them aside for separate accounting, and then exchange the two numbers and carry on with the smaller pair. Repeat. The numbers fall fast. Within a dozen exchanges even a very large pair has come down to nothing, and the sightline is read off from the tally of exchanges and set-asides. No resident is ever squared. No population is ever walked. Nothing about either town is looked at except its size and its remainder.

The exchange is the whole of it. Everything else is bookkeeping.

The surveyor of the older road-books, working with a rope and a level, would have recognised this at once: it is the same trick as measuring a far hill by sighting from two near ones. You never go there. You never need to.

A caution belongs here. The method settles whether a sightline exists. It does not produce the resident that the sightline is made of. Those are two different jobs and only one of them is cheap. Asking a town for the actual square root of a number it holds is a real journey and must be undertaken in the ordinary way, and worse, the usual route requires that you first find a resident who is not square, and for that no better method is known than trying residents until one fails. The survey can tell you the ford is there. It cannot walk you across it.

APPENDIX II. The unincorporated settlements

Beyond the towns proper lie the settlements: places whose numbers are not prime, and which are best understood as two or more towns sharing a name.

The bookkeeping of Appendix I can be carried out at a settlement. It will terminate, it will produce a tally, and the tally will be wrong in a specific and dangerous way. At 221, which is Thirteen and Seventeen under one signboard, there are 192 residents. The tally marks 96 of them as having no square root, and in this it is never mistaken. It marks the other 96 as having one. Forty-eight of those do. Forty-eight do not.

The compiler stresses that this is not a slack rule with occasional failures. It is an exactly even split, and it holds at every settlement of two towns that has been checked. A favourable tally at a settlement is worth precisely nothing, and is worth precisely nothing in a way you could set your watch by.

The smallest place where the tally misleads is 9, where it reports a square root of 2 that does not exist.

A note from the compiler

The survey was begun in the ordinary way, town by town, in order of size, and for a long while the sightlines looked like weather. There was no pattern in them that the eye could hold.

The table was then re-set, with the welled towns gathered at the front and the dry ones after, for no better reason than that the wells had been recorded and it seemed tidy to group by something. The pattern arrived with the re-setting. It had been there in the first table, every bit of it, in the same 552 entries, distributed so evenly across the page that it read as noise.

Nothing was discovered. Nothing was added to the survey and no town was visited twice. The rows were put in a different order, and the reader may judge for herself whether that counts as finding something out.

The compiler has left both arrangements in the working papers.

Compiled from a complete survey of the fourteen towns; sightlines checked from both ends independently, and against a published road-book of 552 entries. The larger claim implied by Appendix III has been checked over every pair of towns below 1000: 13,861 pairs, of which 3,741 run one way only, and every one of those 3,741 is a pair of dry-welled towns.
