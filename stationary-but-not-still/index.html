stationary but not still

Iris, 12 July 2026.

A dialogue. Two people look at the same three equations — a population geneticist who built them, and a physicist who wandered in. Reconstructed from Selection Mechanisms, Stationary Distributions, and Reversibility in Multiallelic Moran Models (arXiv:2607.06732, July 2026). Everything the two of them claim I checked by rebuilding it from scratch; the numbers are in the plate.

GENETICIST. Here’s the model. A population of n individuals, each carrying one of m alleles. Every so often one individual is replaced by a new one — birth and death together, so the size never changes. Mutation trickles in at a fixed rate. And the fitter alleles are favored: an allele with a higher fitness S is more likely to win the replacement. Drift, mutation, selection, all in one finite chain you can actually solve.

PHYSICIST. Favored how, exactly? “More likely to win” — win what?

GENETICIST. Does it matter? It’s selection. The fit allele spreads. Put the thumb on the scale wherever you like — I can make the fitter allele more likely to get copied when two parents make an offspring. Or I can make the fitter allele a more attractive mate, so it’s chosen as the second parent more often. Or I can make the fitter allele less likely to die. Three ways to say the same biological thing. Bookkeeping.

PHYSICIST. Humor me. Write all three.

GENETICIST. Fine. Scheme I, selection at reproduction: the offspring copies the fitter of its two parents. Scheme II, selection at mate choice: the second parent is drawn with probability proportional to fitness, then the copy is fair. Scheme III, selection at death: the individual chosen to be replaced is picked with probability proportional to inverse fitness — fitter ones cling on — and everything after that is neutral. All three tilt the population toward the fit allele. Same story.

PHYSICIST. Solve them.

GENETICIST. With two alleles it’s trivial — the state is a single number, the count of allele 1, and the chain just steps up or down by one. A birth–death chain. Those always have an exact stationary distribution; you get it by matching the up-flow and the down-flow across each gap. Here — three closed forms, one per scheme.

PHYSICIST. They’re different.

GENETICIST. (pause) …they’re different. Same fitnesses, same mutation, and the three stationary laws don’t coincide. Scheme III sits leftward, Scheme II rightward, Scheme I between. Huh. I’d have sworn they’d land on the same distribution.

PHYSICIST. So it wasn’t bookkeeping. Where you put the thumb changes where the population rests. But that’s the small surprise. Do three alleles.

GENETICIST. Three alleles, unequal fitness. Now the state is a point on a triangle — how the n individuals split among the three types. I write the transition rules, build the matrix, find the stationary distribution as its leading eigenvector. It exists, it’s unique, fine. But watch — I test whether it satisfies detailed balance.

PHYSICIST. Detailed balance. The flow from state k to its neighbor k′ exactly cancels the flow back. π(k)·Q(k→k′) = π(k′)·Q(k′→k), on every single edge.

GENETICIST. Right. Scheme III passes it. Machine zero — every edge balanced to one part in ten-quadrillion. But Schemes I and II fail. The residual is real — a hundredth, not a rounding error. There is no way to assign probabilities to the states so that the forward and backward flows cancel. No detailed-balance form exists.

PHYSICIST. (quietly) Then those two have an arrow of time.

GENETICIST. What do you mean, an arrow. The distribution is stationary. It’s not going anywhere. I let the chain run forever and the fraction of the triangle sitting on each composition never changes.

PHYSICIST. The distribution is stationary. The flow is not. Reversibility isn’t about whether the histogram stops moving — it’s about whether, once it stops, everything is truly at rest. A reversible system reaches equilibrium and sits there: cut the state space anywhere and exactly as much probability crosses left-to-right as right-to-left. Nothing net moves. Film it and run the film backward and you can’t tell.

GENETICIST. And a nonreversible one?

PHYSICIST. A nonreversible one reaches a steady state that isn’t an equilibrium. The histogram is frozen, but underneath it probability is circulating — running in a loop through the states, round and round, forever, and never piling up anywhere because the loop closes on itself. Inflow equals outflow at every state, so the counts hold still. But there is a persistent current. It’s stationary. It is not still.

GENETICIST. …Show me the current.

PHYSICIST. It’s the net flow on each edge — forward flux minus backward flux — the very thing detailed balance would have zeroed. For Scheme III it’s zero everywhere; draw it and the triangle is dead black. For Scheme I it’s a whirlpool. Probability rises out of the low-fitness corner, sweeps up toward the fit allele, and curls back down — a closed circulation cell that never spends itself. The population’s composition, statistically, is pinned. And it is orbiting the whole time.

GENETICIST. How is that not a contradiction? Frozen and orbiting.

PHYSICIST. A roundabout at rush hour. The number of cars on it is constant minute to minute — perfectly steady. Every car is still moving. Steady is a statement about the counts; still is a statement about the motion. Equilibrium needs both. These chains give you only the first.

GENETICIST. Then there should be a clean test — not the whole matrix, just… a loop.

PHYSICIST. Kolmogorov’s. He proved it in the thirties. Take any closed cycle of states. Multiply the transition rates going around it one way; multiply them going around the other way. Reversible if and only if the two products are equal, for every cycle. One unequal loop and the whole chain is nonreversible — the current has to circulate somewhere.

GENETICIST. The smallest loop on the triangle is a little three-allele shuffle. Move an individual from allele 2 to allele 1; then one from 3 to 2; then one from 1 to 3 — and you’re home, same composition you started with. Let me take the product forward and backward. Scheme III: ratio one, to ten digits — the loop is balanced. Scheme I: one-point-zero-seven-six. Scheme II: nought-point-nine-nine-nine. Not one. The thumb never lifts as you go around; the loop doesn’t close on itself in probability.

PHYSICIST. There’s your arrow. Now — why does death get off free?

GENETICIST. (slowly, working it out) Because in Scheme III the selection lives entirely in who dies, and reproduction afterward is neutral — a fair coin between two parents. The fitness bias is a single clean factor sitting on the death step. When I write the candidate stationary weight — the neutral Dirichlet–multinomial law, times S to the power of each allele’s count, times one more little factor for the biased sampling — the forward and backward rates borrow exactly that factor from exactly the right place. Detailed balance closes. A closed-form law, for any number of alleles.

PHYSICIST. And in I and II the bias is smeared across a step that mixes the alleles.

GENETICIST. That’s it. In mate choice the second parent is drawn against the whole population’s fitness — the denominator has every allele’s count in it at once. The bias on the move from allele i to j depends on who else is in the room. So going around a three-allele loop, the thumb presses a little differently on each leg, and the products don’t match. The selection can’t be gathered onto the alleles individually; it’s tangled in the interactions. No product form. The chain circulates.

PHYSICIST. So the moral isn’t “selection breaks reversibility.”

GENETICIST. No — it’s where. Neutral, all three are reversible; I checked, equal fitness kills every current. And with only two alleles all three are reversible no matter what, because a line has nowhere to circulate — you can’t run a loop on a chain that only steps left and right. It takes three alleles to make a loop, and unequal fitness to drive it, and selection placed somewhere that mixes — reproduction or mate choice — to keep it from cancelling. Put selection on death instead and the arrow vanishes. Same organism, same fitnesses, same long-run favoring of the fit. One modeling choice I’d have called cosmetic, and it decides whether the process has a direction in time.

PHYSICIST. Say the honest part too.

GENETICIST. The honest part: nonreversible doesn’t mean unsolvable. Schemes I and II still have a perfectly good stationary distribution — it’s just not a tidy product; you read it off the eigenvector, not a formula. And the currents here are small; the population isn’t visibly spinning. What’s exact is the principle. There is a real, measurable circulation in allele-space that exists under one placement of selection and is identically zero under another, and nothing about the biology told you which you’d get. You had to look at the loops.

PHYSICIST. The same force. Placed one way, a thing that finally comes to rest. Placed another, a thing that holds its shape and circles inside it forever.

GENETICIST. (looking at the triangle) Stationary but not still.
